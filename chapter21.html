1. Understanding generative models

Most people first encounter machine learning through prediction. A model receives information and predicts an answer. For example, an image classifier receives a photograph and predicts whether it contains a dog or a cat. A generative model solves a different problem: it learns how examples are structured and uses that knowledge to create new samples.

Suppose we give a model thousands of handwritten digit images. We do not simply want it to label an image as a three or an eight. We want it to learn the shapes, curves, stroke patterns, and variations found in those images. After training, it should be able to generate a new handwritten digit that resembles the training examples without merely copying one of them.

Training examples
→
Learned probability model
→
New samples

A generative model learns a representation of the patterns in its training data and uses it to produce samples.

1.1 What does it mean to learn a distribution?

Imagine a bag containing red and blue balls. If 70% of the balls are red and 30% are blue, repeatedly picking a ball produces a distribution with those proportions. A generative model learns a more complicated version of this idea.

Instead of choosing only between two colours, it may need to assign probabilities to thousands of possible images, sequences, or scientific configurations. We represent the real data distribution by pdata(x), where x is one possible data sample. The model learns an approximation pmodel(x).

pmodel(x) ≈ pdata(x) The aim is to learn a distribution that captures the important patterns in the real data.

This does not mean that the model must reproduce every training example exactly. In fact, simply memorising the training set is usually a sign of poor generalisation. A good model should capture the rules and variations that make the examples meaningful.

1.2 Why introduce quantum computing?

Classical computers represent information using bits. Quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits, whose states can involve superposition and entanglement. A system of many qubits can represent a quantum state with amplitudes associated with many computational basis states.

For n qubits, the state can be written as |ψ⟩ = Σₓ αₓ|x⟩, where each x is an n-bit string and the amplitudes satisfy Σₓ |αₓ|² = 1.

The probability of observing the bit string x in the computational basis is given by the Born rule:

p(x) = |αx|² Measurement converts the quantum state's amplitudes into observable probabilities.

This relationship is the starting point for an important family of quantum generative models. By preparing and measuring carefully designed quantum states, we can obtain samples from a probability distribution. Training changes the circuit so that the measured distribution better matches the desired data distribution.

Important distinction: A quantum state may describe exponentially many amplitudes, but this does not mean we can read all of them from one measurement. A measurement returns a limited classical result. Whether a quantum model provides a practical advantage depends on the learning task, circuit, data-loading cost, noise, and classical alternatives.

1.3 The common structure behind quantum generative models

Although different models use different learning strategies, many follow the same broad pattern:

Prepare qubits
→
Apply trainable gates
→
Measure
→
Collect samples

The trainable quantum circuit changes the probabilities of different measurement outcomes.

Let the circuit have parameters represented by θ. These parameters may be rotation angles in quantum gates. Applying the circuit to an initial state gives |ψ(θ)⟩. Measuring this state produces a model distribution:

pθ(x) = |⟨x|ψ(θ)⟩|²

Training attempts to find parameters θ that make this distribution resemble the target distribution. Depending on the model, training may minimise a statistical distance, maximise a likelihood, minimise an energy-based objective, or play an adversarial game.

This brings us to the first major model: the Born machine.

21.1 Born machines

21.1.1 The central idea

A Born machine is a generative model that uses the Born rule of quantum mechanics to define the probability of each possible output. The simplest picture is straightforward: prepare a quantum state, measure it, record the result, and repeat the process to obtain a collection of samples.

The term Born machine refers to a family of approaches rather than one single circuit design. Some use parameterised quantum circuits; others use tensor-network representations or different methods for constructing the quantum state.

21.1.2 Building a Born machine step by step

Consider a system containing two qubits. When measured in the computational basis, the system can produce four outcomes: 00, 01, 10, and 11. The model assigns a probability to each outcome.

Suppose a circuit prepares the state |ψ⟩ = α₀₀|00⟩ + α₀₁|01⟩ + α₁₀|10⟩ + α₁₁|11⟩. Its output probabilities are the squared magnitudes of the corresponding amplitudes.

p(00) = |α₀₀|²,   p(01) = |α₀₁|²

p(10) = |α₁₀|²,   p(11) = |α₁₁|²

The probabilities must add up to one. The circuit's gates determine the amplitudes, while the measurement basis determines how the outcomes are interpreted.

21.1.3 A numerical example

Imagine that our training data contains four possible two-bit patterns. Their observed frequencies suggest the following target distribution:

Bit string Target probability Expected count in 1,000 samples
000.50500
010.25250
100.15150
110.10100

A trained Born machine should produce these patterns with approximately the desired frequencies. The actual counts will fluctuate because quantum measurement is probabilistic. Even a perfectly trained model need not return exactly 500 instances of 00 in every batch of 1,000 measurements.

The table gives an idealised target. A real circuit may not be capable of representing every possible distribution with its chosen gates and number of qubits. Training is meaningful only within the model's representational limits.

21.1.4 Training the Born machine

Training means adjusting the circuit parameters so that its output probabilities match the target distribution as closely as possible. There are several possible objectives.

Maximum-likelihood training

If the model can evaluate the probability of each observed sample, it can maximise the likelihood of the training data. For samples x₁, x₂, ..., xₘ, the log-likelihood is:

L(θ) = Σᵢ log pθ(xᵢ)

The optimiser changes θ to increase this quantity. Equivalently, it can minimise the negative log-likelihood. Maximum-likelihood training is convenient when the probabilities can be computed or estimated reliably.

Distribution matching

Another approach compares the target distribution with the distribution obtained from measurements. For example, a model may minimise a divergence between the two distributions. The difficulty is that the complete quantum distribution can have an enormous number of outcomes, making direct estimation expensive.

For this reason, some methods use sample-based objectives, kernel distances, or other statistical measures instead of explicitly calculating every probability.

21.1.5 Why quantum correlations matter

If we prepare qubits independently, their joint distribution may have a restricted structure. Entangling gates can create correlations between qubits that cannot be explained by a product of independent pure quantum states.

This is useful when the data contains relationships between variables. For example, two pixels in an image may frequently change together, or two binary variables may be strongly correlated. A quantum circuit can encode such relationships through its joint state.

However, entanglement alone does not guarantee that the model will learn the correct correlations. The circuit architecture, available parameters, objective function, and optimisation procedure all matter. Some quantum distributions can also be represented efficiently by classical models.

21.1.6 Strengths and limitations

Potential strengthPractical difficulty
Quantum states can represent rich joint probability structures. Measurements reveal only samples, not the complete state.
Sampling can be a natural output of the circuit. Many circuit executions may be needed for accurate statistics.
Parameterized circuits allow flexible model construction. Training can become difficult as the circuit grows.
Some distributions may be difficult for classical computers to simulate. Classical hardness does not automatically establish a useful learning advantage.
Key idea: A Born machine learns a probability distribution by controlling a quantum state and using measurement probabilities as the model's output.

21.2 Quantum GANs

21.2.1 First understand a classical GAN

A Generative Adversarial Network, commonly called a GAN, contains two models: a generator and a discriminator. The generator tries to produce convincing synthetic examples. The discriminator tries to distinguish real examples from generated ones.

Think of an art student who produces imitation paintings and a critic who tries to identify which paintings are genuine. The student improves by learning from the critic's feedback. The critic also improves as the student's work becomes more convincing.

Random input
→
Generator
→
Fake sample

Real samples and fake samples are both presented to the discriminator.

Real sample
↘
Discriminator
→
Real or fake score

The generator and discriminator have opposing objectives. The generator wants generated samples to appear real, while the discriminator wants to identify the source correctly.

21.2.2 What changes in a quantum GAN?

A quantum GAN introduces one or more quantum components into this adversarial process. In a common hybrid design, the generator is a parameterised quantum circuit, while the discriminator is a classical neural network. Other designs use quantum circuits for both components.

Noise input
→
Quantum generator
→
Measured sample
↓
Classical or quantum discriminator
→
Training feedback

A hybrid quantum GAN uses quantum measurement outcomes as generated data and trains the components through an adversarial objective.

The generator may receive a random seed, encode it into qubits, apply trainable gates, and measure the resulting state. The measurement outcomes form the synthetic sample. The discriminator compares these samples with real training examples.

An important design choice is what the generator actually outputs. It may generate a bit string directly, generate a probability distribution over bit strings, or produce quantum states that are evaluated by a suitable measurement procedure. These are different tasks, and they should not be treated as interchangeable.

21.2.3 The adversarial objective

A standard GAN uses the following minimax objective:

minG maxD V(D,G) = Ex ~ pdata[log D(x)] + Ez ~ pz[log(1 − D(G(z)))]

Here, G is the generator, D is the discriminator, x is a real example, and z is a random input. The discriminator aims to assign high scores to real samples and low scores to generated samples. The generator tries to make its samples difficult to distinguish from real ones.

In a quantum GAN, the generator's parameters may be quantum gate rotations. The objective still describes an adversarial learning problem, but estimating it may involve repeated circuit execution, measurement, and classical optimisation.

The displayed objective is the original minimax form. Practical GANs often use alternative generator losses or discriminator objectives because the original form can provide weak gradients during training.

21.2.4 How training works

  1. Collect real training samples and prepare a batch of random inputs.
  2. Run the generator to produce synthetic samples.
  3. Train the discriminator to distinguish real samples from generated ones.
  4. Use the discriminator's feedback to update the generator.
  5. Repeat these steps while monitoring sample quality and training stability.

The generator and discriminator are normally updated in alternating steps. If the discriminator becomes too strong too early, the generator may receive unhelpful feedback. If the generator produces only a narrow set of examples, the model may fail to represent the diversity of the real data.

21.2.5 Mode collapse

Suppose a dataset contains many different handwritten digits. A generator might discover that a small number of digit-like patterns fool the discriminator. It then repeatedly generates similar patterns instead of covering the entire dataset.

This is called mode collapse. A model may produce convincing samples individually while failing to represent the variety of the target distribution.

Quantum generators can face this problem too. Their quantum nature does not remove the adversarial optimisation difficulties of GANs. Limited circuit expressivity, noisy gradients, finite sampling, and unstable updates can add further challenges.

21.2.6 Why use quantum components?

The research motivation is that quantum circuits may provide useful ways to represent and sample certain complex distributions. A quantum generator may also be studied in settings where quantum data or quantum states are themselves the target of generation.

Nevertheless, a quantum GAN is not automatically better than a classical GAN. It must be compared against a suitable classical baseline, using the same task, appropriate computational budgets, and reliable evaluation metrics.

Key idea: A quantum GAN combines quantum generation with adversarial feedback. The generator learns to produce samples that the discriminator struggles to distinguish from real data.

21.3 Quantum Boltzmann machines

21.3.1 Learning through energy

A Boltzmann machine is an energy-based generative model. Instead of directly assigning an independently learned probability to every possible sample, it defines an energy for each configuration. Lower-energy configurations are assigned higher probabilities.

Imagine a landscape of hills and valleys. A ball placed in the landscape is more likely to settle in a valley than on a high hill. An energy-based model uses a mathematical version of this idea to describe which configurations are more likely.

The classical Boltzmann distribution is commonly written as:

p(x) = e−βE(x) / Z E(x) is the energy of configuration x, β is the inverse temperature, and Z is the partition function.

The partition function Z normalises all the probabilities so that they sum to one:

Z = Σx e−βE(x)

At a fixed positive temperature, lower-energy states have larger probabilities. In machine learning, this framework can capture relationships among visible variables and hidden variables.

21.3.2 Visible and hidden variables

Visible variables represent the data we observe. Hidden variables provide extra flexibility for modelling relationships that are not obvious from the data alone.

For example, if a model learns images of handwritten digits, visible variables may represent the pixels. Hidden variables may help represent properties such as curved strokes, stroke thickness, or combinations of shapes. These interpretations are conceptual; hidden units do not automatically learn human-readable features.

A classical Boltzmann machine can assign energy to joint configurations of visible and hidden variables. To obtain a distribution over visible data, it sums over the possible hidden configurations.

p(v) = Σh p(v,h)

The difficulty is that calculating the distribution may require summing over a very large number of configurations. This is one reason why training and sampling from energy-based models can be computationally demanding.

21.3.3 From a classical energy model to a quantum one

A quantum Boltzmann machine uses quantum-mechanical structures to represent an energy-based model. In an important formulation, a Hamiltonian operator plays the role of energy. A Hamiltonian is a mathematical operator describing the energy of a quantum system.

A common thermal quantum state is the Gibbs state:

ρ = e−βH / Z

Z = Tr(e−βH) H is the Hamiltonian, ρ is the density operator, and Tr denotes the trace.

In a classical model, energy is a function evaluated on a configuration. In a quantum model, the Hamiltonian is an operator acting on a quantum state space. When the Hamiltonian is diagonal in the measurement basis, its eigenvalues directly determine the corresponding thermal probabilities. More general Hamiltonians can have quantum effects arising from terms that do not commute with one another.

For example, a Hamiltonian may contain interactions between qubits, local energy terms, and transverse-field terms that change the computational-basis configuration. Such non-commuting terms make the model genuinely quantum in structure.

21.3.4 What does training involve?

The goal is to adjust model parameters so that the generated distribution resembles the training data. Training may involve estimating expectations of observables, comparing data statistics against model statistics, and updating the Hamiltonian parameters.

One can think of the training process as asking two questions:

  • What patterns appear frequently in the real data?
  • How should the model's energy landscape change so that it generates those patterns with appropriate probabilities?

Depending on the formulation, learning may require thermal-state preparation, repeated sampling, approximate partition-function calculations, or quantum subroutines. These operations can be difficult on present-day hardware, particularly when low temperatures, strong correlations, or large systems are involved.

21.3.5 A small conceptual example

Consider two binary variables that are usually equal in the training data. The patterns 00 and 11 occur frequently, while 01 and 10 are rare.

The model should assign lower energy to the matching configurations and higher energy to the mismatching configurations. A quantum energy model can encode interactions between the two qubits, while additional quantum terms may alter the structure of its state.

This simple example illustrates how an energy landscape expresses statistical relationships. It does not, by itself, demonstrate a quantum advantage: a classical model can represent this two-variable distribution easily.

21.3.6 Where could quantum Boltzmann machines help?

Researchers investigate them for structured probability modelling, sampling, learning quantum systems, and tasks where quantum interactions are relevant to the data. They are also useful as a framework for studying how quantum state preparation and quantum statistics interact with machine learning.

Their practical limitations include the difficulty of preparing suitable thermal states, estimating expectations with limited measurements, and training parameters reliably. The partition function can also be difficult to estimate, although some training approaches avoid calculating it directly.

Key idea: A quantum Boltzmann machine learns an energy-based probability model using quantum states and interactions. The energy structure determines which configurations are more likely.

21.4 Quantum autoencoders

21.4.1 Why compress information?

Consider a photograph containing millions of pixel values. Many of those values are related because they describe the same objects, edges, colours, and shapes. A useful compression method tries to retain the important information while using a smaller representation.

An autoencoder learns this compression process. It contains an encoder that converts an input into a compact representation and a decoder that reconstructs the input from that representation.

Input

Encoder
→
Latent representation
→
Decoder
→
Reconstruction

The encoder compresses useful information; the decoder attempts to reconstruct the original input.

Training usually minimises a reconstruction loss that measures the difference between the original input and the reconstructed output. An autoencoder is not necessarily a generative model in the same sense as a GAN or Born machine, but its latent representation can support generative tasks when combined with an appropriate sampling or decoding mechanism.

21.4.2 The quantum version

A quantum autoencoder uses quantum circuits to encode quantum states into a smaller set of qubits or to learn a compact representation of classical data using quantum states. These are related but distinct settings, so it is important to identify which kind of input the model receives.

In a quantum-data setting, the input is a quantum state that we want to compress. The encoder is a quantum operation that moves useful information into a smaller subsystem. The remaining qubits should become approximately independent of the encoded information and, in a common compression scheme, approach a fixed reference state.

For example, a four-qubit input might have most of its meaningful variation captured by two qubits. If the other two qubits contain no additional information needed for reconstruction, they may be discarded or reset. The exact amount of compression depends on the family of states and the required reconstruction accuracy.

The encoder is often represented by a parameterised unitary U(θ). In a simple reversible construction, the decoder may use the inverse operation U†(θ). More general architectures may use a different decoder or a more general quantum channel.

If the encoder can move the unwanted subsystem towards a fixed state while retaining the information in the latent subsystem, the state becomes easier to store or process using fewer active qubits.

21.4.4 What does the loss measure?

One possible objective compares the original quantum state with the reconstructed state using fidelity. For pure states, fidelity is commonly expressed as:

F(|ψ⟩, |φ⟩) = |⟨ψ|φ⟩|²

Here, |ψ⟩ is the target state and |φ⟩ is the reconstructed state. Fidelity equals one when the two pure states are physically identical, up to an irrelevant global phase. Lower values indicate a greater difference.

A training objective may maximise fidelity or minimise 1 − F. In practice, fidelity is not always directly available as a simple measurement, so the circuit may instead optimise an experimentally accessible loss or use a method designed for the particular input-state family.

21.4.5 Quantum autoencoders as generative models

How does compression connect to generation? Once a model has learned a useful latent representation, we can try to sample latent states and decode them into new outputs. A quantum autoencoder can therefore form part of a generative pipeline.

However, compression alone does not guarantee generation. If the latent states are simply stored examples and no meaningful latent distribution has been learned, sampling new latent states may not produce realistic outputs. A complete generative design must specify how latent samples are obtained and how they are converted into meaningful data.

21.4.6 Practical uses and limitations

  • Quantum-state compression: reducing the qubits needed to represent a known family of states.
  • Quantum communication: exploring whether fewer resources can preserve the information needed by a receiver.
  • Feature learning: learning compact representations for downstream tasks.
  • Generative modelling: combining latent representations with a suitable sampling and decoding process.

The central challenge is that quantum states cannot generally be copied or inspected freely. Training examples may require repeated state preparation, and evaluating reconstruction quality may itself require carefully designed measurements.

Key idea: A quantum autoencoder learns to preserve important information in a smaller quantum representation. It becomes generative when a suitable method is added to produce and decode new latent samples.

21.5 Quantum diffusion and sampling models

21.5.1 How classical diffusion models work

Diffusion models have become an important family of classical generative models. They learn to generate data by reversing a gradual corruption process.

Imagine taking a clear photograph and repeatedly adding a small amount of random noise. After many steps, the photograph becomes difficult to recognise. A diffusion model learns a process that starts from noisy data and gradually removes the noise to produce a meaningful sample.

The forward process destroys structure; the learned reverse process attempts to recover the structure characteristic of the data.

In a common mathematical formulation, the forward process adds Gaussian noise over a sequence of time steps. A neural network learns to predict the noise or another equivalent quantity, allowing a reverse sampling procedure to gradually generate data from random noise.

21.5.2 A simplified mathematical view

Let x₀ be a clean data sample and xₜ be its noisy version at step t. A common forward-process expression is:

q(xₜ | x₀) = N(√ᾱₜ x₀, (1 − ᾱₜ)I)

Here, N denotes a Gaussian distribution, ᾱₜ controls how much of the original signal remains, and I is the identity covariance matrix. As the noise schedule progresses, the original signal is gradually obscured.

A neural network is trained to estimate the added noise or a related quantity from the noisy sample and time step. During generation, the model repeatedly uses its predictions to move towards cleaner samples.

21.5.3 What makes a quantum diffusion model different?

The term quantum diffusion model does not refer to one universally established architecture. It describes a developing research direction in which quantum circuits or quantum processes are incorporated into some part of a diffusion-style generative procedure.

Several distinct approaches are possible:

  • Quantum denoising models: use a parameterised quantum circuit, or a hybrid quantum-classical network, to estimate a denoising quantity or learn a reverse transition.
  • Quantum-state generation: model distributions over quantum states or quantum measurement outcomes rather than ordinary photographs or images.
  • Quantum sampling procedures: investigate whether a quantum process can sample from a target distribution that is difficult to sample from classically.
  • Hybrid diffusion pipelines: use classical networks for most of the denoising process while a quantum component handles a specific representation, feature transformation, or sampling step.

These approaches should not be confused with one another. A classical diffusion model that uses a quantum computer only for a small subroutine is different from a model whose primary state is a quantum state. Likewise, a quantum circuit that generates bit strings is not automatically a quantum version of an image diffusion model.

21.5.4 Can quantum circuits reverse noise?

This question needs care. An arbitrary noisy process loses information, and no general quantum circuit can perfectly reverse every possible noisy channel. Quantum circuits are constrained by quantum mechanics, including the fact that general quantum operations are not all reversible.

A practical quantum generative model therefore does not simply run an inverse of every physical noise process. It learns or implements a suitable generation procedure that produces samples with the desired distribution. In a hybrid diffusion model, this could involve a classical denoiser, a quantum circuit, or both.

21.5.5 Sampling as the broader connection

Sampling is the task of producing an outcome from a probability distribution. It is central to all the models in this chapter. Born machines sample quantum measurement outcomes. Quantum GANs generate samples to fool a discriminator. Quantum Boltzmann machines sample from energy-based distributions. Diffusion models generate samples by applying a sequence of learned transitions.

The important difference lies in how each model represents the distribution and how it learns to produce the desired outcomes.

21.5.6 The cost of repeated generation steps

A classical diffusion model may require many denoising steps. If a quantum component is invoked at every step, circuit execution, measurement, and communication with the classical computer can add considerable cost.

Reducing the number of steps, improving sample efficiency, and designing circuits that can be trained reliably are therefore important research problems. A model that produces good samples but takes too long to generate them may be less useful than a simpler classical alternative.

Key idea: Classical diffusion learns to generate data through gradual denoising. Quantum diffusion research explores whether quantum circuits or quantum sampling can improve particular parts of such generation procedures. A practical advantage has not been established for generative tasks in general.

21.6 Evaluating generative quality

21.6.1 Why evaluation is difficult

Suppose a model generates a thousand images. How do we decide whether they are good? Looking at a few samples may reveal obvious errors, but it cannot tell us whether the model represents the entire data distribution.

A generative model has several responsibilities. Its samples should be realistic, cover the important variations in the data, avoid simply memorising training examples, and be produced at an acceptable cost. No single metric captures all these properties.

Realism
Diversity
Distribution match
Generalisation
Sampling cost

A sound evaluation combines several measurements instead of relying on one attractive-looking output.

21.6.2 Distribution distance

If both the target and model distributions are known or can be estimated, we can measure how different they are. One widely used quantity is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, or KL divergence.

DKL(P || Q) = Σₓ P(x) log(P(x) / Q(x))

Here, P is the target distribution and Q is the model distribution. KL divergence is non-negative when defined, and it is zero when the distributions agree. It is not symmetric: generally, DKL(P || Q) differs from DKL(Q || P).

If the model assigns zero probability to an event that has positive probability under the target distribution, the forward KL divergence can become infinite. This makes support coverage an important concern. In large generative problems, estimating either distribution accurately can itself be difficult.

Another measure is total variation distance:

DTV(P,Q) = ½ Σₓ |P(x) − Q(x)|

Total variation measures the overall difference between discrete distributions. It ranges from zero to one. It is intuitive, but calculating it requires reliable estimates across the relevant outcomes, which may be impractical for a distribution with an enormous number of possible bit strings.

21.6.3 Fidelity for quantum states

When the goal is to generate or reconstruct quantum states, classical distribution metrics may not capture all the relevant properties. Quantum-state fidelity measures the similarity between quantum states.

For density operators ρ and σ, one common convention defines fidelity as:

F(ρ, σ) = [Tr √(√ρ σ √ρ)]²

Under this convention, fidelity lies between zero and one. A value of one means the states are identical, while smaller values indicate a greater difference. For pure states, the expression reduces to the squared overlap used earlier.

Fidelity can be useful for quantum autoencoders and quantum-state generation. However, estimating it may require multiple measurements, special experimental procedures, or additional copies of the state. It is not a universal metric for every generative task.

21.6.4 Maximum mean discrepancy

In many quantum experiments, we can generate samples but cannot efficiently evaluate every probability in the model distribution. Sample-based comparison methods are therefore useful.

Maximum mean discrepancy, or MMD, compares two distributions through the difference between their average representations in a feature space defined by a kernel function.

MMD²(P,Q) = E[k(X,X′)] + E[k(Y,Y′)] − 2E[k(X,Y)] X and X′ are independent samples from P; Y and Y′ are independent samples from Q; k is a chosen kernel.

Intuitively, the method asks whether samples from one distribution and samples from the other distribution have similar relationships under the chosen kernel. If the kernel is characteristic, a population-level MMD of zero implies that the two distributions agree.

MMD is especially useful when samples are available but explicit likelihoods are not. However, the result depends on the kernel, finite-sample uncertainty, and the quality of the sample collection. A poorly chosen kernel may fail to reveal important differences.

21.6.5 Image metrics: FID and Inception Score

When evaluating image generation, researchers often use metrics that depend on a pretrained image classifier or feature extractor. Two well-known examples are the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and the Inception Score (IS).

Fréchet Inception Distance

FID compares the distributions of feature vectors extracted from real and generated images. A common implementation approximates both feature distributions as Gaussians and compares their means and covariance matrices.

FID = ||μᵣ − μg||² + Tr(Cᵣ + Cg − 2(Cᵣ Cg)1/2)

Here, μᵣ and Cᵣ are the mean and covariance of real-image features, while μg and Cg describe generated-image features. Lower FID values generally indicate closer feature distributions under this approximation.

FID is not a universal quality score. It depends on the feature extractor, preprocessing, dataset, and number of generated samples. A low FID does not prove that the model has learned every rare pattern or that it has avoided all forms of memorisation.

Inception Score

Inception Score evaluates whether generated images are confidently classified and whether the predicted classes are diverse across the sample set. In broad terms, it rewards samples that appear recognisable to the classifier and a collection that covers multiple predicted classes.

However, it does not directly compare the generated distribution with the real distribution. It may also fail when the classifier's categories do not match the dataset or when realistic images are not well represented by those categories.

Quantum-specific caution: FID and Inception Score are useful for appropriate image-generation tasks, not for arbitrary quantum bit-string distributions. If a quantum model generates binary samples or quantum states, choose metrics suited to that output.

21.6.6 Measuring diversity and mode coverage

A generator may produce very realistic examples but repeat the same type of example over and over. This is why realism and diversity must be measured separately.

For a small binary dataset, diversity can be assessed by counting the distinct outcomes generated and comparing their frequencies with the target. For images, one can compare feature distributions, inspect nearest neighbours, and measure coverage of known classes or clusters.

Such checks help detect mode collapse, but simple unique-sample counts are not sufficient by themselves. A model can generate many distinct samples that are still unrealistic, or repeat a sample that genuinely occurs often in the target distribution.

21.6.7 Train-test separation and memorisation

A model should be evaluated on data that was not used to train it. Otherwise, a generator that memorises the training set may appear better than it really is.

For image data, nearest-neighbour comparisons can help identify generated images that are suspiciously close to training examples. For discrete distributions, compare estimated probabilities on held-out samples when likelihood evaluation is available.

Quantum models introduce an additional practical issue: measurement noise can make frequency estimates uncertain. Researchers should report the number of circuit executions, repeat experiments where appropriate, and include uncertainty estimates rather than treating every measured frequency as exact.

21.6.8 Computational cost and fair comparisons

Quality alone is not enough. A generative model may need many circuit executions to estimate its probabilities or train its parameters. A meaningful comparison should report the resources required to obtain the measured quality.

Useful quantities include:

  • Number of qubits, circuit depth, and number of trainable parameters.
  • Number of circuit executions or measurement shots.
  • Training time, generation time, and classical optimisation cost.
  • Hardware noise, sampling uncertainty, and any error mitigation.
  • Quality and diversity compared with suitable classical baselines.
  • Performance on held-out data and sensitivity to random initialisation.

A fair experiment must use comparable data, preprocessing, evaluation procedures, and resource accounting. If a quantum model is compared with an intentionally weak classical baseline, the result does not establish a meaningful advantage.

Key idea: Generative quality is multidimensional. A strong evaluation measures distribution matching, realism, diversity, generalisation, uncertainty, and computational cost using metrics suited to the particular output.

2. Comparing the main approaches

All these models are concerned with generation or representation, but they organise the learning problem differently. The right choice depends on what the model must produce and which mathematical structure is useful for that task.

Quantum GAN Trains a generator against a discriminator. Synthetic samples that resemble training data. Adversarial instability and mode collapse.
Quantum Boltzmann machine Uses an energy-based quantum model. Samples from an energy-defined distribution. State preparation, sampling, and parameter estimation.
Quantum autoencoder Compresses information into a smaller representation. Latent representations or reconstructed states; generation with an added sampling mechanism. Preserving information and evaluating reconstruction.
Quantum diffusion model Incorporates quantum processes into diffusion-style generation or sampling. Generated samples or quantum states, depending on the design. Repeated execution cost and the lack of a generally established advantage.

How to choose a model for a research problem

If the problem is to learn a distribution over binary measurement outcomes, a Born machine is a natural starting point. If an adversarial objective is important, a quantum GAN may be suitable. If the problem is naturally expressed through energy and interactions, a quantum Boltzmann machine is worth studying.

If the goal is to compress a family of quantum states, a quantum autoencoder is more directly relevant. If the goal is iterative denoising or generation from noise, diffusion-style methods are the appropriate conceptual family.

These are not strict boundaries. The models can be combined. For example, a quantum autoencoder may provide a latent representation for another generative model, or a quantum circuit may act as a component inside a classical diffusion pipeline.

3. A practical learning experiment

3.1 Start with a small distribution

Before trying to generate photographs or large datasets, begin with a problem whose complete probability distribution is easy to inspect. A two-qubit Born machine is a useful educational experiment because there are only four possible measurement outcomes.

The experiment can be organised as follows:

  1. Define a target distribution over the four two-bit strings.
  2. Build a parameterised circuit with two qubits.
  3. Execute the circuit repeatedly and count its measurement outcomes.
  4. Convert the counts into estimated probabilities.
  5. Define a suitable loss comparing model samples with the target.
  6. Optimise the circuit parameters and repeat the measurements.
  7. Evaluate the final distribution on new measurement batches.

This experiment teaches the central loop shared by many quantum generative models: prepare, sample, compare, update, and evaluate.

3.2 Pseudocode for training a Born machine

target_distribution = {
    "00": 0.50,
    "01": 0.25,
    "10": 0.15,
    "11": 0.10
}

parameters = initialise_circuit_parameters()

for step in range(number_of_training_steps):
    circuit = build_quantum_circuit(parameters)

    counts = execute_and_measure(
        circuit,
        shots=number_of_measurement_shots
    )

    model_distribution = estimate_probabilities(counts)

    loss = compare_distributions(
        target_distribution,
        model_distribution
    )

    gradients = estimate_parameter_gradients(
        parameters,
        loss
    )

    parameters = optimiser_update(
        parameters,
        gradients
    )

final_counts = execute_and_measure(
    build_quantum_circuit(parameters),
    shots=evaluation_shots
)

evaluate_distribution(
    target_distribution,
    final_counts
)

This is conceptual pseudocode, not a complete implementation for a specific quantum SDK. The gradient method must match the circuit and loss. A sample-based loss may be noisy, and the optimiser must account for that uncertainty.

3.3 What should be recorded?

  • Target probabilities and measured probabilities before training.
  • Target probabilities and measured probabilities after training.
  • The loss across training steps.
  • The number of measurement shots used at each step.
  • Variation across repeated runs with different initial parameters.
  • Performance against a simple classical baseline.

An important lesson is that a better training loss does not always mean that the model has learned the target distribution reliably. If the training loss is estimated from a small number of samples, it may improve by chance. Independent evaluation batches help reveal this problem.

3.4 Suggested progression

Stage 1 — Probability
Understand normalised distributions, conditional probability, and sampling error.

Stage 2 — Quantum circuits
Build rotation gates, entangling gates, parameterised circuits, and computational-basis measurements.

Stage 3 — Optimisation
Implement a loss function and compare gradient-based optimisation with derivative-free methods where appropriate.

Stage 4 — Model comparison
Compare a Born machine with a classical distribution model using the same target data.

Stage 5 — Research question
Increase the problem size or change the target distribution, then investigate where training, sampling, or classical simulation becomes difficult.

4. Limitations and open research questions

4.1 The data-loading problem

A quantum model must receive information about the training data. Encoding classical data into a quantum state can be expensive. If preparing the quantum input costs more than the work saved by the quantum computation, the overall approach may not be advantageous.

This is particularly important when the training dataset is large. An efficient circuit does not automatically imply an efficient end-to-end machine-learning pipeline.

4.2 Measurement and sampling costs

Quantum measurements are probabilistic. Estimating an outcome probability accurately generally requires repeated circuit executions. For a simple measurement with probability p, the standard deviation of its empirical frequency after N independent shots is √(p(1 − p)/N).

Therefore, increasing the number of shots reduces statistical uncertainty, but the improvement follows a square-root relationship. Obtaining ten times smaller standard deviation can require roughly one hundred times as many independent shots under the same conditions.

4.3 Noise and hardware restrictions

Real quantum processors experience errors caused by imperfect gates, measurement errors, and interactions with their environment. Deeper circuits can accumulate more errors, making it harder to obtain the intended output distribution.

Error mitigation can improve some estimates, but it often introduces extra measurements or computational overhead. It does not guarantee that a noisy generative model will recover the ideal distribution.

4.4 Trainability and barren plateaus

Many quantum generative models rely on parameterised circuits. If the gradients become extremely small, optimisation may struggle to find useful parameter updates. This problem is related to barren plateaus, discussed in the chapter on trainability.

The difficulty depends on the circuit architecture, initialisation, objective function, and noise. Useful research directions include structured circuit designs, local objectives, layerwise training, and better gradient-estimation methods.

4.5 What counts as quantum advantage?

A quantum model producing plausible samples is not enough to prove quantum advantage. A strong claim must specify the task and show that the quantum method outperforms appropriate classical approaches under a clearly defined measure of computational cost or solution quality.

Some quantum sampling problems are believed to be difficult to reproduce classically, but sampling hardness and useful learning advantage are different claims. A model may be difficult to simulate while still being difficult to train, difficult to evaluate, or irrelevant to the real-world task.

4.6 Important research questions

  • Which data distributions can quantum models learn more efficiently than the best classical alternatives?
  • How should the cost of loading data and measuring outputs be included in comparisons?
  • Can circuit architectures be designed to avoid barren plateaus while representing useful correlations?
  • How can researchers evaluate a model when the complete target distribution is unavailable?
  • Can quantum generative models provide useful benefits for quantum-state preparation, chemistry, or scientific sampling?
  • How can quantum and classical components be combined without making the complete pipeline unnecessarily expensive?
Research perspective: Quantum generative modelling is an active area of investigation, not a settled replacement for classical generative AI. Its value must be established separately for each task and model design.

5. Chapter summary

Quantum generative models connect three central ideas: probability distributions, trainable quantum processes, and sampling. Their architectures differ, but all attempt to represent or produce structured outcomes that follow a desired pattern.

  1. Born machines use the Born rule to convert quantum state amplitudes into probabilities and generate samples.
  2. Quantum GANs train a generator against a discriminator so that generated samples become difficult to distinguish from real samples.
  3. Quantum Boltzmann machines use energy-based quantum models to assign probabilities to configurations and learn distributions.
  4. Quantum autoencoders learn compact representations and can support generation when combined with a suitable latent sampling mechanism.
  5. Quantum diffusion and sampling models explore quantum components in iterative generation, denoising, and distribution-sampling procedures.
  6. Generative evaluation requires measures of distribution matching, sample quality, diversity, generalisation, uncertainty, and computational cost.
The most important lesson: A quantum circuit does not become a useful generative model simply because it contains qubits. It must learn a meaningful distribution, generate useful samples, and demonstrate its value through careful evaluation and comparison.

Further thought

Suppose a two-qubit Born machine learns a distribution over four bit strings. A classical computer can easily represent the same distribution. What would have to change for the quantum model to become scientifically interesting?

A useful answer would identify a larger or more structured task, specify an appropriate classical baseline, account for state preparation and measurement costs, and demonstrate a reproducible improvement in sampling or learning. This is the difference between demonstrating a quantum circuit and establishing a meaningful machine-learning result.
