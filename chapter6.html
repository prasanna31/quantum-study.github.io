The Quantum Fourier Transform and Phase Estimation

Why do we need these techniques?

Imagine listening to a musical recording. You hear a mixture of sounds, but the recording may contain several notes at different frequencies. A Fourier transform helps separate those frequencies so that we can understand which notes are present and how strongly they contribute.

A similar problem appears in computing. Sometimes, the information we need is not obvious when data is represented in its original form. A useful pattern may become much easier to recognise after changing the way the information is represented.

The Fourier Transform is a mathematical tool for changing representations. It appears in signal processing, image processing, communications and many other areas of classical computing.

Quantum computing has its own version, called the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT). It changes the amplitudes of quantum states in a carefully controlled way. This can reveal patterns related to periodicity and phase, provided the quantum algorithm is designed so that the desired information becomes measurable.

The second major idea in this chapter is Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE). It uses quantum operations and measurements to estimate a phase associated with a quantum operation. QPE uses the QFT as one of its central ingredients.

The connection between the two The QFT is a transformation that helps convert phase patterns into measurement probabilities. QPE uses this transformation to estimate an unknown phase. In short, the QFT provides an important mathematical tool, while QPE uses that tool to solve a particular problem.

A small amount of mathematical preparation

Before proceeding, recall that a classical bit is either 0 or 1. A qubit can be in a quantum state written as

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

Here, α and β are complex numbers called probability amplitudes. Their squared magnitudes determine the probabilities of measurement:

P(0) = |α|²,    P(1) = |β|²
|α|² + |β|² = 1

The amplitudes contain more information than the probabilities alone. In particular, their relative phases affect how quantum states interfere with one another. The QFT takes advantage of this property.

For multiple qubits, a state can be written as a combination of computational basis states. For example, two qubits have the basis states |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩ and |11⟩. A general two-qubit state is

|ψ⟩ = α₀|00⟩ + α₁|01⟩ + α₂|10⟩ + α₃|11⟩

The same idea extends to n qubits, which have 2ⁿ computational basis states. The QFT acts on the amplitudes associated with these states.

6.1 The Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT)

6.1.1 Understanding the basic idea

Suppose a teacher gives you a long sequence of numbers and asks you to find a repeating pattern. Looking at the numbers directly may not be enough. You might need a different representation to make the pattern visible.

The Fourier Transform performs this kind of change of representation. In classical mathematics, it can represent a signal in terms of frequency components. The QFT performs a related operation on the amplitudes of a quantum state.

It is important not to misunderstand this comparison. The QFT does not simply inspect every possible answer and print the result. A quantum computer still produces measurement outcomes according to the probabilities of the final state. The advantage comes from arranging amplitudes so that useful outcomes become more likely.

6.1.2 The mathematical definition

Let N = 2ⁿ, where n is the number of qubits. The QFT transforms a computational basis state |x⟩ into a superposition of basis states with carefully chosen complex phases.

QFTN|x⟩ = (1/√N) ∑y=0N−1 e2πixy/N|y⟩

Let us understand each part of this expression rather than trying to memorise it immediately.

  • N: the number of computational basis states, equal to 2ⁿ for n qubits.
  • x: the integer represented by the input basis state.
  • y: the index of each output basis state.
  • 1/√N: the normalisation factor that ensures the total probability is one.
  • e2πixy/N: a complex phase factor that determines the relative phase of each output amplitude.

The summation means that the output is a superposition over all N basis states. Each state has the same amplitude magnitude when the input is a basis state, but the phases differ according to x and y.

This phase pattern is the key. The QFT does not merely spread an input across many basis states. It creates a structured relationship between the input and output amplitudes.

6.1.3 A worked example with one qubit

For one qubit, N = 2. The QFT transforms the two computational basis states as follows:

QFT₂|0⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2

QFT₂|1⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2

The first transformation is familiar. It is exactly the action of the Hadamard gate on |0⟩. The second is also the action of the Hadamard gate, but the relative sign is different.

Why does the minus sign matter? Consider the states (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2. Both give a 50% probability of measuring 0 and a 50% probability of measuring 1 in the computational basis. Yet they are different quantum states. When combined with other quantum operations, their relative phases can produce different interference patterns.

The Hadamard gate and the one-qubit QFT therefore coincide, but this should not lead us to conclude that the QFT is just a Hadamard gate. For several qubits, the QFT also needs controlled phase rotations.

6.1.4 A two-qubit example

Two qubits have four basis states, so N = 4. Consider the input |1⟩, represented in binary as |01⟩. Its QFT is

QFT₄|1⟩ = (1/2)(|0⟩ + i|1⟩ − |2⟩ − i|3⟩)

In two-qubit notation, the same result is

QFT₄|01⟩ = (1/2)(|00⟩ + i|01⟩ − |10⟩ − i|11⟩)

Notice that all four states have an amplitude magnitude of 1/2. Consequently, measuring immediately in the computational basis gives each outcome a probability of 1/4. The phase factors 1, i, −1 and −i do not change these immediate probabilities.

However, those phases are not useless. A later operation can make the amplitudes interfere, changing the probabilities of measurement. Quantum algorithms are designed to use this interference to make certain answers easier to observe.

6.1.5 What happens when the input is a superposition?

Quantum operations are linear. This means that if the input is a combination of basis states, the QFT transforms each component and combines the resulting amplitudes.

For example, suppose

|ψ⟩ = a|0⟩ + b|1⟩

Applying the QFT gives

QFT₂|ψ⟩ = [(a+b)|0⟩ + (a−b)|1⟩]/√2

This expression shows why phases and amplitudes matter. The output amplitude of |0⟩ depends on a+b, whereas the output amplitude of |1⟩ depends on a−b. Depending on the values of a and b, the components may reinforce or cancel one another.

This is quantum interference: probability amplitudes are combined before measurement, and their relative phases determine the resulting probabilities.

6.1.6 Why is the QFT useful for periodic patterns?

A periodic sequence repeats after a particular interval. In quantum algorithms, a computation may create a state whose amplitudes have a repeating structure. The QFT can transform this structure into a distribution concentrated around particular frequencies.

Imagine a pattern of evenly spaced marks on a circular track. A suitable Fourier transform can reveal the spacing by producing strong responses at related frequencies. In a quantum algorithm, the same general principle applies to structured amplitudes and phases, although the exact output depends on the state being transformed.

This property is especially important in algorithms that search for periods, including the order-finding procedure used by Shor's factoring algorithm. The QFT helps turn information about a hidden period into measurement outcomes that can be processed classically.

The central lesson of the QFT The QFT is not valuable simply because it creates a superposition. Its importance lies in how it rearranges amplitudes and relative phases. When the input contains useful structure, this transformation can help make that structure visible through measurement.

6.2 Circuit implementation and complexity

6.2.1 From a mathematical formula to quantum gates

The QFT definition describes a transformation mathematically. A quantum computer, however, needs a circuit made of physical or simulated quantum gates to perform it.

The circuit implementation of the QFT relies mainly on three kinds of operations:

  1. Hadamard gates: create and combine superposition components.
  2. Controlled phase rotations: introduce phase changes depending on the state of another qubit.
  3. SWAP gates: exchange the positions of qubits when required by the chosen circuit convention.

The circuit builds the required phase relationships one step at a time. Instead of calculating the entire transformation as one large operation, it uses a sequence of simpler gates.

6.2.2 The Hadamard gate

The Hadamard gate is one of the basic tools in quantum computing. Its matrix representation is

H = (1/√2) [[1, 1], [1, −1]]

Its action on the computational basis states is

H|0⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2

H|1⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2

Thus, the Hadamard gate changes a basis state into a superposition. It also allows the relative sign of the amplitudes to depend on the input. This makes it a natural building block for the QFT.

6.2.3 Controlled phase rotations

A phase rotation changes the phase of a quantum amplitude without necessarily changing its magnitude. A controlled phase rotation applies such a change only when the control qubit is in the specified state.

A common controlled phase gate is written as

CP(θ) = diag(1, 1, 1, eiθ)

In the computational basis |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩ and |11⟩, this gate leaves the first three basis states unchanged and multiplies the amplitude of |11⟩ by eiθ.

The QFT uses controlled rotations with angles that become progressively smaller. A commonly used family of angles is

θk = 2π/2k

For example, the first few angles are π, π/2 and π/4. The exact gate labels and qubit ordering depend on the circuit convention, but the underlying idea remains the same: the controlled rotations establish the phase relationships needed by the Fourier transform.

6.2.4 The general structure of a QFT circuit

In a standard n-qubit QFT circuit, each qubit participates in a Hadamard operation and controlled rotations connecting it with other qubits. A final reversal of the qubit order may be needed to match the usual binary representation of the output.

Some implementations also return the output in reversed bit order and leave the SWAP gates out. These conventions change how the circuit is written, not the mathematical transformation it represents.

6.2.5 Why does the QFT require so few gates?

An n-qubit state has 2ⁿ amplitudes. If we tried to represent the full QFT as a general matrix, that matrix would have 2ⁿ rows and 2ⁿ columns. It would contain an enormous number of entries as n increases.

But the QFT has a special structure. We can exploit that structure to implement it using a sequence of elementary quantum gates.

A standard exact QFT circuit uses:

  • n Hadamard gates.
  • n(n−1)/2 controlled phase rotations.
  • At most floor(n/2) SWAP gates for reversing the qubit order.

Therefore, the number of elementary gates grows quadratically with the number of qubits, giving a gate complexity of O(n²) when controlled rotations are counted individually.

Number of controlled rotations = n(n−1)/2

Total standard gate count = O(n²)

This is much smaller than the O(4ⁿ) number of entries in a general 2ⁿ by 2ⁿ matrix. The comparison is between the circuit gate count and the size of the general matrix representation. It does not mean that every task involving the QFT becomes exponentially faster.

6.2.6 Approximate QFT

The controlled rotations in a QFT become smaller as their separation between qubits increases. For sufficiently distant qubits, the associated angles can be extremely small.

In some applications, these small rotations can be omitted to obtain an approximate QFT. The circuit then uses fewer gates, while introducing a controlled approximation error.

If rotations smaller than a chosen threshold are omitted, the number of retained rotations can be reduced substantially. For common approximate-QFT constructions with a suitable cutoff, the gate count can be brought down to O(n log n), with the approximation accuracy depending on how the cutoff is selected.

Approximation is useful when the saved gates are more valuable than the small loss in precision. However, it should not be used blindly. A phase-estimation problem may require greater precision than another application, so the circuit must be chosen according to the task.

6.2.7 Circuit cost is not the whole story

The number of gates is only one measure of efficiency. On actual quantum hardware, we must also consider how reliably each gate works, how long qubits remain coherent, whether two qubits can interact directly, and how expensive it is to move quantum information between different locations.

A circuit with fewer mathematical operations may still perform poorly if its gates are difficult to execute on the available hardware. This is why practical quantum circuit design involves both mathematics and engineering.

6.3 Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE)

6.3.1 What is a quantum phase?

We have already seen that a quantum state can contain complex amplitudes. These amplitudes may have both magnitude and phase. Phase is particularly important when different parts of a quantum state later interfere.

Quantum Phase Estimation is a procedure for estimating a phase associated with a unitary operation. A unitary operation is a reversible quantum transformation that preserves the total probability of the state.

Suppose a unitary operation U acts on a state |u⟩ according to

U|u⟩ = e2πiφ|u⟩

Here, |u⟩ is an eigenstate of U, and φ is its phase expressed in cycles. We can take 0 ≤ φ < 1, because phases that differ by an integer represent the same complex factor in this expression.

The goal of QPE is to estimate φ. For example, if φ = 0.375, the procedure attempts to determine a binary representation of that value from quantum measurements.

Why estimate a phase instead of measuring it directly? The phase is not an ordinary label that can simply be read from a quantum state. A global phase multiplying an entire state cannot be observed on its own. QPE works by comparing phase information through controlled operations, converting it into relative phases that affect measurable outcomes.

6.3.2 The main ingredients of QPE

A standard QPE circuit contains two registers:

  1. The counting register: a collection of qubits that stores information about the phase. Hadamard gates are applied to place this register into a superposition.
  2. The target register: a register prepared in the eigenstate |u⟩ of the unitary operation U.

The circuit then uses controlled applications of U, U², U⁴ and higher powers. These operations encode increasingly detailed information about the phase into the counting register. Finally, an inverse Quantum Fourier Transform converts the encoded phase pattern into a form that can be measured.

There are three main stages: prepare a superposition, encode the phase using controlled powers of U, and apply the inverse QFT before measurement.

6.3.3 Stage one: prepare the counting register

Suppose the counting register contains m qubits. It has M = 2ᵐ possible computational basis states. Initially, we prepare it in the state |0⟩ repeated m times.

Applying a Hadamard gate to each qubit creates an equal superposition:

|0⟩⊗m → (1/√M) ∑k=0M−1 |k⟩

The counting register now contains amplitudes for every integer from 0 to M−1. This is the starting point for encoding the phase.

6.3.4 Stage two: encode the phase

Each counting qubit controls a power of U. The qubit associated with the lowest power controls U, the next controls U², the next controls U⁴, and so on.

Because U|u⟩ = e2πiφ|u⟩, repeated applications give

U2ʲ|u⟩ = e2πi2ʲφ|u⟩

Each controlled operation introduces a phase that depends on the corresponding power of two. Taken together, these phases create a structured pattern in the counting register.

The target register remains in |u⟩, apart from the action of the unitary operation, because |u⟩ is its eigenstate. The useful phase information is encoded in the counting register.

6.3.5 Stage three: apply the inverse QFT

After the controlled operations, the counting register contains a phase pattern related to φ. The inverse QFT, written as QFT†, transforms this pattern into a distribution over binary outcomes.

If the phase has an exact m-bit binary representation, the ideal circuit produces the corresponding m-bit result with probability one. If the phase cannot be represented exactly using m bits, the measurement probabilities are spread across nearby outcomes.

The inverse QFT is used here because the controlled operations prepare a state with the phase pattern of a Fourier transform. Applying the inverse transformation converts that pattern back into a computational basis result that encodes the phase.

6.3.7 A numerical example: estimating φ = 0.375

Let us consider a phase that can be represented exactly using three binary digits:

φ = 0.375 = 3/8 = 0.011₂

The binary fraction 0.011₂ means

0 × 1/2 + 1 × 1/4 + 1 × 1/8 = 0.375

We use three counting qubits, giving M = 2³ = 8 possible outcomes. Under ideal conditions, with the correct eigenstate and controlled operations, QPE returns the binary integer 011₂, which is 3 in decimal.

The phase estimate is then obtained by dividing the measured integer by M:

φ̂ = y/M = 3/8 = 0.375

This example is especially convenient because the phase is exactly representable using three binary digits. Real problems are not always so convenient.

6.3.8 What if the phase cannot be represented exactly?

Suppose the true phase is 0.3. With three counting qubits, the possible estimates are multiples of 1/8: 0, 1/8, 2/8, 3/8 and so on.

Since 0.3 is not one of these values, the circuit cannot return it exactly using a single three-bit measurement. Instead, the measurement distribution is concentrated around nearby binary fractions. More counting qubits provide a finer grid of possible estimates.

Increasing the number of counting qubits improves the available precision, but it also increases the circuit resources needed for the controlled operations and the inverse QFT. On real hardware, additional gates may also introduce additional errors.

Phase estimate: φ̂ = y/2ᵐ

Resolution of the binary grid: 1/2ᵐ

The grid spacing is not, by itself, a guarantee that the estimate will be accurate to within that spacing. The probability of obtaining a particular outcome depends on the true phase and the circuit. More precision requires both adequate resources and a reliable implementation.

6.3.9 Why does QPE need an eigenstate?

The usual explanation of QPE assumes that the target register starts in an eigenstate |u⟩ of U. This assumption is important because the unitary operation then multiplies the state by a well-defined phase while leaving its eigenstate unchanged.

If the target is instead a superposition of several eigenstates, each component has its own eigenphase. QPE can then produce measurement outcomes associated with the different phases, with probabilities determined by the weights of those components.

This is not necessarily a failure. In some applications, the aim is to learn about the eigenvalues present in a quantum state. However, it means that the result must be interpreted differently from the single-eigenstate example.

QFT and QPE are not the same thing The QFT is a quantum transformation. QPE is a larger algorithm that uses controlled unitary operations, a counting register, an inverse QFT and measurement to estimate a phase. The QFT alone does not automatically reveal an unknown eigenphase.

6.4 Applications and error considerations

6.4.1 Application one: order finding and Shor's algorithm

One of the best-known applications of the QFT is the order-finding procedure used in Shor's factoring algorithm. The order of a number a modulo N is the smallest positive integer r such that

aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N)

Finding this order can help factor a composite integer N, provided the selected values and the resulting order satisfy the conditions needed by the factoring procedure.

The quantum part of Shor's algorithm creates a state containing information about repeated behaviour in modular arithmetic. The QFT is used to help extract information about the period. A classical algorithm then processes the measurement result to obtain useful candidates for the order.

This illustrates a recurring pattern in quantum algorithms: the quantum circuit prepares and transforms a state, measurement produces a limited classical result, and classical computation interprets that result.

Does the QFT factor a number by itself? No. The QFT is one ingredient in a larger algorithm. Shor's factoring algorithm also needs modular arithmetic, carefully chosen inputs, measurements and classical post-processing. The complete procedure is what provides the factoring advantage.

6.4.2 Application two: quantum phase estimation

QPE is useful whenever the phase of an eigenvalue contains information about a problem. The phase may be related to an energy, a frequency, or a property of a mathematical operation.

The general approach is to construct a suitable unitary operation, prepare a suitable target state, and use controlled applications of the unitary to encode the phase. The inverse QFT then converts this phase information into measurement outcomes.

The difficult part is often not the inverse QFT itself. Preparing the right state and implementing the controlled unitary accurately may require substantial resources.

6.4.3 Application three: quantum simulation and energy estimation

In quantum chemistry and physics, researchers often want to estimate the energies associated with a quantum system. Such problems involve the eigenvalues of an operator called the Hamiltonian, which describes the system's energy.

Under suitable conditions, a Hamiltonian can be used to construct a unitary time-evolution operation. The eigenphases of this operation are related to the system's energy eigenvalues. Phase estimation can then be used to estimate those energies.

For example, in an idealised setting, a time-evolution operator can be written as

U(t) = e−iHt/ℏ

Here, H is the Hamiltonian, t is the evolution time, and ℏ is the reduced Planck constant. If an energy eigenstate has energy E, the corresponding evolution introduces a phase related to E. Estimating that phase can therefore provide information about the energy.

This is one reason phase estimation is important in quantum simulation. However, useful energy estimation also depends on preparing a state with overlap with the desired energy eigenstate, controlling errors, and choosing suitable evolution times.

6.4.4 Application four: period detection

Periodic structures occur in many mathematical problems. A function has period r when its values repeat after shifts of size r, under the relevant definition of periodicity.

Quantum algorithms can sometimes encode information about such a period into amplitudes or phases. A Fourier transform can then produce a measurement distribution that reveals information about the period.

The connection is not limited to one specific algorithm. It reflects the general usefulness of Fourier methods for detecting regularity and repeated structure. The challenge is to prepare a quantum state whose structure carries the required information.

6.4.5 Understanding the main sources of error

The mathematical descriptions of QFT and QPE assume ideal operations. Actual quantum computers are imperfect. Their physical qubits interact with their surroundings, gates have limited accuracy, and measurements may return incorrect results.

These issues are particularly important in QPE because phase information is encoded through controlled operations. Errors can change the phase relationships and distort the final measurement distribution.

Error source 1: gate errors

A physical gate may not implement the exact mathematical operation specified by the circuit. For instance, a controlled phase rotation intended to introduce a particular angle may introduce a slightly different angle.

Since a QFT circuit uses many gates, small errors can accumulate. Their exact effect depends on the type of error, the circuit structure and the input state. It is not always correct to assume that all errors simply add together.

Error source 2: decoherence

Quantum states are fragile. Interaction with the environment can disturb the phase relationships or the populations of the states. This process is broadly called decoherence, although noise models also include other kinds of physical errors.

If the phase information is lost before the controlled operations and inverse QFT are completed, the final measurement may no longer provide a reliable estimate.

Error source 3: measurement errors

A measurement device may incorrectly identify whether a qubit is in state 0 or state 1. Since QPE reads the phase estimate from a collection of measured bits, mistakes can change the final binary number.

Repeating a circuit can help characterise its measurement distribution. However, repetition does not automatically eliminate systematic errors or correct errors that affect every run in a similar way.

Error source 4: limited phase precision

With m counting qubits, the output estimate is based on multiples of 1/2ᵐ. If the true phase lies between two such values, the circuit may return a nearby estimate rather than the exact phase.

Adding counting qubits provides finer resolution. Yet greater precision may require more controlled applications of the unitary, which can increase circuit depth and exposure to noise. Precision and physical reliability must therefore be considered together.

Error source 5: approximate QFT

Omitting small controlled rotations reduces the circuit size, but it changes the transformation. In phase estimation, this approximation can alter the output probabilities.

An approximate QFT may be a good choice when its error is small enough for the intended task. The correct cutoff depends on the required accuracy and the resources available on the quantum device.

6.4.6 Practical methods for reducing errors

  • Choose an appropriate circuit: remove unnecessary gates and use an approximate QFT only when the accuracy is sufficient.
  • Use hardware-aware compilation: arrange the circuit to work with the device's available connections and supported gates.
  • Calibrate and characterise the hardware: understand the accuracy of gates and measurements before relying on the results.
  • Repeat measurements where appropriate: use repeated runs to estimate the output distribution and distinguish likely results from statistical fluctuations.
  • Use error mitigation when suitable: apply validated techniques that reduce the effect of known errors, while recognising that mitigation does not guarantee perfect results.
  • Use quantum error correction when feasible: encode logical information across physical qubits and use error-correction procedures to protect the computation. This requires additional hardware and resources.
A useful engineering principle A mathematically efficient circuit is not automatically a practical circuit. The best implementation balances the number of gates, the required phase precision, the hardware's noise characteristics and the cost of running the computation.

6.4.7 Exact QFT, approximate QFT and QPE compared

Technique Main purpose Important consideration
Exact QFT Performs the precise Fourier transformation on the chosen number of qubits. Uses a number of gates that grows quadratically with the qubit count in the standard circuit.
Approximate QFT Performs a close approximation using fewer controlled rotations. Introduces approximation error in exchange for reduced circuit cost.
QPE Estimates an eigenphase using controlled unitary operations and the inverse QFT. Requires a suitable target state, sufficient precision and reliable operations.

Chapter summary

The Quantum Fourier Transform and Quantum Phase Estimation are closely connected, but they serve different purposes. The QFT transforms the amplitudes of a quantum state by introducing carefully structured phase relationships. These relationships can help expose periodic patterns when the input state has the appropriate structure.

A standard QFT circuit uses Hadamard gates, controlled phase rotations and, depending on the convention, SWAP gates. Its elementary gate count grows as O(n²) for n qubits. Approximate implementations can use fewer gates at the cost of some precision.

QPE uses controlled powers of a unitary operation to encode its eigenphase into a counting register. The inverse QFT then converts that phase information into measurement outcomes. With a suitable eigenstate and ideal operations, QPE can estimate the phase to the precision supported by the counting register.

These ideas support important algorithms in period finding, integer factoring and quantum simulation. Their practical success depends not only on mathematical correctness but also on state preparation, circuit complexity, noise and measurement reliability.

Remember these five ideas
  1. The QFT changes the representation of quantum amplitudes and phases.
  2. Quantum interference allows phase relationships to influence measurement probabilities.
  3. The standard QFT can be implemented using O(n²) elementary gates.
  4. QPE uses controlled powers of a unitary and the inverse QFT to estimate an eigenphase.
  5. More precision requires suitable resources, and hardware noise can reduce the accuracy of the result.
