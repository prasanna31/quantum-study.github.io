Quantum Reinforcement Learning

How can an intelligent system learn to make better decisions through experience, and where might quantum computing help it learn?

This chapter builds the idea from ordinary reinforcement learning to quantum policies, value functions, exploration and real-world control problems.

The central idea: learning by making decisions

Reinforcement learning is a way of teaching a computer to make decisions by allowing it to interact with an environment and learn from the consequences of its actions.

Think about learning to ride a bicycle. Nobody gives you a complete mathematical description of how to balance your body, turn the handlebar and control your speed. You try, observe what happens, make mistakes and gradually improve. You learn which actions help and which actions cause problems.

Reinforcement learning follows a similar principle. An agent takes an action, receives feedback and uses that experience to improve its future decisions. The feedback is called a reward. The goal is not simply to obtain a reward immediately. It is to learn a strategy that produces good results over time.

Quantum reinforcement learning combines this learning framework with quantum computing. A quantum circuit may represent a policy, estimate the value of a state, or help solve a subproblem involved in choosing actions.

One important distinction: reinforcement learning describes how an agent learns to make decisions. Quantum computing describes a way of processing information using quantum systems. Combining them does not automatically make the learning process faster or better. That advantage must be demonstrated for the particular problem.

What makes quantum reinforcement learning interesting?

Quantum states can contain superpositions, and quantum operations can create entanglement and interfere with one another. These properties allow quantum circuits to represent and process certain kinds of information differently from ordinary circuits.

Researchers investigate whether these properties can improve policy representation, sampling, optimisation or exploration. However, an agent still needs useful rewards, a suitable learning algorithm and reliable interaction with its environment.

Example: Imagine a robot that must move a box through a warehouse without hitting people or other objects. Classical reinforcement learning can learn a navigation strategy. A quantum reinforcement learning system might use a parameterised quantum circuit to represent its action probabilities. Both systems must still obey the physical rules of the warehouse.

22.1 — Reinforcement learning primer

1. The four main ingredients

Every reinforcement learning problem contains an agent, an environment, actions and rewards. The agent is the decision-maker. The environment is the world with which it interacts.

  • Agent: the system that learns what to do.
  • Environment: the world or process being controlled.
  • Action: a choice made by the agent.
  • Reward: numerical feedback indicating how desirable an outcome is.

The environment also has a state, which describes the situation at a particular moment. For a warehouse robot, the state could include its location, speed, nearby obstacles and the position of the box.

The basic interaction is a repeated cycle: observe the situation, choose an action, receive a reward and observe the new situation. This cycle continues until the task ends or the agent reaches a predefined stopping condition.

2. States, actions and transitions

Let the state at time step t be written as st, and let the action be at. After the agent acts, the environment moves to a new state, st+1, and returns a reward, rt+1.

st  →  at  →  (rt+1, st+1)

This transition captures the essential feedback loop. The same action may produce different outcomes when the environment is uncertain. A robot moving forward might make progress on an empty path but collide with an obstacle if the path is blocked.

In a fully observable Markov decision process, or MDP, the state contains the information needed to model the distribution of future states and rewards given the current state and action. Formally, the next-state distribution does not need the entire history once the current state and action are known.

P(st+1 | st, at, history) = P(st+1 | st, at)

This is the Markov property. It is a modelling assumption, not a rule that every real-world system automatically satisfies.

3. The policy: deciding what to do

A policy is the agent's decision-making strategy. It tells the agent which action to choose in a given state. A policy can be deterministic or stochastic.

A deterministic policy chooses one action for each state: a = π(s). A stochastic policy assigns a probability to each possible action: π(a | s).

Suppose a robot has three choices: move left, move straight or move right. Its policy might assign probabilities of 0.2, 0.6 and 0.2, respectively. These probabilities sum to one. The robot can sample an action from this distribution rather than always selecting the same action.

Why use probabilities? A robot that always chooses the same action may fail to discover a better route. Stochastic policies allow controlled variation in decisions, which can be useful during learning.

4. Rewards are not the same as long-term success

A reward measures the immediate feedback received from the environment. However, the best immediate reward may not lead to the best overall outcome.

Consider a delivery robot. Taking a shortcut might save a few seconds now but create a high risk of collision. A sensible learning objective must account for future consequences, not just immediate progress.

The discounted return from time t is commonly defined as:

Gt = rt+1 + γrt+2 + γ²rt+3 + ...

Here, γ is the discount factor, normally between zero and one for the standard discounted formulation. A small γ gives more importance to immediate rewards. A value closer to one gives greater importance to future rewards.

For example, with γ = 0.9, a reward received two steps later contributes with a factor of 0.81. This does not mean the reward itself becomes smaller in the environment. Discounting expresses how the learning objective weighs future outcomes.

5. Value functions: estimating future rewards

A value function estimates how useful a state is when the agent follows a particular policy. It answers a question such as: "If I find myself in this situation, how much reward can I reasonably expect to collect in the future?"

The state-value function is:

Vπ(s) = Eπ[Gt | st = s]

The expectation averages over possible future outcomes when the agent follows policy π. A high value indicates a state from which the policy expects to achieve a high return.

An action-value function, also called a Q-function, evaluates a particular action in a particular state:

Qπ(s,a) = Eπ[Gt | st = s, at = a]

The difference is important. V(s) evaluates the state, whereas Q(s,a) evaluates what happens when a specific action is selected in that state and the policy is followed afterwards.

6. The Bellman equation: connecting now to later

The central idea behind many reinforcement learning algorithms is that long-term value can be expressed using immediate reward and the value of the next state.

Vπ(s) = Eπ[r + γVπ(s')]

This is the Bellman expectation equation. The reward r represents the immediate outcome, and V(s') represents the future value. The expectation accounts for uncertainty in actions and state transitions.

The corresponding optimal action-value equation is:

Q*(s,a) = E[r + γ maxa' Q*(s',a')]

It states that the best long-term value of an action depends on its immediate reward and the best achievable future value. Algorithms such as Q-learning use this relationship to improve estimates of action quality.

7. Exploration versus exploitation

The agent faces a basic dilemma. Should it exploit what it has already learnt, or explore an unfamiliar action that might turn out to be better?

  • Exploitation: choose an action currently believed to be good.
  • Exploration: try actions to gain information about their outcomes.

An agent that only exploits may become stuck with a poor strategy. An agent that explores forever may never make sufficient use of what it has learnt.

A common method is epsilon-greedy exploration. With probability ε, the agent chooses a random action; otherwise, it chooses an action with the highest current Q-value. This is a classical exploration strategy, and it provides a useful starting point for understanding what quantum methods might change.

8. What exactly does the agent learn?

Different reinforcement learning methods learn different objects. Some learn a policy directly. Others learn a value function and derive actions from it. Actor-critic methods learn both.

Method What it learns How actions are chosen
Policy-based A mapping from states to action probabilities Sample from the learned policy
Value-based Values of states or state-action pairs Usually select the action with the highest estimated value
Actor-critic An actor policy and a critic value estimate The actor chooses; the critic helps evaluate decisions

Quantum reinforcement learning can use these same learning structures. The key change is that one or more components may be represented by a quantum circuit rather than only a classical neural network or table.

22.2 — Quantum policy and value function approximators

1. Why do we need function approximators?

Imagine a game with only ten possible states. We could maintain a table containing the best action for every state. But what if the environment contains millions of states, or the state consists of camera images, sensor readings and several continuous variables?

A table would become too large, and many states might never have been encountered during training. Instead, we use a function approximator to generalise from the states we have observed to states we have not encountered yet.

A classical neural network is one possible approximator. A parameterised quantum circuit is another candidate. In quantum reinforcement learning, the circuit may learn a policy, a value function, or a feature representation used by another model.

2. The anatomy of a quantum approximator

A parameterised quantum circuit, often abbreviated as PQC, contains quantum gates whose behaviour depends on adjustable parameters. The parameters are learnt using an optimisation procedure.

A typical quantum model has three stages:

  1. Encode the state. Convert the relevant classical state information into a quantum state using operations called quantum feature maps or state preparation.
  2. Process the encoded information. Apply parameterised gates that transform the quantum state. These gates may include rotations and entangling operations.
  3. Measure the circuit. Measure one or more observables and use the resulting statistics to estimate the model's output.

The circuit does not simply reveal its entire quantum state. Measurement produces classical outcomes. Repeating the circuit allows the system to estimate probabilities or expectation values.

|ψ(x, θ)⟩ = U(θ) |φ(x)⟩
f(x, θ) = ⟨ψ(x, θ)| O |ψ(x, θ)⟩

Here, x represents the encoded input, θ represents trainable parameters, U(θ) is the parameterised quantum circuit, and O is an observable that can be measured. The output f(x,θ) is an expectation value.

The expectation value is not generally obtained from a single measurement. It is estimated by running the circuit repeatedly. This repeated execution is called sampling or taking shots.

3. Quantum policy approximators

A policy tells an agent how likely it is to choose each action. A quantum policy approximator uses a quantum circuit to produce information from which these action probabilities are calculated.

Consider a simple robot with two available actions: move forward and turn left. The circuit may produce an expectation value z between -1 and 1. A classical output layer can convert this value into a probability:

p(forward | s) = (1 + z) / 2
p(left | s) = 1 - p(forward | s)

This is a simple illustrative mapping, not a universal rule for quantum policies. More complicated policies can use multiple measured outputs and a classical softmax layer.

The agent samples an action from the resulting distribution, performs the action in the environment, observes the reward and uses that experience to update the circuit parameters.

Worked intuition: Suppose the measured value is z = 0.6. The illustrative mapping gives a forward probability of 0.8 and a left-turn probability of 0.2. If the forward action repeatedly produces good outcomes in a particular state, training may adjust the circuit so that forward becomes more likely there.

4. Quantum value function approximators

A quantum value function approximator estimates the expected return associated with a state or state-action pair. It can be used in a value-based algorithm or as the critic in an actor-critic architecture.

For a state-value model, the circuit may return an estimate of V(s). For an action-value model, it may estimate Q(s,a) for a given state and action. Another design produces several outputs, one for each possible action.

Because a simple expectation value lies between -1 and 1, it cannot directly represent every possible return. The output can be scaled, shifted or passed through a classical layer when the task requires a different numerical range.

Important distinction: a quantum value approximator does not directly calculate the exact future reward merely because the circuit is quantum. It learns an estimate from experience, and the estimate can be inaccurate.

5. How do the parameters learn?

Suppose the model has parameters θ. The training algorithm defines a loss or objective that measures how well the model performs according to the selected reinforcement learning method. It then updates θ to improve that objective.

In policy-gradient learning, one common objective is to maximise expected return:

J(θ) = Etrajectories sampled from πθ[G]

The gradient indicates how a small change in the parameters is expected to change the objective. For a stochastic policy, a standard policy-gradient expression is:

∇θ J(θ) = E[ Gt ∇θ log πθ(at|st) ]

This expression gives the basic idea of REINFORCE-style learning. Practical methods often use baselines, advantage estimates and other variance-reduction techniques. The exact update depends on the algorithm.

A quantum circuit can be incorporated into this process if its output defines the policy. Its derivatives can sometimes be estimated using the parameter-shift rule, which evaluates a parameterised circuit at shifted parameter values. The classical optimiser then updates the parameters.

6. Hybrid quantum-classical architectures

Most practical near-term designs are hybrid. The classical computer manages the environment, stores transitions, calculates rewards and runs the optimiser. The quantum processor evaluates the parameterised circuit when needed.

Typical division of work
  • Classical computer: state preparation, replay buffers, reward processing and optimisation.
  • Quantum processor: circuit execution and measurement.
  • Classical computer: converts measurement results into action probabilities or value estimates.
  • Environment: supplies the next state and reward.

This arrangement is useful because reinforcement learning already involves substantial classical computation. There is no need to make every part of the system quantum. A quantum circuit is worth including only if its contribution is useful enough to justify its execution and integration costs.

7. When might a quantum approximator help?

A quantum model may be interesting when the representation of information or the structure of the decision problem connects naturally to quantum states and operations. Quantum feature maps can define distinctive similarity measures, and quantum circuits can implement functions that may be difficult to reproduce efficiently under certain computational assumptions.

Nevertheless, a circuit with many parameters is not automatically more expressive in a useful sense. A large model may be difficult to train, expensive to measure or less accurate than a small classical network.

The main question is not whether a quantum circuit can represent a policy. It can. The important question is whether the resulting policy learns more effectively, generalises better or solves the task more efficiently than a fair classical alternative.

22.3 — Quantum-enhanced exploration

1. Why exploration is difficult

An agent cannot know which action is best before it has collected enough information about the possible outcomes. Unfortunately, obtaining that information may be expensive or risky.

Imagine a robot searching a large building for an object. It has already discovered one room containing a small reward. A distant room may contain a much larger reward, but the robot does not know that yet. If it keeps returning to the familiar room, it may never discover the better option.

This problem becomes more difficult when the environment has a huge number of states, actions are expensive, and rewards are sparse. In a sparse-reward environment, an agent may receive little or no feedback until it completes a long sequence of decisions.

2. Quantum superposition is not the same as exploration

A quantum state can be a superposition of basis states. For example, a single qubit can be prepared as:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩
|α|² + |β|² = 1

When measured in the computational basis, the qubit produces outcome 0 with probability |α|² and outcome 1 with probability |β|².

This is a quantum probability distribution, but it does not mean the agent has tried both actions in the environment. A physical action still needs to be selected and executed. If a robot can either move left or right, preparing a superposition does not cause the robot to move in both directions.

A common misconception: quantum superposition does not automatically let a reinforcement learning agent explore every possible action at once and learn the reward of every outcome. Measuring a circuit produces an outcome, and the environment usually responds to the action that is actually taken.

3. Interference and the design of action distributions

Quantum amplitudes can interfere. Depending on the circuit, amplitudes associated with different computational paths may reinforce or cancel one another. This can change the probability distribution obtained when the circuit is measured.

A trainable quantum policy can use these effects to produce a structured distribution over actions. The learning algorithm can then adjust the circuit to increase the probability of useful actions and decrease the probability of poor ones.

However, interference alone does not identify the best action. The circuit still needs a learning signal, and its parameters must be trained using information about rewards or performance.

4. Quantum amplitude amplification

One of the most widely studied quantum techniques related to searching is amplitude amplification. Grover's search algorithm is a famous example.

Suppose a search problem has N possible candidates and a way to recognise a desired candidate. Classical unstructured search requires O(N) candidate checks in the worst case. Under the appropriate quantum oracle model, Grover-style search can find a marked candidate using O(√N) oracle queries, with suitable assumptions about the number of marked candidates.

The intuition is that a sequence of quantum operations increases the amplitudes of marked states and decreases those of unmarked states. Measurement then has a higher probability of returning a marked state.

In reinforcement learning, similar ideas may be relevant when the problem can be formulated as searching for a desirable action, trajectory or solution and the required oracle is available efficiently.

But a quantum oracle is not free. Constructing it may require expensive calculations or access to information that is difficult to load into a quantum processor. The theoretical query advantage does not automatically become a wall-clock speed advantage.

5. Quantum methods for multi-armed bandits

A multi-armed bandit is a simplified decision problem in which the agent repeatedly chooses among actions, often called arms. Each arm produces a reward drawn from an unknown distribution. The agent must balance trying uncertain arms against selecting arms that already appear profitable.

Bandit problems are useful for studying exploration because they remove much of the complexity of state transitions. They also allow researchers to examine whether quantum procedures can reduce the number of queries needed to identify a good action under particular assumptions.

Quantum algorithms may provide advantages for some structured search or estimation tasks associated with bandits. Such advantages depend on the access model, reward assumptions, oracle cost and exact learning objective. There is no general result stating that a quantum bandit algorithm always learns faster than its classical counterpart.

6. Quantum-enhanced sampling and uncertainty

Sampling is a fundamental part of both reinforcement learning and quantum computing. A quantum circuit produces measurement outcomes according to its probability distribution. Repeated executions estimate the distribution or the expectation values required by the learning algorithm.

In principle, researchers can investigate quantum circuits for representing complex distributions or quantum algorithms for estimating certain quantities. Such methods might be useful when uncertainty estimation is an important part of a decision problem.

However, the statistical uncertainty of measurement results matters. For independent shots of a bounded observable, the standard error of a sample mean generally decreases in proportion to 1/√M, where M is the number of shots. Reducing the estimation error by a factor of ten can therefore require about one hundred times as many shots under the same conditions.

Standard error ∝ 1 / √M

This creates a practical trade-off. A quantum circuit might represent a useful distribution, but measuring it accurately enough for training can require many executions.

7. Quantum exploration compared with classical exploration

Approach Main idea Important limitation
Epsilon-greedy Randomly try an action sometimes Random choices may waste many attempts
Upper confidence bound Account for both estimated reward and uncertainty Requires suitable uncertainty estimates
Thompson sampling Sample a plausible model or reward parameter Depends on the statistical model and its assumptions
Quantum amplitude amplification Increase the measurement probability of marked candidates Needs an appropriate oracle and coherent quantum operations
Quantum policy circuit Learn a probability distribution over actions Training and measurement may be costly
Quantum-enhanced exploration is best understood as a collection of possible techniques for search, sampling and decision-making. It is not a special ability that removes the need for experience, rewards or careful experimental evaluation.

22.4 — Variational quantum reinforcement learning

1. What does variational mean?

A variational method uses a model with adjustable parameters. It changes those parameters repeatedly to improve an objective. In a variational quantum circuit, the adjustable parameters control quantum gates.

This approach is attractive for near-term quantum processors because it does not always require the long, highly specialised circuits used by some fault-tolerant quantum algorithms. Nevertheless, today's quantum hardware can still suffer from noise, limited circuit depth and measurement costs.

A variational quantum reinforcement learning system places a parameterised quantum model inside a reinforcement learning loop. The environment supplies experience, the circuit produces policy or value estimates, and an optimiser adjusts the circuit.

2. The complete learning loop

  1. Initialise the circuit. Choose the circuit structure and starting values for its parameters.
  2. Observe the state. Receive the current state or observation from the environment.
  3. Encode the state. Prepare a quantum state that represents the input information.
  4. Run the circuit. Apply the trainable gates and measure the relevant observables.
  5. Select an action. Convert the circuit output into action probabilities or action-value estimates.
  6. Collect experience. Execute the action and observe the reward and next state.
  7. Update the parameters. Use the selected learning algorithm to calculate an update and improve the policy or value estimate.
  8. Repeat and evaluate. Continue learning, then test the trained agent on separate episodes or evaluation scenarios.

This loop is similar to ordinary reinforcement learning. The quantum-specific work lies mainly in encoding information, executing the circuit, measuring it and calculating gradients or other update quantities.

3. A simple mathematical model

Let the quantum policy be written as πθ(a|s), where θ contains the trainable circuit parameters. The objective is to maximise expected return:

J(θ) = Eπθ[G]

The optimisation process seeks parameter values that increase J(θ). A gradient-based method updates the parameters according to:

θnew = θold + η ∇θJ(θ)

Here, η is the learning rate. It controls the size of the update. This equation illustrates gradient ascent for maximising expected return. Other algorithms may minimise a loss instead, or use trust regions, adaptive optimisers or natural-gradient methods.

The gradient may be estimated from sampled trajectories and quantum circuit measurements. Since the gradient itself is estimated, it can be noisy. An excessively large learning rate may destabilise training, while a very small one may make learning painfully slow.

4. The parameter-shift rule

Quantum circuits require a method for estimating how their outputs change when a gate parameter changes. For gates with the appropriate generator and spectrum, the parameter-shift rule expresses a derivative using circuit evaluations at shifted parameter values.

A common form for a gate generated by a Pauli operator with the usual half-angle convention is:

∂f(θ)/∂θ = [f(θ + π/2) - f(θ - π/2)] / 2

The exact shift and prefactor depend on the gate parameterisation and generator spectrum. The expression above is not a universal derivative rule for every possible circuit parameter.

The main intuition is simple: instead of relying only on a numerical finite difference, we can estimate certain quantum circuit derivatives using carefully chosen shifted circuits. Each shifted evaluation still requires measurements, so calculating gradients can be expensive.

5. Policy-gradient training with a quantum circuit

A policy-gradient algorithm increases the probability of actions that contribute to better returns, while reducing the probability of actions that perform poorly relative to the learning signal.

If a quantum circuit produces the action distribution, its parameters can be trained using policy-gradient estimates. The procedure is not fundamentally different from a classical policy-gradient algorithm at the level of the objective; the circuit changes how the policy is represented and evaluated.

Training can become difficult when the circuit output changes only slightly with its parameters. This may lead to weak or noisy gradients. Barren plateaus, measurement uncertainty and hardware noise are therefore relevant concerns in variational quantum reinforcement learning.

6. Quantum actor-critic systems

Actor-critic learning separates action selection from value estimation. The actor chooses actions, while the critic estimates how useful states or actions are. The critic provides a learning signal that can reduce the variance of policy updates.

A hybrid actor-critic system may use a quantum circuit for the actor and a classical neural network for the critic. The reverse arrangement is also possible. Researchers can even use quantum circuits for both, although doing so increases the number of quantum computations required.

One common critic-based quantity is the temporal-difference error:

δt = rt+1 + γV(st+1) - V(st)

The error compares the current value estimate with a target based on the immediate reward and the next state's estimated value. It helps the critic learn. In many actor-critic methods, a related estimate of the advantage is used to guide the actor.

7. An illustrative example: a quantum-controlled maze agent

Consider a small maze with four possible actions: up, down, left and right. The agent receives a positive reward for reaching the destination and a penalty for hitting a wall.

  1. The current location is represented by a compact state vector.
  2. The state vector is encoded into a small quantum circuit.
  3. The circuit produces measured values that are converted into four action probabilities.
  4. The agent samples an action and moves through the maze.
  5. The environment returns the reward and the new location.
  6. The training algorithm updates the policy parameters.

Early in training, the agent may choose poor actions. After many episodes, the policy may learn to favour routes that reach the destination while avoiding walls.

This example demonstrates the architecture, not a proven quantum advantage. A classical neural network or even a simple tabular method could solve a small maze much more easily. A meaningful experiment would need a problem where the quantum representation has a justified role.

8. What should be measured during training?

Reward alone is not enough to determine whether a quantum agent is useful. Researchers should track average return, success rate, training stability, the number of environment interactions, quantum circuit executions, wall-clock training time and generalisation to unseen conditions.

The evaluation should also compare the quantum system against well-tuned classical baselines. The models should receive comparable training budgets and access to the same environment information. Otherwise, an apparent advantage may come from experimental differences rather than the quantum circuit.

22.5 — Applications in control and decision-making

1. What is a control problem?

A control system repeatedly observes a physical process and chooses actions to keep it operating as desired. Examples include balancing a robot, regulating temperature, steering a vehicle or controlling the speed of a motor.

Traditional control methods often rely on mathematical models of the system. Reinforcement learning can instead learn a policy from interaction, which is particularly interesting when the dynamics are difficult to model accurately.

Quantum reinforcement learning asks whether quantum models or quantum algorithms can help with some part of this learning and control process.

2. Robotics and motion control

A robot may need to choose movements based on sensor readings, its current position and the location of obstacles. A learned policy maps these observations to motor commands.

A quantum policy approximator could be investigated for a reduced observation space or a small discrete action space. The quantum circuit would output action probabilities or values, while the robot's conventional controller would execute the selected command.

The main difficulty is the gap between a small experimental circuit and a real robot with high-dimensional sensor inputs, continuous motor commands and strict safety requirements. State encoding and repeated circuit execution may make a direct implementation impractical.

A robot cannot wait indefinitely for a quantum circuit to finish. Real-time control imposes strict latency and reliability requirements. In many applications, classical controllers remain the more practical choice.

3. Industrial process control

Factories contain processes that must balance productivity, energy consumption, equipment wear and product quality. A controller may adjust temperature, pressure, flow rate or machine settings to maintain the desired operating point.

Reinforcement learning can be used to optimise control policies when an environment or simulator can provide feedback. Quantum models might be studied as policy or value approximators for simplified process-control tasks.

Before deploying a learned controller, engineers must test it under disturbances, unexpected states and sensor failures. Safety constraints may need to be enforced separately from the learned reward objective. A reward function alone is not a reliable safety guarantee.

4. Energy management and smart grids

An energy management system may decide when to charge batteries, use stored energy or draw power from the grid. Its decisions depend on demand, electricity prices, renewable generation and storage limits.

Reinforcement learning can model such decisions as a sequence of actions with long-term consequences. A good policy must balance immediate cost against future demand and battery availability.

Quantum reinforcement learning could be investigated for particular optimisation or policy-learning components. However, the full system also needs forecasts, physical constraints, market data and dependable control logic. Quantum hardware does not remove those requirements.

5. Scheduling and resource allocation

Many decision problems involve allocating limited resources over time. A computing cluster, for example, must decide where to run jobs while considering capacity, deadlines, energy usage and service requirements.

Such problems may be represented as sequential decision-making tasks. An agent can receive rewards for completing jobs on time and penalties for violating constraints.

Quantum reinforcement learning may be explored when a policy representation or a related quantum optimisation subroutine offers a plausible advantage. Still, it is important not to confuse quantum optimisation with quantum reinforcement learning: an optimisation algorithm can solve a static decision problem without learning a policy through repeated environmental interaction.

6. Network and computing-system management

A network controller may decide how to route traffic, allocate bandwidth or respond to changing congestion. A computing platform may choose how to allocate machines or schedule workloads as demand changes.

These environments contain delayed rewards and changing conditions. An action that improves performance now may increase congestion later. Reinforcement learning is suitable for studying these long-term trade-offs.

Quantum models might be evaluated for a carefully selected policy or subproblem, particularly in controlled simulations. Before using them in a production backend or network, engineers would need to establish that circuit latency, reliability and total cost meet operational requirements.

7. Financial decision-making

Financial decision-making can also be expressed as a sequential problem. An agent may allocate a portfolio, manage inventory risk or choose among actions based on changing market conditions. Rewards might depend on returns, transaction costs and risk.

Researchers may explore quantum policies or quantum optimisation components for simplified financial environments. However, financial data are noisy, markets change over time, and a simulated advantage may disappear when transaction costs and real-world constraints are included.

A high reward in historical data is not proof that a strategy will perform well in the future. Evaluation must account for uncertainty, risk and the possibility of overfitting.

9. What would a convincing application look like?

A convincing quantum reinforcement learning experiment starts with a clearly defined decision problem, not with the assumption that a quantum circuit must be useful.

Researchers first establish a reliable classical baseline, specify the state, action and reward definitions, and decide what resource they want to reduce. It could be the number of environment interactions, the time required to learn a useful policy, or the cost of a particular computation.

They then implement a quantum model and compare the results under fair conditions. Repeated runs are needed because reinforcement learning is stochastic and quantum measurements introduce additional statistical variation.

The most valuable outcome may be a clear understanding of when quantum methods help and when classical methods remain better. A scientifically useful result does not need to demonstrate quantum superiority in every setting.

Limitations and practical challenges

Quantum reinforcement learning is a research area, and many proposed architectures are still being evaluated. Several difficulties must be understood before expecting a practical advantage.

1. Data encoding

Reinforcement learning often receives classical information, such as sensor readings or numerical state vectors. Preparing a quantum state from that information requires gates and execution time. Loading a large classical input can remove an advantage that appears to exist at the algorithmic level.

2. Measurement cost

Quantum outputs are obtained statistically. A training step may require many circuit executions to estimate the policy, value function or gradient with sufficient accuracy.

3. Hardware noise

Current quantum devices are imperfect. Gate errors, readout errors and limited coherence can distort the circuit outputs. These errors may make policy learning unstable or prevent the model from representing the intended function accurately.

4. Barren plateaus and difficult optimisation

Some parameterised quantum circuits have regions where the gradient becomes extremely small as the circuit or system grows. Such regions are called barren plateaus. When gradients are too small relative to measurement noise, the optimiser may struggle to identify a useful direction for improvement.

The problem depends on the circuit, cost function, initialisation and other design choices. It is not correct to say that every quantum circuit suffers from barren plateaus, but trainability must be checked.

5. Delayed rewards and unstable learning

Reinforcement learning can be difficult even with classical models. Rewards may arrive late, useful actions may be rare, and the environment may change as the policy improves. Measurement noise and imperfect quantum gradients can add further difficulty.

6. A fair classical comparison

A small quantum model should not be compared only with an unnecessarily weak classical model. Researchers should compare it with suitable classical methods, use reasonable hyperparameter tuning and report the complete computational cost.

7. The end-to-end advantage test

Suppose a quantum circuit requires a fraction of the evaluations needed by a classical method, but every circuit evaluation is much slower. The quantum method may still take longer overall.

A practical comparison should therefore include data loading, circuit execution, measurement repetitions, optimisation, communication overhead and environment interaction.

Total training cost = data preparation + circuit execution + measurements + optimisation + environment interaction

This is a conceptual accounting identity, not a universal complexity formula. Each term depends on the implementation. The important point is to measure the complete workflow rather than one isolated component.

Chapter summary

Quantum reinforcement learning joins two ideas. Reinforcement learning provides the framework for learning from actions and rewards. Quantum computing provides a different way to represent and process information through quantum states, gates and measurements.

A quantum policy approximator uses a quantum circuit to help determine which action should be selected. A quantum value approximator estimates the expected usefulness of a state or action. Quantum-enhanced exploration investigates whether quantum techniques can improve search, sampling or particular forms of decision-making. Variational quantum reinforcement learning trains parameterised circuits using feedback from the environment.

These methods can be studied in control, robotics, scheduling, energy management and other sequential decision problems. However, their practical value depends on the problem, the hardware, the training procedure and a fair comparison with classical approaches.

The central lesson: a quantum agent still needs to learn from experience. Quantum mechanics changes the tools available to build the agent; it does not remove the need for a good reward function, effective learning, reliable evaluation or evidence of an advantage.

Further reading

  • Richard S. Sutton and Andrew G. Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction. A foundational textbook for policies, value functions, Bellman equations and learning from interaction.
  • John Preskill, Quantum Computing in the NISQ era and beyond (2018). A useful discussion of near-term quantum hardware, noise and the challenges facing practical quantum algorithms.
  • Maria Schuld and Francesco Petruccione, Machine Learning with Quantum Computers. A broad introduction to quantum machine learning methods and their mathematical foundations.
  • Jerbi and colleagues, research on quantum policy-gradient methods and quantum reinforcement learning. Useful for understanding how variational circuits can be integrated with classical policy optimisation.
  • Research literature on quantum-enhanced reinforcement learning, quantum bandits and amplitude amplification. These areas should be studied with attention to the assumptions behind claimed query or computational advantages.

Note: The equations and examples in this chapter introduce the main ideas. Specific algorithms can use different objectives, circuit architectures and gradient estimators. Quantum reinforcement learning remains an active research area, so proposed advantages should be checked against the latest evidence and suitable classical baselines.
