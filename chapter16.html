Quantum Computing and Quantum Machine Learning

Chapter 16: Encoding Classical Data into Quantum States

How do we teach a quantum computer about an ordinary photograph, a collection of numbers, or a customer's shopping history? The answer begins with encoding: the process of representing classical information using quantum states.

Introduction: Teaching a Quantum Computer About Data

Before a quantum computer can learn from data, it must first receive that data in a form that its hardware can process. This sounds simple, but it is one of the most important design decisions in quantum machine learning.

Consider a normal machine learning model that predicts the price of a house. Its input might contain the area of the house, the number of bedrooms, its age, and its distance from the city centre. These are ordinary classical numbers. A classical computer stores and processes them using bits and mathematical operations.

A quantum computer, however, operates on quantum states. Its basic units of information are qubits, and the operations it performs are quantum gates. We therefore need a method to transform the classical input into a quantum state before the quantum circuit can process it.

Classical Data
→
Encoding Circuit
→
Quantum State
→
Quantum Processing

The general journey from ordinary data to quantum computation.

The encoding circuit is not merely a device for copying numbers into qubits. Different encoding methods produce different quantum states, require different amounts of hardware, and influence which patterns a quantum learning model can represent. Consequently, the choice of encoding affects both computational cost and learning capability.

The central idea of this chapter: Classical data does not automatically become useful quantum information just because it is placed inside a quantum circuit. We must choose a representation that preserves the information we need and allows the circuit to use it effectively.

16.1 Why Data Encoding Matters

16.1.1 Classical data and quantum states are different things

Let us begin with a simple dataset:

x = [0.2, 0.7, 0.4]

These three numbers could represent three normalised features of a person, a product, or an image. A classical computer can store each number in its ordinary memory. A quantum computer cannot directly apply a quantum gate to the classical number 0.7. Instead, a preparation procedure must use that number to determine the state of one or more qubits.

A single qubit has two computational basis states, written |0⟩ and |1⟩. Its general pure state is represented as:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

|α|² + |β|² = 1

The numbers α and β are called probability amplitudes. They can be complex numbers, and their squared magnitudes determine the probabilities of the two computational-basis measurement outcomes.

A data-encoding circuit uses classical values to determine which quantum gates to apply or how those gates are configured. The resulting quantum state then carries information about the input.

16.1.2 Encoding is a representation choice

Imagine that we want to represent the number 0.7 in a quantum circuit. We could encode it in several ways:

  • Use the binary digits of a fixed-precision representation.
  • Use the number to determine a qubit's rotation angle.
  • Include it in the amplitude of a larger quantum state.
  • Use it in a more complicated transformation involving interactions between qubits.

All these approaches can represent information about the same classical number, but they create different quantum states. They also have different preparation costs and different implications for the subsequent quantum computation.

16.1.3 The number of qubits is not the whole story

A common attraction of quantum encoding is that a quantum state can involve a very large number of amplitudes. With n qubits, a pure state can be expressed as a superposition of 2ⁿ computational basis states.

|ψ⟩ = Σ aᵢ|i⟩

i = 0, 1, ..., 2ⁿ − 1

This expression describes the general state of n qubits, with amplitudes satisfying the normalisation condition Σ|aᵢ|² = 1.

The large number of amplitudes does not mean that we can freely write down, load, or read all 2ⁿ values at negligible cost. Preparing a particular state may require a circuit whose gate count grows rapidly with the number of amplitudes. Measurement also does not reveal the entire state in one shot.

Important distinction: A quantum state may mathematically contain many amplitudes, but this does not automatically give a quantum algorithm free access to an equally large classical dataset. State preparation, circuit execution, measurement, and classical post-processing must all be included when evaluating the cost.

16.1.4 The encoding must suit the problem

Suppose we want to classify handwritten digits. If each input contains thousands of pixel values, a method using one qubit per feature may be expensive. An amplitude-based representation may use fewer qubits, but preparing the required state can be difficult.

If the input contains only a few numerical features, rotation encoding may be much simpler. If the task depends on complex relationships between features, an encoding that introduces nonlinear feature combinations or interactions between qubits may be more useful.

There is therefore no universally best encoding. The choice depends on the dataset, the quantum hardware, the available operations, and the learning task.

Encoding matters because it determines how classical information enters the quantum system, how much preparation work is required, and which relationships the quantum model can potentially learn.

16.2 Basis Encoding

16.2.1 The basic idea

Basis encoding represents classical binary information using computational basis states. It is one of the most direct ways to connect ordinary bits with qubits.

A classical bit has a value of either 0 or 1. We can represent these values using the corresponding qubit basis states:

0 → |0⟩
1 → |1⟩

To encode the three-bit string 101, we prepare three qubits in the state:

|101⟩ = |1⟩ ⊗ |0⟩ ⊗ |1⟩

The symbol ⊗ denotes the tensor product. It combines the individual qubit states into one joint state. In this example, each qubit represents one classical bit.

16.2.2 Preparing a basis state

A quantum circuit usually begins with qubits initialised to |0⟩. The Pauli-X gate changes a qubit from |0⟩ to |1⟩, and from |1⟩ to |0⟩.

X|0⟩ = |1⟩
X|1⟩ = |0⟩

To prepare |101⟩ from |000⟩, we apply an X gate to the first and third qubits, leaving the middle qubit unchanged.

Initial: |000⟩
→
X on qubits 1 and 3
→
Final: |101⟩

A simple circuit prepares a computational basis state using bit flips.

For an n-bit input, this basic procedure requires at most n qubits and at most n bit-flip gates when the initial state is |0⟩ⁿ. This assumes that the classical bits are already available and that we are encoding a single binary string.

16.2.3 Encoding integers

Basis encoding can also represent an integer by first writing it in binary. For example, the decimal number 5 is represented by the three-bit binary string 101.

5 in decimal = 101 in binary
5 → |101⟩

Three qubits are enough to represent any integer from 0 to 7 using three-bit binary encoding. More generally, n qubits can represent 2ⁿ distinct binary strings, corresponding to integers from 0 to 2ⁿ − 1.

Notice that the qubits in this example do not store the integer in the same way as a classical register physically stores its bits. Rather, the computational basis state labels the corresponding bit string. When measured in the computational basis, an ideally prepared state returns that bit string.

16.2.4 Can basis encoding represent real-valued data?

Yes, but real-valued data must first be converted into a finite-precision representation, such as a fixed-point or floating-point binary format. The resulting bits can then be basis-encoded.

For example, if we choose to represent a number using a fixed-point scheme with a specified scale, the encoding circuit receives the corresponding bit string. The choice of scale, number of bits, and rounding rule determines the precision of the representation.

This conversion introduces a design decision before quantum computation even begins. A representation that uses too few bits may lose useful information, while a representation that uses many bits requires more qubits.

16.2.5 Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • Conceptually simple.
  • Directly represents binary strings.
  • Can be efficient for discrete data.
  • Computational-basis measurements recover the encoded string reliably in the ideal case.

Limitations

  • Requires one qubit per encoded bit.
  • Numerical precision can require many qubits.
  • Binary representation may hide useful numerical relationships.
  • It does not automatically create a useful superposition of inputs.

Basis encoding is useful when the information is naturally discrete or binary. However, if a dataset contains many real-valued features, other approaches may represent it more compactly in terms of qubit count.

16.3 Amplitude Encoding

16.3.1 Using amplitudes to represent data

Basis encoding uses qubits to represent individual bits. Amplitude encoding takes a different approach: it places classical data into the probability amplitudes of a quantum state.

Consider a classical vector containing four values:

x = [x₀, x₁, x₂, x₃]

First, we normalise the vector so that the sum of the squares of its entries is one. If the vector is non-zero, define:

||x|| = √(x₀² + x₁² + x₂² + x₃²)

aᵢ = xᵢ / ||x||

We can then represent the normalised values as amplitudes of a two-qubit state:

|ψ⟩ = a₀|00⟩ + a₁|01⟩ + a₂|10⟩ + a₃|11⟩

The amplitudes satisfy:

|a₀|² + |a₁|² + |a₂|² + |a₃|² = 1

Two qubits provide four computational basis states. In this way, a vector with four components can be represented using two qubits, provided the required state can be prepared.

16.3.2 A numerical example

Consider the classical vector:

x = [1, 2, 2, 1]

Its Euclidean norm is:

||x|| = √(1² + 2² + 2² + 1²)
||x|| = √10

Dividing each component by √10 gives the normalised vector:

a = [1/√10, 2/√10, 2/√10, 1/√10]

The corresponding two-qubit state is:

|ψ⟩ = (1/√10)|00⟩
+ (2/√10)|01⟩
+ (2/√10)|10⟩
+ (1/√10)|11⟩

This is a valid quantum state because the sum of the squared amplitudes equals one:

1/10 + 4/10 + 4/10 + 1/10 = 1

If we measure this state in the computational basis, the probabilities of obtaining 00, 01, 10, and 11 are respectively 1/10, 4/10, 4/10, and 1/10.

Do not confuse amplitudes with probabilities. In this example, the amplitude associated with |01⟩ is 2/√10, but the probability of observing 01 is its squared magnitude, which is 4/10. The measurement outcome is probabilistic, even though the quantum state itself is precisely specified.

16.3.3 Why amplitude encoding looks attractive

A state of n qubits has 2ⁿ computational basis amplitudes. Consequently, amplitude encoding can represent a vector of dimension 2ⁿ using n qubits.

Number of amplitudes = 2ⁿ
Qubits needed for a vector of dimension N:
n = ⌈log₂ N⌉

If the vector dimension is not a power of two, we can pad the vector with zeroes to reach the next power of two. For example, a five-component vector can be embedded into an eight-component vector and represented using three qubits.

This is a compact representation in terms of qubit count. It does not mean that preparing an arbitrary amplitude-encoded state is equally cheap.

16.3.4 The hidden cost: state preparation

Suppose a classical dataset contains one million unrelated numerical values. Amplitude encoding may describe the normalised vector using about twenty qubits because 2²⁰ is slightly above one million.

However, a general quantum state with one million amplitudes may require a large number of quantum gates to prepare. If the original million values exist in classical memory, the preparation circuit must somehow make the appropriate amplitudes appear in the quantum system.

For a general dense vector with no special structure, standard exact state-preparation procedures can require a number of operations that grows linearly with the vector dimension, up to details of the gate set and preparation method. Specially structured vectors may be much cheaper to prepare.

A key lesson: Reducing the number of qubits does not necessarily reduce the total computational cost. The cost of loading the data may dominate the later quantum computation.

16.3.5 What about negative and complex values?

Quantum amplitudes may be negative or complex. This allows amplitude encoding to represent more than just non-negative probability weights.

For real-valued input vectors, negative components can be represented as negative amplitudes. Their signs affect relative phases and can influence later interference. For complex-valued vectors, both magnitudes and phases can be represented in the amplitudes.

Nevertheless, measurement probabilities depend on squared magnitudes. A direct measurement of the computational basis does not reveal the signs or phases of all amplitudes. Those features must be accessed through suitable quantum operations and measurements, often with repeated experiments.

16.3.6 When is amplitude encoding useful?

Amplitude encoding is especially interesting when an algorithm operates directly on quantum states and the input can be prepared efficiently. It appears in discussions of quantum linear algebra, quantum state processing, and algorithms that assume access to quantum data.

It is less automatically attractive when a large classical dataset must be loaded afresh for every experiment. The preparation cost, data-access method, and number of circuit repetitions must be considered before claiming an advantage.

16.4 Angle (Rotation) Encoding

16.4.1 Representing numbers through rotations

Angle encoding is one of the most intuitive approaches for numerical inputs. Instead of representing a number as a collection of binary digits or as an amplitude in a large state, we use the number to control the angle of a quantum gate.

A common choice is a rotation around the Y axis of the Bloch sphere. This gate is written as Rᵧ(θ), where θ is the rotation angle.

Rᵧ(θ) =
[ cos(θ/2) −sin(θ/2) ]
[ sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2) ]

Applying this gate to the initial state |0⟩ gives:

Rᵧ(θ)|0⟩ =
cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + sin(θ/2)|1⟩

The classical value determines the angle θ. The rotation transforms the qubit into a superposition whose amplitudes depend on that value.

16.4.2 A simple example

Suppose we have one classical feature x and encode it by choosing θ = x. The encoding operation is:

|ψ(x)⟩ = Rᵧ(x)|0⟩
= cos(x/2)|0⟩ + sin(x/2)|1⟩

If x = 0, the rotation does nothing and the state remains |0⟩. If x = π, the state becomes |1⟩, up to the sign of the resulting amplitude. Between these values, the qubit moves continuously between the two basis states.

The probability of measuring the outcome 1 is:

P(1|x) = sin²(x/2)

This equation shows how the classical input affects the measurement statistics of the quantum state.

Angles must be defined consistently. Rotation gates usually use radians in mathematical definitions and in many programming interfaces. If a feature is originally measured in metres, rupees, or years, it should normally be scaled or transformed before being used as an angle.

16.4.3 Encoding several features

Suppose a data point contains three features:

x = [x₁, x₂, x₃]

A straightforward approach is to use three qubits and apply one rotation to each:

|ψ(x)⟩ = Rᵧ(x₁)|0⟩ ⊗ Rᵧ(x₂)|0⟩ ⊗ Rᵧ(x₃)|0⟩

Equivalently, the encoding circuit applies Rᵧ(x₁) to the first qubit, Rᵧ(x₂) to the second, and Rᵧ(x₃) to the third. The result is a product state before any entangling gates are introduced.

x₁ → Rᵧ(x₁)
x₂ → Rᵧ(x₂)
x₃ → Rᵧ(x₃)

Each numerical feature controls a rotation on a qubit.

In this simple arrangement, the number of qubits grows with the number of features. The encoding uses a small number of gates per feature, assuming that the necessary single-qubit rotations are directly available.

16.4.4 Choosing a scaling rule

Real-world data does not always lie in a convenient angular range. A feature such as a person's age might lie between 0 and 100, while an image pixel might lie between 0 and 255. Feeding these raw values directly into a rotation can create many repeated or rapidly changing states because rotation angles are periodic.

A common strategy is to normalise each feature and map it to a chosen interval. For example, if a feature has been scaled to the interval [0, 1], one may define:

θ = πx

The encoding then uses Rᵧ(πx). This is only one possible choice. Another task may benefit from a different range, an offset, or a data-dependent transformation.

Scaling is not a trivial preprocessing detail. It influences how sensitive the encoded quantum state is to changes in the input. A poor scaling rule can make distinct data points produce very similar states or can create undesirable periodic behaviour.

16.4.5 Other rotation gates

Angle encoding does not have to use only Rᵧ rotations. The Pauli-X, Pauli-Y, and Pauli-Z axes define three common rotation gates:

Rₓ(θ) = exp(−iθX/2)
Rᵧ(θ) = exp(−iθY/2)
R𝓏(θ) = exp(−iθZ/2)

Here, X, Y, and Z are Pauli operators and i is the imaginary unit. These rotations change the quantum state in different ways. In particular, a Z-axis rotation applied to |0⟩ alone changes only a global phase, which cannot be observed. A Z rotation becomes useful for encoding relative phase when the state has appropriate superposition components.

16.4.6 Advantages and limitations

  • Compact and practical: each feature can often be encoded with a single rotation.
  • Natural for numerical inputs: continuous values directly determine gate parameters.
  • Hardware-friendly: single-qubit rotations are supported by many quantum platforms.
  • Potential limitation: one qubit per feature may still be costly for high-dimensional datasets.
  • Potential limitation: a simple product-state encoding does not initially create entanglement between features.
  • Potential limitation: periodic rotations can map different classical values to the same or similar quantum states.

Rotation encoding is widely used in parameterised quantum circuits because it gives a simple connection between classical features and quantum operations.

16.5 Higher-Order and IQP-Style Encodings

16.5.1 Why encode features together?

Imagine predicting whether a student will perform well in an examination. The number of hours studied may matter, and sleep duration may matter. But the relationship between studying and performance could also depend on sleep duration. Studying for ten hours after a good night's sleep may have a different effect from studying for ten hours after almost no sleep.

In machine learning, this is an example of an interaction between features. A model that only examines each feature independently may struggle to represent such relationships.

Quantum encoding can be designed to introduce interactions between input features. Instead of allowing each number to control only a separate single-qubit rotation, we can use products of features to determine operations involving multiple qubits.

16.5.2 Higher-order feature combinations

In classical machine learning, a feature map might transform two input features x₁ and x₂ into a richer representation:

φ(x) = [x₁, x₂, x₁², x₂², x₁x₂]

The term x₁x₂ is especially important because it represents an interaction between the two original features. The squared terms can represent curvature or nonlinear effects.

Quantum circuits can produce related effects through their input-dependent rotations and interactions. For example, a gate may have an angle proportional to x₁x₂ rather than only x₁ or x₂.

θ₁ = λx₁
θ₂ = λx₂
θ₁₂ = λx₁x₂

Here, λ is a scaling constant. The first two angles depend on individual features, while the third depends on their product.

This is called higher-order feature encoding when the encoding explicitly incorporates combinations of multiple input features. The exact meaning depends on the construction: higher-order terms may appear directly in rotation angles, in phases, or in the resulting measurement functions.

16.5.3 A two-qubit example

Suppose we begin with two qubits in the state |00⟩. We apply Hadamard gates to create an equal superposition:

H⊗H|00⟩ =
(|00⟩ + |01⟩ + |10⟩ + |11⟩)/2

We now apply an input-dependent controlled phase operation. One possible gate is a controlled-Z rotation, written schematically as:

U(x₁,x₂) = exp(iλx₁x₂ Z⊗Z)

In this expression, Z⊗Z acts jointly on the two qubits. The phase angle depends on the product x₁x₂. As the input values change, the relative phases of the basis components change.

Relative phases may be difficult to observe directly, but later gates can make their effects visible in measurement statistics. As a result, the output may depend on the interaction between x₁ and x₂.

The operation above is an illustrative higher-order phase encoding. It should not be confused with a claim that every two-qubit phase operation creates entanglement for every input. Whether the resulting state is entangled depends on the operation and the state to which it is applied.

16.5.4 What is IQP-style encoding?

IQP stands for Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial-time. IQP circuits are a family of quantum circuits with a particular structure, commonly involving commuting gates that are diagonal in a chosen basis, together with basis-changing operations such as Hadamard gates.

In quantum machine learning, an IQP-style feature map uses input-dependent phase operations to encode a classical data point into a quantum state. The phase operations may involve individual features and products of several features.

A simplified feature-map construction has the following form:

|φ(x)⟩ = Uφ(x)|+⟩ⁿ

Here, |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2, and Uφ(x) is an input-dependent unitary operation built from selected phase gates.

One possible illustrative form is:

Uφ(x) = exp(i Σ φᵢ(x)Zᵢ + i Σ φᵢⱼ(x)ZᵢZⱼ)

The operators Zᵢ act on individual qubits, while ZᵢZⱼ denotes an interaction between a pair of qubits. The functions φᵢ(x) and φᵢⱼ(x) determine how the input values influence these phases.

This expression describes one possible diagonal phase feature map, not every IQP circuit. In some implementations, Hadamard gates surround the diagonal operations; in others, the construction is described directly as a feature map applied to an initial superposition.

16.5.5 Connection to quantum kernels

One reason these feature maps are important is that they can be used to define quantum kernels. In classical machine learning, a kernel measures a notion of similarity between two inputs. A quantum feature map defines a quantum state for each input, and the overlap between those states can serve as a similarity measure.

K(x,z) = |⟨φ(x)|φ(z)⟩|²

This is the fidelity between the two pure states when they are represented by normalised state vectors. It lies between zero and one.

If two inputs produce identical states, the kernel value is one. If their states are orthogonal, the value is zero. Intermediate values describe partial overlap.

The feature map determines which aspects of the classical data influence this similarity. A richer map may capture more complicated relationships, but it can also make the circuit harder to execute and the resulting kernel harder to estimate accurately.

16.5.6 More complexity is not always better

Adding more feature interactions may increase the range of functions that a model can represent. However, a more complicated feature map may require more gates, more entangling operations, and more measurements.

On noisy hardware, deep circuits can lose useful information before the final measurement. A feature map that is mathematically expressive but impossible to execute reliably may be less useful than a simpler one.

The goal is not to add the largest possible number of interactions. The goal is to introduce the relationships that are useful for the task without making the circuit unnecessarily expensive.

16.6 Data Re-uploading

16.6.1 The idea of using the same data repeatedly

In a simple angle-encoding circuit, we may encode each input feature once and then apply trainable quantum gates. This creates a quantum state that depends on the input, but a shallow circuit may have limited ability to represent complicated functions of that input.

Data re-uploading takes a different approach. The same classical data is inserted into the circuit multiple times, with trainable operations placed between the encoding stages. Each stage allows the input to influence the state again.

Input x
→
Encode x
→
Trainable Gates
→
Encode x Again
→
Measurement

Data re-uploading repeatedly introduces the input between trainable quantum transformations.

16.6.2 A mathematical description

Let Uenc(x) be an encoding operation determined by the input x. Let Uθ₁ and Uθ₂ be trainable quantum operations whose parameters are represented by θ₁ and θ₂.

A two-stage re-uploading circuit can be written as:

|ψ(x,θ)⟩ = Uenc(x) Uθ₂ Uenc(x) Uθ₁ |0⟩ⁿ

This expression specifies the order of operations when read from right to left: first the initial trainable transformation is applied, followed by data encoding, another trainable transformation, and the final encoding.

Different conventions may place the trainable gates and encoding gates in different orders. The essential idea is that the input-dependent operation appears repeatedly throughout the circuit.

16.6.3 Why can repeated encoding help?

Consider the simpler case of a single qubit with rotations whose angles depend on x. The resulting measurement probabilities and expectation values are trigonometric functions of x.

A single rotation may produce a relatively simple dependence on x. When additional input-dependent rotations and trainable operations are composed, the resulting trigonometric expression can contain more combinations of frequencies and phases.

These additional combinations can allow the model to approximate a wider range of functions. In suitable constructions, increasing the number of re-uploading layers can improve the expressive power of the model.

However, repeated encoding does not guarantee that every target function becomes easy to learn. The available frequencies, circuit architecture, optimisation procedure, and measurement choices still matter.

16.6.4 A practical example

Suppose we want a quantum classifier to distinguish two groups of points on a graph. The boundary between the groups may be curved rather than a straight line.

A shallow circuit with a simple encoding might fail to represent the boundary accurately. Re-uploading the same coordinates through several trainable layers may introduce additional nonlinear relationships into the measured output.

The trainable parameters can then be adjusted using a classical optimisation algorithm. During training, the circuit predicts an output, a loss function evaluates the prediction, and the parameters are updated to improve the result.

The classical data is not changed during re-uploading. Instead, the same values influence the quantum state at multiple points in the circuit.

16.6.5 Expressivity versus trainability

A deeper circuit may represent more complicated functions, but deeper is not always better. Additional gates increase execution time and create more opportunities for noise to affect the state.

Some deep, highly parameterised circuits also suffer from barren plateaus, where gradients of the training objective become extremely small in certain regions of parameter space. Such gradients can make optimisation difficult. This behaviour depends on the circuit, cost function, initialisation, and problem structure; it is not an inevitable consequence of every deep circuit.

Data re-uploading is a way to make a small quantum circuit reuse the same input across several transformations. It can increase expressive power without requiring one new qubit for every additional encoding layer, although the circuit depth and gate count still grow.

16.7 QRAM and Its Practical Limitations

16.7.1 The data-loading problem

So far, we have discussed how classical values can be transformed into quantum states. This raises a bigger question: what happens when the dataset is enormous?

A real machine learning application might contain millions of records. Preparing an individual quantum state from each record could become expensive. We would like a mechanism that allows a quantum algorithm to access classical data efficiently.

Quantum Random Access Memory, commonly called QRAM, is a proposed architecture for accessing stored classical information using quantum-address inputs. It is intended to support certain kinds of data loading in quantum algorithms.

16.7.2 How ordinary random-access memory works

In a conventional computer, memory is organised into addressable locations. A program supplies an address, and the memory returns the data stored at that location.

For example, a memory containing eight records can use a three-bit address to select one of the eight locations. If the address is 101, the system returns the data stored at location 5 when counting from zero.

16.7.3 The quantum version

QRAM aims to extend this idea to quantum superpositions of addresses. Consider a simplified idealised operation:

Σ αᵢ|i⟩|0⟩
→
Σ αᵢ|i⟩|dᵢ⟩

Here, |i⟩ represents an address, |dᵢ⟩ represents the data associated with that address, and αᵢ is the amplitude of the address state. The summation runs over the addresses included in the input superposition.

The operation correlates each address with its corresponding data. If the address register is in a superposition, the idealised result is a superposition of address-data pairs. This is different from a classical memory operation that selects one definite address at a time.

The expression is an idealised model of quantum data access, not a complete engineering description of a practical memory device. Real implementations must account for how the stored information is physically represented, how the query is routed, and how errors are controlled.

16.7.4 Why is QRAM interesting?

Some quantum algorithms are described under the assumption that they can access data through an efficient quantum oracle or memory structure. QRAM is one proposed way to realise certain such access patterns.

In an ideal setting, an algorithm may exploit a coherent query that acts on a superposition of addresses. This can be important in theoretical algorithms for searching, sampling, linear algebra, and quantum machine learning.

The potential advantage, however, depends on what the algorithm does with the data after accessing it. Even if a coherent query is efficient, the algorithm may require many queries, complicated post-processing, or repeated measurements.

16.7.5 Why practical QRAM is difficult

QRAM is a theoretical and engineering challenge rather than a universal, readily available replacement for ordinary computer memory. Several issues must be solved.

1. Hardware size and routing

A memory containing many entries requires physical resources to store or encode those entries and route queries to the relevant locations. A design that appears compact in an abstract circuit diagram may involve a large physical structure.

2. Noise and errors

Quantum information is vulnerable to noise and unwanted interactions. A large memory-access structure may involve many components and operations, each of which can introduce errors. Preserving coherent information throughout the query is a major challenge.

3. Building and maintaining the memory

The memory must be loaded with data, and its stored information must remain usable. Loading the dataset, updating it, and maintaining the hardware may require substantial resources.

4. The cost of a query

An idealised algorithm may count one coherent memory query as a single operation. A practical device must implement that query using physical components and elementary operations. These costs can be very different.

5. Error correction and fault tolerance

A large-scale coherent memory may require reliable components, control systems, and potentially quantum error correction. These additional resources can be substantial, depending on the architecture.

16.7.6 QRAM does not remove the input bottleneck automatically

Suppose a classical dataset contains one million numbers. If the data begins in an ordinary database, a practical system still needs a way to transfer or represent those values in the proposed memory structure. The time and resources required to build and initialise that structure cannot simply be ignored.

If the same data is used repeatedly, preparation costs may be amortised across many queries. If the data changes frequently or is used for only one small computation, those costs may make the approach unattractive.

Furthermore, the quantum algorithm cannot simply print every item in a superposition as though it were a conventional database response. Measurement returns limited classical information, so the algorithm must be designed around the information it actually needs.

Practical rule: A quantum speedup based on efficient QRAM access must state its assumptions about data loading, memory construction, query cost, errors, and the number of times the memory is used. An efficient theoretical query alone does not establish an end-to-end speedup for a machine learning task.

16.8 Encoding as the Source of Expressivity

16.8.1 What does expressivity mean?

In machine learning, a model is expressive if it can represent a sufficiently wide range of useful relationships between inputs and outputs. A model that can represent only very simple relationships may perform poorly on a task requiring a complicated decision boundary.

Consider a classifier that receives two features, x₁ and x₂, and predicts either class A or class B. A simple model may separate the classes using a straight line. Another model may need a curved boundary or several disconnected regions.

The representation used by the model influences which boundaries it can express. Quantum machine learning is no different: the encoding and the subsequent quantum circuit together determine how the input affects the final prediction.

16.8.2 The quantum feature map

We can describe encoding as a mathematical function that maps classical inputs to quantum states:

x → |φ(x)⟩

The function that performs this transformation is called a quantum feature map. It converts an ordinary data point into a state in a quantum state space.

A trainable quantum circuit can then process the encoded state, and a measurement produces the quantity used by the learning model. A common form of a prediction is:

f(x;θ) = ⟨φ(x)|U†(θ) M U(θ)|φ(x)⟩

Here:

  • |φ(x)⟩ is the state created by encoding the input x.
  • U(θ) is a trainable quantum operation.
  • M is an observable, representing the quantity measured from the circuit.
  • U†(θ) is the adjoint of the unitary operation.
  • f(x;θ) is the expectation value used to form a prediction.

This equation is important because it separates the two main sources of modelling capability: the way data enters the quantum state and the way the trainable circuit transforms that state before measurement.

16.8.3 Encoding changes the function being learned

Imagine two quantum models with the same number of qubits and the same trainable circuit, but different encodings. One uses a simple rotation based on each individual feature. The other introduces input-dependent interactions between features.

Although the hardware structure may be similar, the two models can represent different families of functions. Their measurements depend on different mathematical combinations of the input values.

In this sense, encoding helps determine the feature space in which learning takes place. It is not merely a preliminary step that can be ignored after the circuit has been prepared.

16.8.4 Interference creates useful relationships

One distinctive property of quantum computation is interference. Quantum amplitudes can reinforce or cancel one another when paths through a circuit are combined. Input-dependent phases and rotations can change how this interference occurs.

For example, two inputs may produce states with similar measurement probabilities immediately after encoding, but their relative phases may differ. A subsequent quantum operation can transform those phase differences into different measurement probabilities.

This is one reason why the full quantum state matters more than a list of the probabilities obtained from measuring it immediately. The later circuit can use relative phase information through interference.

Important: Interference is a computational resource, not a guarantee of better prediction. The circuit must be designed so that useful distinctions in the input affect the final measurement in a way that the learning process can exploit.

16.8.5 Expressivity and quantum kernels

Recall the quantum kernel:

K(x,z) = |⟨φ(x)|φ(z)⟩|²

The feature map determines which pairs of inputs have high or low overlap. If the map transforms inputs in a way that reflects useful similarities for the task, the resulting kernel may help a classifier distinguish the relevant classes.

If the feature map does not preserve the relationships needed for the task, the kernel may be unhelpful. A more complicated feature map can also create problems: its overlaps may be difficult to estimate, or the resulting kernel may be unsuitable for the available training data.

Quantum kernels therefore illustrate a broader point: the choice of representation can matter as much as the choice of the algorithm that operates on the representation.

16.8.6 Expressivity versus trainability

It is tempting to assume that the most expressive circuit must be the best model. In practice, the ability to represent a function and the ability to learn that function are different things.

A highly expressive circuit may have many parameters, require substantial measurement resources, or produce gradients that are difficult to optimise. Noise can also make its theoretical behaviour hard to realise on physical hardware.

A simpler encoding may perform better if it preserves the important information and allows the model to train reliably. Classical machine learning has a similar principle: a model should be complex enough for the task, but unnecessary complexity can increase training difficulty and overfitting.

16.8.7 Expressivity versus generalisation

Generalisation means performing well on new data rather than merely memorising the training examples. An encoding that allows a model to fit very complicated patterns may also allow it to fit noise in a small dataset.

Consequently, more expressive quantum feature maps do not automatically generalise better. Their performance depends on the data distribution, training procedure, model architecture, regularisation, and the relationship between the chosen features and the task.

The objective is to find an encoding that makes the relevant patterns accessible without creating needless computational expense or making the model unnecessarily difficult to train.

Quantum machine learning depends on the entire pipeline, from the original data to the final classical prediction.

A quantum learning model should therefore be evaluated as a complete system. The number of qubits and the expressive power of the circuit are only part of the story. We must also examine the cost of preparing the input, the number of measurements required, the effects of noise, the difficulty of optimisation, and the model's performance on unseen data.

Encoding is one of the main sources of expressivity because it decides how classical information is represented in the quantum state. But useful expressivity is not simply about creating the most complicated state. It is about representing the right relationships efficiently and making them accessible to the learning process.

Comparing the Main Encoding Methods

We have now encountered several ways of converting classical information into quantum states. Each method makes a different trade-off between qubit count, preparation complexity, and the type of information represented.

Method How data is represented Typical qubit requirement Main advantage Main limitation
Basis encoding Binary values determine computational basis states. One qubit per encoded bit. Simple and natural for discrete data. Many bits may be needed for high-precision values.
Amplitude encoding Vector components determine quantum amplitudes. About log₂(N) qubits for N components. Compact representation of large vectors in terms of qubit count. General state preparation can be expensive.
Angle encoding Numerical values determine rotation angles. Often one qubit per feature in a simple design. Simple to implement with rotation gates. Feature scaling and circuit depth must be managed.
Higher-order encoding Individual features and combinations determine phases or gates. Depends on the feature map and its interactions. Can capture relationships between features. More complex interactions can increase gate cost.
IQP-style encoding Input-dependent diagonal phase operations encode the data. Depends on the chosen feature map. Useful for constructing quantum feature maps and kernels. Expressivity and useful advantage depend on the construction and hardware.
Data re-uploading The same input is encoded repeatedly throughout the circuit. Can reuse a fixed number of qubits across layers. Can increase expressive power with repeated input-dependent transformations. Greater circuit depth may increase noise and training difficulty.
QRAM-assisted access Quantum address states are correlated with stored data. Address registers scale logarithmically with the number of locations in the idealised model, but the memory requires additional physical resources. Potentially supports coherent access to large datasets. Practical construction, loading, routing, and error control are challenging.
The qubit counts in this table are not measures of total computational cost. A fair comparison must include state preparation, circuit depth, gate quality, measurement repetitions, classical preprocessing, and data access.

How to Choose an Encoding for a Real Problem

Suppose you are designing a quantum machine learning model for a practical dataset. Rather than selecting an encoding because it sounds advanced, ask a sequence of questions.

1. What kind of data do you have?

Binary labels, discrete categories, and numerical features have different representation needs. Basis encoding may suit binary information, while angle encoding is a natural starting point for small numerical feature vectors.

2. How large is the dataset?

A dataset with five features presents a different problem from an image with tens of thousands of pixel values. Consider how many qubits are required and whether the encoding procedure can be executed efficiently.

3. How often must the data be loaded?

If the same data is reused for many quantum computations, some preparation costs may be amortised. If a large state must be prepared from scratch for every training example, the overall cost can become significant.

4. Which relationships must the model learn?

If the task depends on interactions between features, consider whether an encoding with input-dependent phases, multiple encoding stages, or suitable entangling gates can represent those interactions.

5. Can the hardware execute the circuit reliably?

The number and type of gates matter. A deep circuit with many two-qubit gates may be difficult to execute on noisy hardware. A simpler feature map may offer a better practical balance.

6. How will you measure success?

Compare the quantum model against relevant classical baselines using the same data splits and a suitable evaluation procedure. Include data preparation and measurement costs where they are relevant to the proposed application. Do not treat a large state space or a small qubit count as proof of a computational advantage.

Chapter Summary

Encoding is the bridge between classical information and quantum computation. It determines how ordinary numbers, binary strings, and feature vectors become quantum states. Different encodings can lead to very different resource requirements and learning behaviour.

  • Basis encoding represents classical bits using computational basis states.
  • Amplitude encoding places normalised vector components into the amplitudes of a quantum state.
  • Angle encoding uses classical values to control quantum rotation angles.
  • Higher-order encoding introduces feature combinations that can represent interactions between input variables.
  • IQP-style encoding uses input-dependent phase operations and is relevant to quantum feature maps and kernels.
  • Data re-uploading introduces the same classical input multiple times between trainable transformations.
  • QRAM proposes coherent access to stored classical data, but practical construction and operation remain challenging.
  • Expressivity depends on the encoding, the trainable circuit, and the measurements used to produce predictions.
The most important lesson: A quantum computer does not gain a practical advantage merely because a large amount of information can be described by a small number of qubits. The information must be encoded efficiently, processed usefully, and extracted through measurements at a cost that makes sense for the problem.

5. Why probabilities are not enough

Squared magnitudes remove signs and complex phases. For example, the states (3/5)|0⟩ + (4/5)|1⟩ and (3/5)|0⟩ − (4/5)|1⟩ produce the same computational-basis measurement probabilities. Their relative phases can nevertheless affect the results of later quantum operations.

Looking Ahead

We now know how classical data can enter a quantum computer. But encoding alone does not create a complete learning algorithm. After preparing the state, we must decide which quantum operations to apply, which parameters to train, which measurements to perform, and how to use the results.

These questions lead naturally to parameterised quantum circuits and variational quantum algorithms, where quantum operations and classical optimisation work together to solve a learning task.

Remember this connection: Chapter 16 explains how data enters the quantum model. The next step is to understand how the model transforms that data, learns from examples, and produces a prediction.
