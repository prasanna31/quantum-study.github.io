Chapter 15 · Quantum Computing and AI

Where Quantum Meets Machine Learning

Machine learning helps computers discover patterns in data. Quantum computing offers a different way to represent and process information. Quantum machine learning brings these two ideas together, asking whether quantum systems can help us learn things that are difficult to learn using classical computers alone.

Introduction: Why Bring Quantum Computing and Machine Learning Together?

Imagine that you are teaching a computer to recognise handwritten digits. You give it thousands of examples, such as images of the numbers 0 to 9. The computer studies these examples, adjusts its internal parameters and gradually learns to predict which digit appears in a new image. This is a typical machine learning task.

Today, almost all practical machine learning runs on classical computers. These include CPUs, GPUs and specialised AI accelerators. They represent information using bits, which take the value 0 or 1. Modern classical systems are extremely powerful and can learn complicated patterns from enormous datasets.

Quantum computers process information using qubits. A qubit can occupy a quantum state that is a superposition of the basis states associated with 0 and 1. Qubits can also become entangled, creating correlations that cannot be described as ordinary independent classical bits.

Quantum machine learning, usually abbreviated as QML, studies how these quantum properties might be used in learning algorithms, data representations, model training and prediction.

The central question: Can a quantum computer help a learning system represent, discover or predict patterns more effectively than a suitable classical approach?

Notice the wording. The aim is not simply to put a quantum computer inside an AI system and expect it to become more intelligent. The aim is to identify specific mathematical or computational advantages and determine whether they translate into useful improvements on real learning problems.

Two fields with different starting points

Machine learning begins with data, a model and an objective. The model makes predictions, compares them with the correct answers or another learning signal, and adjusts its parameters.

Quantum computing begins with quantum states, quantum gates and measurements. A quantum circuit prepares a state, transforms it through a sequence of operations, and produces measurement outcomes from which useful information is extracted.

QML connects these approaches. A classical computer might prepare the input data and optimise model parameters, while a quantum processor evaluates part of the model. Alternatively, a learning algorithm may run largely on a quantum computer and use quantum data directly.

The important point is that quantum machine learning is not one algorithm. It is a broad family of approaches, and understanding the type of data and the type of computation involved is the natural place to begin.

15.1 Four Flavours: CC, CQ, QC and QQ

One of the clearest ways to understand quantum machine learning is to classify systems according to two questions:

  1. Is the input data classical or quantum?
  2. Is the learning computation classical or quantum?

Combining the answers gives four broad categories: CC, CQ, QC and QQ. The letters describe the type of data and the type of learning computation, in that order.

Notation used in this chapter:
The first letter refers to the data, and the second refers to the learning computation. C means classical and Q means quantum. These labels describe a useful taxonomy, although terminology can vary between research papers.
Category Input data Learning computation Basic idea
CC Classical Classical Ordinary machine learning.
CQ Classical Quantum Encode classical examples into quantum states and process them with quantum circuits.
QC Quantum Classical Use quantum data or measurements as inputs to a classical learning procedure.
QQ Quantum Quantum Use quantum data and quantum learning computation together.

15.1.1 CC: Classical Data, Classical Learning

CC is the familiar world of machine learning. The input consists of ordinary digital data, and the learning algorithm runs on classical hardware.

Suppose a bank wants to predict whether a transaction is fraudulent. Each transaction can be represented using features such as its amount, time, location, merchant category and other relevant information. A classical model studies labelled transactions and learns to estimate the likelihood of fraud.

A neural network might calculate a prediction, compare it with the known label, and adjust its weights through gradient descent and backpropagation. The training process repeats until the model learns useful patterns or reaches a chosen stopping condition.

Classical data → Classical model → Classical prediction

CC provides the baseline against which a proposed QML method should be compared. A quantum approach that takes longer, costs more or predicts less accurately than a good classical model has not demonstrated a practical advantage merely because it uses qubits.

15.1.2 CQ: Classical Data, Quantum Learning

In CQ, the data begins as ordinary classical information, but some of the computation takes place on a quantum processor. This is one of the most widely studied approaches in current QML.

Suppose each example contains three numerical features: temperature, pressure and humidity. A quantum circuit cannot directly accept these numbers as abstract mathematical values. They must first be mapped into a quantum state through a process called quantum data encoding.

One approach is angle encoding. A numerical feature determines the angle of a rotation gate applied to a qubit. For example, a feature value may determine the angle of a rotation around the Y axis.

|0⟩ → RY(θ)|0⟩

RY(θ)|0⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + sin(θ/2)|1⟩

Here, θ is an angle derived from the input feature. The rotation changes the qubit's state. The resulting state contains information about the feature, although measurement does not reveal the full quantum state in a single shot.

A quantum model may then apply trainable gates. These gates contain adjustable parameters, often represented by θ or a parameter vector. The circuit is measured, and the measurement outcomes are used to calculate a prediction or a loss value.

A common example is a variational quantum classifier. A classical optimiser adjusts the circuit's parameters to reduce a classification loss. The quantum circuit evaluates the model for the current parameters, while the classical computer helps decide how the parameters should change.

This arrangement is called hybrid quantum-classical learning. It is particularly important because present-day quantum devices have limitations in qubit counts, noise and circuit execution.

Important distinction: A quantum circuit does not automatically learn from classical data. The data must be encoded, a suitable model must be selected, and a training procedure must connect the circuit's outputs to the learning objective.

15.1.3 QC: Quantum Data, Classical Learning

QC reverses the data situation. The input is quantum, but the learning procedure is classical. This category becomes particularly relevant when the data is naturally produced by a quantum system.

Imagine that a researcher studies a molecule. The molecule's electronic state is quantum mechanical. A quantum experiment or quantum simulator may prepare that state and perform measurements on it.

The researcher may collect measurement results, expectation values or other accessible features and provide them to a classical learning algorithm. The classical algorithm could then classify the state, estimate a property or identify patterns across different experimental conditions.

The distinction between the quantum state and the data obtained from it matters. A quantum state cannot generally be copied perfectly or read in full through one measurement. Instead, experiments provide samples from measurement distributions. Learning systems must work with the information that can actually be extracted.

Quantum state → Measurements or accessible features → Classical learning algorithm

Consider quantum state classification. A device prepares states under different conditions, and an experiment measures selected observables. A classical classifier learns to associate the resulting feature vectors with labels.

The resulting learning algorithm may be completely classical. Its special feature is the origin of the data, rather than the hardware used for training.

A subtle point: Measurement outcomes from a quantum experiment are ordinary classical records, such as strings of 0s and 1s. In the QC category, the underlying examples originate as quantum states. Once those states have been measured, the learner usually receives classical information about them.

15.1.4 QQ: Quantum Data, Quantum Learning

QQ combines quantum data with quantum learning computation. Both sides of the learning problem involve quantum systems.

Suppose a quantum device prepares a collection of quantum states representing different physical conditions. Another quantum circuit processes these states and produces measurement outcomes that are used to distinguish between categories.

In an idealised setting, a learning procedure may operate directly on quantum states and quantum operations rather than converting all the relevant information into ordinary feature vectors first. This can be useful when the structure of the original data is itself quantum mechanical.

However, QQ is not automatically more powerful than the other categories. Preparing the data, applying quantum operations, estimating outputs and obtaining useful results all have costs. Quantum information also cannot be extracted arbitrarily from a state, so the learning task must respect the rules of quantum measurement and information.

Quantum data → Quantum learning process → Quantum measurement → Classical decision or output

Even in a QQ system, the final answer may be a classical value, such as a predicted label or an estimated energy. The classification describes the data and learning computation, not necessarily the format of the final answer.

The four flavours are a map of the field, not a ranking from weakest to strongest. CC can be the best choice for an ordinary business dataset, CQ may be worth studying for certain circuit models, and QC or QQ may be natural when the source data is genuinely quantum.

15.2 Motivations: Speed, Expressivity and New Model Classes

Why should anyone try to combine quantum computing with machine learning when classical machine learning is already so successful? Researchers investigate QML for three broad reasons: computational speed, expressive power and the possibility of developing new kinds of models.

These motivations are related, but they are not interchangeable. A model may represent a complicated function without being faster to train. A quantum algorithm may have a theoretical speed advantage without producing a better prediction. A new model class may be scientifically useful even if it does not outperform classical systems on every benchmark.

15.2.1 Motivation One: Computational Speed

Classical machine learning can be expensive. Training large neural networks may require enormous numbers of operations, repeated passes over data and significant memory. Researchers therefore ask whether quantum algorithms can reduce the cost of certain computational tasks that arise in learning.

A quantum computer is not simply a classical computer that performs every calculation faster. Its potential advantage comes from the way quantum states evolve and interfere, and from the kinds of operations that can be performed efficiently on those states.

Quantum algorithms have demonstrated theoretical advantages for particular problems. Some QML proposals attempt to use related ideas for tasks such as linear algebra, estimating properties of structured data or processing quantum states.

Where could speed come from?

  • Quantum state processing: If the input is already available as a quantum state, certain quantum operations may process its structure without first converting every detail into an ordinary classical representation.
  • Quantum linear algebra: Some algorithms offer theoretical speedups for specific matrix problems under particular assumptions about input access, conditioning, precision and output requirements.
  • Quantum sampling: A quantum device can generate samples from distributions that may be difficult for classical systems to reproduce efficiently in certain settings. Whether those samples improve a learning task is a separate question.
  • Structured physical problems: Quantum processors may eventually help with learning tasks involving quantum chemistry, materials or quantum experiments, where the underlying system is naturally quantum mechanical.

There is an important qualification. A theoretical speedup often depends on how the input is supplied. If loading a large classical dataset into quantum states takes substantial time, the loading cost may remove the advantage.

Likewise, if an algorithm produces a compact quantum state but the user needs millions of individual classical predictions, extracting those results can be expensive. A fair comparison must include data preparation, circuit execution, measurement, optimisation and output extraction.

Do not confuse a fast internal calculation with a fast end-to-end learning system. The relevant question is whether the complete quantum-assisted workflow solves the intended task more efficiently than a competitive classical alternative.

15.2.2 Motivation Two: Expressivity

Expressivity refers to the range and complexity of functions a model can represent. A highly expressive model can describe complicated relationships between inputs and outputs. However, having more expressive power does not automatically mean that the model can learn those relationships from limited data.

Consider a dataset containing points that form a circle. A simple linear classifier draws a straight boundary. It may fail to separate points inside the circle from points outside it. A more flexible model can represent a curved decision boundary.

Classical machine learning addresses such problems using methods including kernel functions, decision trees and neural networks. Quantum machine learning explores whether quantum states and quantum circuits can provide useful alternative representations of the input data.

Quantum feature maps

A feature map transforms an input into a representation that a learning model can use. In a quantum model, a classical input x may be encoded into a quantum state using a parameterised quantum operation.

|φ(x)⟩ = U(x)|0...0⟩

Here, x represents the input features, U(x) is a quantum circuit that depends on those features, and |0...0⟩ is the initial state of the qubits. The resulting state |φ(x)⟩ is the quantum feature representation.

The circuit may apply rotations, entangling gates and other operations. These can transform the input into a state whose measurement statistics depend on relationships among the features.

The point is not that the quantum state magically stores every possible answer. Rather, the feature map defines a particular mathematical representation. The quality of that representation depends on the problem, the circuit design and the information that can be extracted from the state.

Quantum kernels

Kernel methods compare examples through a similarity function. Instead of explicitly constructing every feature of a complex representation, a kernel calculates a measure of similarity between two inputs.

A quantum feature map can define a kernel based on the overlap between the states associated with two inputs.

K(x, z) = |⟨φ(x)|φ(z)⟩|²

This expression calculates the squared magnitude of the overlap between the quantum states representing x and z. If the states are identical, the overlap is maximal. If they are orthogonal, the overlap is zero.

A quantum computer can estimate such similarities using an appropriate circuit and measurement procedure. A classical learning algorithm, such as a support vector machine, can then use the resulting kernel values to construct a decision boundary.

This is a useful example of how a quantum representation can be combined with a classical learning algorithm. However, calculating the kernel for many pairs of examples may require many circuit executions. The resulting kernel matrix can become expensive to construct.

Expressivity is not the same as learnability. A model might represent a very complicated function but still be difficult to train, sensitive to noise or prone to fitting the training examples without generalising to new data.

15.2.3 Motivation Three: New Model Classes

A model class is a family of mathematical functions that can be produced by a particular learning architecture. Neural networks, decision trees and support vector machines are examples of different model classes.

Quantum computing introduces additional design possibilities. Quantum circuits have parameters, gates, entanglement patterns and measurement choices. These ingredients can be combined to define learning models that differ from ordinary neural networks.

Variational quantum circuits

A variational quantum circuit contains adjustable parameters. The circuit produces an output that depends on both the input and the parameters. A classical optimiser can tune those parameters to improve the model's predictions.

f(x, θ) = ⟨ψ(x, θ)| O |ψ(x, θ)⟩

In this expression:

  • x represents the input data.
  • θ represents the trainable circuit parameters.
  • |ψ(x, θ)⟩ is the quantum state prepared by the circuit.
  • O is an observable, a measurable physical quantity.
  • The expectation value gives the average measurement result predicted by the model.

By changing θ, the circuit changes the state and therefore changes its expected output. Training searches for parameter values that minimise a loss function or otherwise improve a learning objective.

The architecture may be designed for classification, regression, generative modelling or another task. It may use a quantum circuit as the whole model or as one component in a larger classical system.

Quantum generative models

Generative models learn how to produce new examples resembling a dataset. Classical examples include generative adversarial networks, variational autoencoders and diffusion models.

A quantum generative model may use a quantum circuit to produce measurement samples from a learned probability distribution. The training process adjusts the circuit so that its outputs match useful properties of the target data.

Such models are interesting because quantum measurement naturally produces probabilistic outcomes. However, a probabilistic output alone does not make a model useful. The generated distribution must capture meaningful structure, and the complete model must be competitive with suitable classical generative methods.

Quantum-enhanced scientific learning

Some of the most natural opportunities for QML may occur in scientific research. Quantum systems can be difficult to simulate accurately using classical methods as their size and complexity increase. Quantum hardware may eventually help researchers process quantum data or estimate properties of physical systems.

For example, researchers may use learning methods to identify phases of matter, estimate molecular properties or recognise patterns in quantum experiments. A quantum computer may assist with state preparation, measurement or parts of the model.

This is different from claiming that a quantum computer will improve every image classifier, chatbot or recommendation engine. The strongest motivation depends on the structure of the actual problem.

How the three motivations differ

Motivation Main question What would count as progress?
Speed Can the learning task be completed with fewer resources? A meaningful end-to-end improvement in time, cost or computational scaling.
Expressivity Can the model represent useful relationships more effectively? A useful representation that improves learning on relevant tasks.
New model classes Can quantum circuits enable learning approaches that are otherwise difficult to realise? A model with useful properties, validated against suitable alternatives.

These motivations should be treated as research questions rather than guaranteed benefits. Quantum machine learning becomes scientifically convincing when a proposed advantage can be explained mathematically, tested experimentally and compared fairly with classical methods.

15.3 The QML Landscape and Taxonomy

Quantum machine learning contains several research directions. They overlap, but each focuses on a different part of the learning process. A useful way to organise the field is to examine how quantum circuits are used, what type of information is processed, and which learning objective is being solved.

15.3.1 Variational Quantum Machine Learning

Variational quantum machine learning uses parameterised quantum circuits as models. The circuit has trainable parameters, and an optimisation procedure adjusts them to improve a chosen objective.

A typical workflow begins by preparing an input state, applying parameterised gates, measuring an observable and calculating a loss from the output. A classical optimiser proposes new parameters, and the process repeats.

This approach is popular because it can be adapted to different hardware devices and learning objectives. It is also compatible with hybrid systems, in which quantum and classical computations share the work.

The difficulty is that training may be unstable or expensive. Quantum measurements are probabilistic, so estimating an output with sufficient accuracy may require repeated circuit executions. Some circuits also suffer from barren plateaus, where gradients become extremely small and optimisation becomes difficult.

15.3.2 Quantum Kernel Methods

Quantum kernel methods use quantum circuits to calculate similarities between data points. The kernel values are then supplied to a classical learning algorithm.

In a typical example, a quantum circuit encodes each input into a state. An experiment estimates the similarity between pairs of states. A classical support vector machine uses the resulting kernel matrix to classify new examples.

The attraction is that the quantum feature representation may capture relationships that are difficult for certain classical representations to reproduce efficiently. The challenge is demonstrating that this advantage is useful after accounting for circuit costs, measurement noise and classical alternatives.

15.3.3 Quantum Neural Networks

The term quantum neural network, or QNN, is often used for trainable quantum circuit models. Despite the name, these models do not necessarily reproduce the architecture of a classical neural network.

A classical neural network combines weighted sums, activation functions and layers. A quantum circuit instead applies quantum gates to a state and obtains outputs through measurement. Trainable rotations may play a role similar to adjustable parameters, but the underlying mathematics is different.

A QNN can contain encoding layers, trainable layers and entangling operations. The choice of these components affects its representational ability, trainability and sensitivity to hardware noise.

Researchers are studying QNNs for classification, regression, scientific prediction and other tasks. Whether a particular QNN is better than a classical neural network depends on the problem and the quality of the comparison.

15.3.4 Quantum Generative Learning

Quantum generative models attempt to learn probability distributions and generate samples from them. They may use variational circuits, quantum Born machines or hybrid architectures that combine quantum sampling with classical optimisation.

In a Born-machine-style model, the probability of obtaining a particular measurement result is determined by the squared magnitude of the corresponding quantum-state amplitude. Training aims to make the resulting distribution resemble the target distribution according to a selected objective.

Potential applications include structured data generation and the study of probability distributions that arise in physical systems. Yet efficient training and evaluation remain difficult, especially when the target distribution is large or when only a limited number of measurements are available.

15.3.5 Quantum Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning involves an agent interacting with an environment. The agent selects actions, receives rewards and learns a policy that aims to maximise long-term reward.

Quantum reinforcement learning explores the use of quantum computation in parts of this process. A quantum circuit might help represent a policy, estimate values or process certain structured states.

The environment itself may be classical, quantum or simulated. A quantum agent does not automatically learn a better policy; it must still balance exploration, exploitation, reward estimation and the costs of interacting with the environment.

15.3.6 Quantum Learning Theory

Quantum learning theory studies the mathematical foundations of learning with quantum resources. It asks questions such as how much data is needed, how well a model can generalise, which problems can be learned efficiently, and what quantum resources are required.

This branch is essential because a successful experiment on a small dataset does not establish a general computational advantage. Theoretical analysis can identify conditions under which an advantage is possible and the assumptions needed for it to hold.

15.3.7 Quantum-Inspired Classical Machine Learning

Quantum-inspired algorithms use ideas associated with quantum information or quantum algorithms but execute entirely on classical computers. They are related to QML, but they are not themselves quantum computation.

For example, a classical algorithm may use tensor networks or techniques inspired by quantum-state representations to organise a large computation. These methods can sometimes perform well without quantum hardware.

Quantum-inspired methods are important for two reasons. First, they may provide useful practical algorithms today. Second, they are strong competitors in comparisons with quantum approaches. A quantum method should not be compared only with an outdated or deliberately weak classical baseline.

15.3.8 Quantum Data and Quantum Experiments

Another major direction focuses on learning directly from quantum systems. Examples include estimating molecular properties, classifying quantum states, identifying physical phases and analysing experimental measurements.

These problems are distinctive because the data may not be available as an ordinary table of numbers at the start. Researchers must consider how quantum states are prepared, which measurements can be performed and how much information can be extracted from each experiment.

In this setting, QML may be valuable not because quantum computation makes an unrelated classical task faster, but because the underlying scientific problem already involves quantum information.

How the different directions fit together

These categories are not mutually exclusive. A variational quantum neural network may also be a hybrid learning model. A quantum kernel can be used for classification. A quantum generative model may be trained using variational optimisation. A scientific QML project may combine quantum data, a quantum circuit and classical statistical analysis.

The taxonomy therefore helps us ask precise questions. What is the input? What does the quantum processor actually do? Which part is trained? What is the output? Which classical method is the correct comparison? These questions are more useful than simply labelling a project as quantum AI.

15.4 Realistic Expectations vs. Hype

Quantum machine learning attracts attention because quantum computing is based on unfamiliar physical principles and may offer computational advantages for certain tasks. However, the excitement surrounding a technology must be separated from what experiments and mathematical results have actually established.

The most useful attitude is neither to dismiss QML nor to assume it will replace classical AI. It is to understand the conditions under which quantum resources may be useful and to test each claim carefully.

15.4.1 Myth: A Quantum Computer Tries Every Answer at Once

Superposition allows a quantum state to contain amplitudes associated with multiple basis states. For example, a system of n qubits can have a state described using 2ⁿ computational basis amplitudes.

|ψ⟩ = Σₓ αₓ|x⟩

Σₓ |αₓ|² = 1

Here, each |x⟩ represents a computational basis state, αₓ is its complex amplitude, and the squared magnitude of an amplitude determines the probability of observing that state in the corresponding measurement.

Although the mathematical state contains many amplitudes, a measurement does not reveal all of them. It produces an outcome according to the probability distribution. Repeated experiments can estimate that distribution, but the number of measurements and the structure of the circuit matter.

Quantum algorithms aim to manipulate amplitudes through interference so that useful outcomes become more likely. This can be powerful, but it does not provide automatic access to every possible answer.

15.4.2 Myth: More Qubits Automatically Mean Better AI

The number of qubits is only one measure of a quantum processor. A learning application also depends on gate quality, connectivity, circuit depth, measurement accuracy, error rates and the ability to run the required circuit reliably.

A large circuit may be difficult to train if its operations are noisy or if the model's gradients are too small. A smaller circuit with a carefully designed architecture may be more useful for a particular experiment.

Furthermore, more parameters do not automatically improve generalisation. A model still needs suitable data, an appropriate objective and a training procedure that discovers useful parameter values.

15.4.3 Myth: Quantum Models Will Replace Neural Networks

Classical neural networks benefit from mature software, specialised hardware, large datasets and decades of research into optimisation and generalisation. They already perform extremely well on many language, vision, audio and recommendation tasks.

Quantum models face additional costs, including quantum state preparation, circuit execution, repeated measurements and transfers between classical and quantum systems. Current quantum processors also have limitations in reliability and scale.

It is therefore entirely possible that classical models will remain the best choice for many practical applications. QML may eventually offer an advantage in selected problems without replacing the broader classical machine learning ecosystem.

15.4.4 The Encoding Bottleneck

Encoding is one of the most important practical challenges in QML. Classical datasets may contain millions of examples, each with hundreds or thousands of features. A quantum processor has a limited number of physical qubits and requires a circuit to map classical values into quantum states.

Some encodings use a separate qubit or gate operation for each feature. Others attempt to place information into amplitudes or more structured quantum states. These approaches have different costs and requirements.

A compact encoding is not necessarily cheap. Preparing a complicated quantum state can require a large number of operations, and retrieving useful information may require many measurements.

If a claimed advantage depends on representing a large classical dataset inside a quantum state, always ask: how is that state prepared, how long does preparation take, and what information can actually be recovered from it?

15.4.5 The Training Bottleneck

Many QML models are trained by repeatedly evaluating a quantum circuit and adjusting its parameters. A single optimisation step may require multiple circuit evaluations. If each evaluation must be repeated many times to estimate its output accurately, the total cost can grow quickly.

Training can also be difficult because of the shape of the loss function. Some parameterised quantum circuits exhibit barren plateaus: regions where gradients become extremely small, making it hard for a gradient-based optimiser to identify a useful direction for improvement.

Barren plateaus do not occur in every quantum model, but their existence highlights a fundamental lesson: a model that can represent a solution may still be difficult to train to find that solution.

15.4.6 The Measurement Bottleneck

Quantum circuits typically return measurement outcomes rather than an unrestricted description of the final quantum state. If a model needs an expectation value, that value is usually estimated by repeating the circuit and averaging the observed results.

Measurement uncertainty decreases as more independent samples are collected. For many simple sampling settings, achieving a fixed estimation precision requires a number of samples that scales roughly with the inverse square of the desired error tolerance.

Number of samples ∝ 1 / ε²

Here, ε represents a target estimation error, under appropriate assumptions about the measured quantity. This is not a universal formula for every quantum experiment, but it illustrates why obtaining highly precise results can be costly.

The overall learning process may require measurements during training, validation and final prediction. These costs must be included when evaluating performance.

15.4.7 The Classical Baseline Problem

Suppose a quantum classifier achieves 92 per cent accuracy on a small dataset. Is that evidence of a quantum advantage? Not by itself.

A classical logistic regression model, support vector machine or carefully tuned neural network might achieve 96 per cent accuracy on the same task. Alternatively, the quantum model might perform better on a small sample but require much more computation.

A convincing comparison should use appropriate classical baselines, comparable data, sound statistical procedures and transparent reporting of computational costs. The goal is to test whether the quantum method contributes something meaningful, not merely to show that it can produce a prediction.

15.4.8 What Counts as a Quantum Advantage?

The phrase quantum advantage can refer to different achievements, and these should not be confused.

  • Computational advantage: A quantum method performs a well-defined task more efficiently than the relevant classical alternatives under specified assumptions.
  • Learning advantage: Quantum resources improve a meaningful learning measure, such as sample efficiency or the ability to learn a target function, under a carefully defined comparison.
  • Practical advantage: A quantum-assisted system solves a useful real-world task with better end-to-end performance, cost or resource usage.

A theoretical result may establish an advantage under certain assumptions without demonstrating that today's hardware can realise it. Similarly, a quantum experiment that is difficult to simulate classically does not by itself show that the system can train a useful predictive model.

15.4.9 A Balanced View of QML

What is promising? What must still be established?
Quantum circuits provide new ways to represent and transform information. Whether those representations help on useful learning tasks.
Some quantum algorithms have theoretical computational advantages. Whether the assumptions hold for complete learning workflows.
Quantum data occurs naturally in chemistry and physics. Whether available devices can produce reliable scientific improvements.
Hybrid quantum-classical training is possible. Whether optimisation, measurement and hardware costs are manageable.
New quantum model classes can be studied mathematically. Whether they generalise and compete with well-designed classical models.

The realistic expectation is that QML will develop through carefully selected problems, better hardware, improved algorithms and rigorous experiments. Its future may be significant without requiring every AI application to become quantum.

15.5 Open Questions in the Field

Quantum machine learning is an active research area because many important questions remain unanswered. These questions are not minor implementation details. They concern whether quantum learning can provide a reliable, scalable and scientifically meaningful advantage.

15.5.1 Which Learning Problems Truly Benefit from Quantum Computation?

This is one of the central questions in QML. A quantum algorithm may be mathematically interesting without offering a useful advantage for a practical learning task.

Researchers need to identify problems where quantum resources improve a clearly defined objective. The improvement might concern computation time, the amount of training data required, prediction quality under a specified resource budget or the ability to learn a function that is difficult for a particular class of classical models.

The comparison must be precise. Which classical algorithms are allowed? What assumptions are made about data access? How many measurements can the quantum model use? What output must the algorithm produce?

Until these questions are answered, a statement such as "quantum machine learning is faster" is too broad to be scientifically useful.

15.5.2 Can Quantum Models Generalise Well?

Generalisation is the ability to perform well on unseen examples rather than merely memorising the training data. It is essential for any useful learning system.

Quantum models may have large state spaces and complicated parameterised circuits. But the number of possible states does not directly tell us how well a model will generalise. Generalisation depends on the hypothesis class, training procedure, data distribution and other properties of the learning problem.

Researchers study how circuit depth, entanglement, parameter count and the structure of the quantum feature map affect the ability to learn useful patterns from limited data.

A model that fits the training set perfectly but performs poorly on new examples is not a successful learning model, regardless of whether its operations are quantum or classical.

15.5.3 How Should Quantum Data Be Encoded?

Data encoding affects both the expressivity of a quantum model and its computational cost. A good encoding should represent relevant features without requiring an impractical number of qubits or operations.

Angle encoding is relatively intuitive, but may require repeated operations when the input has many features. Amplitude encoding can represent a vector through state amplitudes, but preparing an arbitrary amplitude-encoded state can be expensive.

More advanced encodings may exploit problem structure, symmetry or correlations. The open question is how to select an encoding that preserves useful information and supports efficient training on available hardware.

15.5.4 How Can Quantum Models Be Trained Reliably?

Training a quantum model involves estimating outputs, calculating or approximating gradients and updating parameters. Noise and limited measurement budgets can make each of these steps difficult.

Research directions include better circuit architectures, improved initialisation, gradient estimation methods, optimisation strategies and techniques for reducing the effect of noise.

One important question is how to avoid barren plateaus without making the circuit too restricted to represent useful functions. Another is how to balance circuit depth against the need for expressive power.

15.5.5 How Much Does Noise Matter?

Physical quantum processors are affected by errors in gate operations, measurements and interactions with their surroundings. These errors can alter the output distribution of a circuit.

A learning model may tolerate some noise, but the effect depends on the task and the architecture. Noise may reduce accuracy, distort gradients or change the similarity values used by a quantum kernel.

Researchers must determine when error mitigation is sufficient, when quantum error correction is necessary, and how the resource costs of either approach affect the final learning system.

Fault-tolerant quantum computers could enable more reliable and deeper computations, but error correction introduces overhead. The relevant question is not simply whether noise can be reduced, but whether the resulting system remains useful after accounting for the resources required.

15.5.6 Can We Make Fair and Reproducible Comparisons?

A research result is only as convincing as the comparison used to support it. QML experiments can be difficult to reproduce because they depend on circuit design, optimiser settings, random initialisation, hardware calibration and measurement budgets.

Strong evaluations should report the data preprocessing, model architecture, training procedure, number of circuit evaluations, measurement shots and relevant hardware conditions.

They should also compare against classical algorithms that have been tuned appropriately. If a quantum method is evaluated on a small dataset, the researchers should explain why that dataset is relevant and whether the result is likely to hold at a larger scale.

Reproducibility is especially important when the measured improvement is small. A tiny difference in accuracy may arise from statistical variation rather than a genuine improvement in the learning method.

15.5.7 How Should We Measure Resource Costs?

Counting qubits alone is insufficient. A useful resource analysis should include the operations needed to prepare inputs, the depth of quantum circuits, the number of measurement shots, classical optimisation and the cost of transferring data between systems.

For fault-tolerant algorithms, the number of logical qubits and the cost of implementing reliable logical operations also matter. Physical qubits and logical qubits are not interchangeable because quantum error correction generally requires additional physical resources to protect logical information.

Researchers need consistent ways to compare these resources across different hardware platforms and algorithms. Without such comparisons, claims about efficiency can be misleading.

15.5.8 Can Quantum Learning Work at Realistic Data Scales?

Many demonstrations use small datasets because current quantum hardware has limited capacity and execution time. However, practical machine learning often involves millions of examples, high-dimensional inputs or repeated predictions under strict latency requirements.

A model that works on a handful of features may not scale efficiently when the input grows. The circuit may become deeper, state preparation more expensive and measurement requirements larger.

Future research must establish whether useful quantum methods can operate at relevant scales, or whether they are most effective as specialised components within larger classical systems.

15.5.9 What Role Will Hybrid Architectures Play?

Quantum computers are unlikely to operate in isolation from classical systems for many near-term applications. Classical computers are well suited to data handling, optimisation, orchestration and conventional numerical calculations.

Quantum processors may be used for a specialised operation, such as evaluating a circuit or estimating a quantity that is difficult to obtain in another way. The classical system then incorporates the result into the larger workflow.

This raises practical questions about how to divide computation between the two systems, minimise communication overhead, schedule quantum jobs and handle noisy or delayed results.

A successful hybrid architecture will need to provide a genuine benefit from its quantum component rather than simply adding another complex dependency.

15.5.10 Can We Understand What a Quantum Model Has Learned?

Interpretability is the study of how a model makes predictions and which aspects of its input influence the output. It matters in scientific discovery and in applications where predictions must be explained.

Quantum circuits are governed by mathematical operations, but understanding a circuit's internal state can be difficult. Measurement reveals only selected information, and a large quantum state cannot generally be inspected completely through a single experiment.

Researchers are therefore investigating how to analyse quantum feature maps, identify the role of circuit components and connect model predictions to meaningful physical or statistical properties.

Interpretability could be particularly valuable when a quantum model is used to study molecules, materials or physical systems. In those applications, the goal may be not only to predict an outcome but also to understand the underlying scientific relationship.

15.5.11 What Are the Best Applications for QML?

It is tempting to apply quantum computing to every important machine learning problem. A better strategy is to identify applications where quantum resources are naturally connected to the structure of the data or the computation.

Quantum chemistry and materials science are important areas because the underlying systems are quantum mechanical. Quantum-state classification and learning from quantum experiments are also natural candidates. Other fields may become suitable if a well-defined computational advantage can be demonstrated.

By contrast, a conventional classification task with abundant labelled data and highly optimised classical solutions may offer little immediate reason to use a quantum processor.

The most promising applications will be identified through evidence rather than by assuming that every complicated problem benefits from quantum computation.

15.5.12 How Will the Field Develop as Hardware Improves?

Better hardware may allow deeper circuits, more reliable measurements and larger experiments. Fault-tolerant systems could make algorithms practical that are currently beyond the reach of noisy devices.

Nevertheless, improved hardware will not automatically solve every learning challenge. Data encoding, model design, optimisation and fair evaluation will remain important even on more reliable quantum processors.

A central open question is which QML ideas will remain useful as hardware evolves. Some methods may be suited to near-term noisy devices, while others may require fault-tolerant quantum computation. Still others may turn out to have efficient classical alternatives.

Understanding these distinctions will help researchers direct their effort towards approaches with a realistic path from mathematical promise to practical use.

How to Evaluate a Quantum Machine Learning Idea

When you encounter a new QML paper, do not begin by asking how many qubits the authors used or whether the model is called a quantum neural network. Begin with the learning problem itself.

2. Inspect the data

3. Identify the quantum role

4. Examine the assumptions

→
5. Check the baseline
What classical methods were tested?
→
6. Measure the benefit

Questions every QML researcher should ask

  • What precise problem is the algorithm solving?
  • Is the input classical data, quantum data or a mixture?
  • How is the input represented in the quantum system?
  • Does the algorithm need a quantum processor during training, prediction or both?
  • How many qubits, circuit operations and measurement shots are required?
  • How are noise and measurement uncertainty handled?
  • Which competitive classical algorithms were used as baselines?
  • Does the advantage include data preparation and output extraction?
  • Does the result hold beyond a small demonstration?
  • Can another researcher reproduce the result?
A good QML researcher needs to understand both sides of the problem: the learning theory that explains how a model learns, and the quantum computing theory that explains what the quantum processor can actually do.

A Worked Example: Classifying Two Types of Data

Let us bring the ideas together using a simple example. Imagine a researcher wants to classify samples into two categories, A and B, based on two numerical features. The aim is to predict the category of a new sample.

Approach 1: Classical machine learning (CC)

The researcher collects labelled samples and trains a classical classifier. The model learns a decision boundary from the training examples. Its predictions are evaluated on samples that were not used for training.

Approach 2: A quantum feature map (CQ)

The researcher maps each feature vector into a quantum state. A circuit applies rotations and entangling gates, and measurements produce quantities used to calculate the predicted category.

A classical optimiser adjusts the circuit parameters. The researcher then compares the trained model with the classical classifier using the same data splits and a suitable evaluation procedure.

Approach 3: A quantum kernel (CQ)

Instead of directly training a quantum circuit to predict the label, the researcher uses a quantum circuit to estimate similarities between pairs of examples. A classical support vector machine uses these similarities to learn a decision boundary.

What would the experiment establish?

Suppose the quantum feature map performs better than a simple classical baseline. That is a reason to investigate further, but it does not yet establish quantum advantage. The researcher should compare against stronger classical models, test statistical reliability and measure the quantum circuit costs.

If the quantum approach consistently improves a meaningful metric or reduces the total resources required, the evidence becomes more interesting. If a well-tuned classical model performs equally well at a lower cost, the quantum method may still be scientifically interesting but has not demonstrated a practical advantage for this particular task.

The lesson: The value of a quantum learning model is determined by what it can reliably achieve, not merely by the fact that it uses a quantum circuit.

Chapter Summary

Quantum machine learning brings quantum computation into the study of learning algorithms. Its central purpose is to discover whether quantum resources can help with particular learning tasks, representations or scientific problems.

  • CC uses classical data and classical learning computation.
  • CQ uses classical data with quantum learning computation.
  • QC uses quantum data with classical learning computation.
  • QQ uses quantum data and quantum learning computation.
  • The main motivations are possible computational speedups, useful feature representations and new model classes.
  • Important research directions include variational quantum models, quantum kernels, quantum generative learning, quantum reinforcement learning and learning from quantum data.
  • Quantum models face challenges involving data encoding, measurement cost, optimisation, noise and generalisation.
  • A convincing advantage must be established through sound theory or fair experiments against competitive classical alternatives.
The big idea to remember: Quantum computing introduces new ways to represent and process information, while machine learning provides methods for discovering patterns from data. Quantum machine learning investigates where these two sets of ideas genuinely complement each other. Its future depends not on hype, but on mathematical insight, reliable hardware and convincing evidence.
