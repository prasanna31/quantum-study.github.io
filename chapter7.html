Chapter 7: Shor's Algorithm

Imagine that someone gives you a very large number and asks you to find the two prime numbers that were multiplied to produce it. Multiplying two numbers is usually straightforward. Reversing the process can be extremely difficult when the numbers are sufficiently large.

Modern cryptography takes advantage of this difference. Shor's algorithm, discovered by mathematician Peter Shor in 1994, showed that a sufficiently powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computer could factor large integers much more efficiently than the best known classical algorithms.

This chapter develops the idea from the factoring problem to the quantum circuit, and finally to its consequences for cybersecurity. The central lesson is that a quantum computer does not simply try every possible factor at once. It uses quantum interference and the Quantum Fourier Transform to uncover a hidden mathematical pattern.

7.1 The Factoring Problem and RSA

7.1.1 Why is factoring important?

Consider the number 21. We can write it as 3 multiplied by 7. Finding these factors is easy because the number is small. Now imagine a number containing hundreds or thousands of decimal digits. It may have been created by multiplying two enormous prime numbers. Recovering those prime numbers is a very different problem.

A prime number is a positive integer greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and itself. Examples include 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13. Prime numbers are important because every integer greater than 1 has a unique prime factorisation, apart from the order in which the factors are written.

Example: Factorisation
21 = 3 × 7

Multiplication takes us from 3 and 7 to 21. Factoring asks us to travel in the opposite direction: given 21, find 3 and 7. For small numbers, both directions are easy. For specially constructed large numbers, the reverse direction becomes much harder for classical computers.

The important distinction is between a problem that is difficult for currently known classical algorithms and one that is mathematically impossible. Factoring is not impossible. In fact, classical computers can factor many large integers. The difficulty grows substantially as the numbers become larger.

7.1.2 How does classical factoring work?

One simple method is trial division. To factor a number N, we test whether smaller integers divide it without leaving a remainder. We only need to test possible divisors up to the square root of N: if N has a non-trivial factorisation, at least one factor must be no greater than that square root.

This method is useful for understanding the problem, but it becomes impractical for very large integers. More advanced classical algorithms, particularly the general number field sieve, can factor suitable integers much faster than trial division. Even so, the computational cost remains significant for properly chosen RSA keys.

Shor's algorithm changes the situation because its main quantum subroutine solves a related problem called order finding. Factoring can then be reduced to finding the period of a mathematical sequence.

7.1.3 Understanding RSA

RSA is a public-key cryptographic system. It allows people to communicate securely without first sharing a secret encryption key through a private channel. RSA can also be used for digital signatures, although real-world signature systems require careful padding and implementation.

RSA relies on a pair of related keys: a public key that can be shared and a private key that must remain secret. The public key is constructed using a large number N formed by multiplying two secret primes.

The mathematical construction can be understood through the following steps.

  1. Choose two distinct prime numbers, p and q.
  2. Multiply them to obtain N.
  3. Calculate the value of Euler's totient function for N.
  4. Choose a public exponent e that is coprime to the totient.
  5. Calculate a private exponent d, which is the multiplicative inverse of e modulo the totient.
N = p × q

When p and q are distinct primes, Euler's totient function is given by:

φ(N) = (p − 1)(q − 1)

The public key contains N and e. The private exponent d satisfies:

e × d ≡ 1 (mod φ(N))

The symbol ≡ means that the two sides have the same remainder when divided by the modulus. The private exponent can be calculated efficiently when the secret primes, or the totient, are known.

A small RSA example

Choose p = 5 and q = 11.

N = 5 × 11 = 55
φ(55) = (5 − 1)(11 − 1) = 40

Choose e = 3. Since 3 and 40 have no common factor other than 1, this is a valid public exponent.

We need d such that 3d leaves a remainder of 1 when divided by 40. Choosing d = 27 works because:

3 × 27 = 81 ≡ 1 (mod 40)

The public key is (55, 3), while the private exponent is 27. These numbers are intentionally tiny, so this example is completely insecure. It illustrates the mathematics, not real-world RSA security.

7.1.4 Why does factoring threaten RSA?

The public modulus N is known to everyone. However, if an attacker can recover p and q, the attacker can calculate φ(N) and then derive d. The private key is no longer secret.

This explains the connection between factoring and RSA. RSA is not based on the claim that factoring is impossible. Its security depends on the computational difficulty of recovering the secret factors from a sufficiently large modulus, together with correct key generation and implementation.

There is an important qualification: factoring N gives an efficient way to break RSA, but the security of RSA is not formally proven to be equivalent to the factoring problem in every possible formulation. The practical attack is clear, even though the theoretical relationship is more nuanced.

7.1.5 Where does quantum computing enter the picture?

A classical computer processes information using bits. A quantum computer uses qubits, which can be placed in superpositions and manipulated through quantum gates.

Superposition alone does not make a quantum computer powerful. Measuring a superposition gives only one outcome. The advantage comes from arranging quantum operations so that amplitudes for useful outcomes reinforce one another while other amplitudes cancel.

Shor's algorithm uses this principle to discover the period of a modular arithmetic function. That period provides enough information to factor N with a high probability of success.

Key idea: Shor's algorithm does not directly ask a quantum computer to guess the secret prime factors. It transforms factoring into a problem of finding periodic structure, then uses quantum interference to extract information about that period.

7.2 Order Finding

7.2.1 From factoring to a repeating pattern

Suppose we want to factor an integer N. We choose another integer a such that 1 < a < N and the greatest common divisor of a and N is 1.

gcd(a, N) = 1

The greatest common divisor, written gcd, is the largest integer that divides both numbers exactly. If gcd(a, N) is already greater than 1, we have immediately found a non-trivial factor of N, and there is no need to run the quantum part of the algorithm.

Otherwise, consider the sequence of remainders obtained by repeatedly raising a to a power and dividing by N:

f(x) = aˣ mod N

Here, mod N means that we keep the remainder after division by N. Although the powers of a grow rapidly, the remainders can only take values from 0 to N − 1. Because a and N are coprime, the sequence eventually repeats in a structured way.

Example: A repeating modular sequence

Take a = 2 and N = 15. Calculate successive powers of 2, keeping only the remainder after division by 15.

x 2ˣ 2ˣ mod 15
011
122
244
388
4161
5322
6644
71288

The sequence of remainders is 1, 2, 4, 8, 1, 2, 4, 8, and so on. The pattern repeats every four steps.

7.2.2 What is the order?

The order of a modulo N is the smallest positive integer r such that raising a to the power r gives a remainder of 1 when divided by N. In mathematical notation:

aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N)

The smallest positive integer satisfying this equation is called the multiplicative order of a modulo N, written ordₙ(a).

In our example, 2⁴ = 16, and 16 leaves a remainder of 1 when divided by 15. No smaller positive power gives a remainder of 1. Therefore:

ord₁₅(2) = 4

Finding this number r is the order-finding problem. It is the central computational task inside Shor's algorithm.

7.2.3 How does the order help us factor N?

Suppose the order r is even. Then we can write:

aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N)

Since r is even, let x = a^(r/2). Squaring x gives aᵣ, so:

x² ≡ 1 (mod N)

Rearranging this relationship gives:

x² − 1 ≡ 0 (mod N)
(x − 1)(x + 1) ≡ 0 (mod N)

This means that N divides the product (x − 1)(x + 1). Under the right conditions, the two expressions have non-trivial common factors with N. We can discover those factors by calculating:

p' = gcd(a^(r/2) − 1, N)
q' = gcd(a^(r/2) + 1, N)

These values are candidate factors. If they are both greater than 1 and less than N, we have obtained a non-trivial factorisation.

7.2.4 Why can this method fail?

Finding an order does not guarantee that every choice of a will reveal the factors. There are two common reasons for failure.

  • The order r may be odd. In this case, the even-order factorisation step cannot be applied directly.
  • Even if r is even, a^(r/2) may be congruent to −1 modulo N. Then one of the two greatest common divisor calculations produces N and the other produces 1, rather than revealing useful factors.

The solution is to choose another value of a and try again. For suitable odd composite integers, the standard algorithm succeeds with a probability bounded away from zero over suitable random choices, and repeated attempts make success highly likely.

7.2.5 Why is order finding difficult classically but efficient quantumly?

A classical computer can calculate individual values of aˣ mod N efficiently. The difficulty is discovering the period efficiently for the large instances relevant to factoring. Merely generating a long list of remainders is not a practical general solution.

A quantum computer can prepare a superposition of many possible exponents, compute the corresponding modular function, and use interference to obtain information about the repeating pattern. The Quantum Fourier Transform, introduced in the previous chapter, is the key mathematical tool that makes this possible.

The quantum part is followed by classical processing, including continued fractions, greatest common divisor calculations and checks of candidate factors. Shor's algorithm is therefore a combination of quantum and classical computation, with the quantum subroutine providing the crucial speedup.

7.3 Full Algorithm Walkthrough

7.3.1 An overview of the complete process

We now have the two essential ingredients: the factoring problem can be reduced to order finding, and the Quantum Fourier Transform can help discover the order. Let us put them together.

  1. Choose the integer N that we want to factor.
  2. Choose a candidate integer a.
  3. Calculate gcd(a, N). If it is greater than 1, a factor is already known.
  4. Use a quantum circuit to obtain information about the order r.
  5. Use classical continued-fraction calculations to recover a candidate order.
  6. Check whether the candidate is valid and even.
  7. Calculate the two greatest common divisors.
  8. If the result is unsuccessful, repeat with another choice of a or another quantum measurement.

The classical steps are important. A quantum measurement does not normally display the order r directly. It produces a measurement result from which we infer the period.

7.3.2 Step 1: Choose the number to factor

Let us factor:

N = 15

In a real application, N might contain hundreds or thousands of bits. We use 15 because every step can be checked by hand.

7.3.3 Step 2: Choose a and check the greatest common divisor

Choose:

a = 2

Calculate gcd(2, 15). Since 2 and 15 share no common factor other than 1, we continue to the quantum order-finding stage.

gcd(2, 15) = 1

7.3.4 Step 3: Prepare the quantum registers

The order-finding circuit typically uses two quantum registers. The first register stores possible exponents, while the second register stores the results of modular exponentiation.

Let the first register have enough qubits to represent a range of exponents. If it contains m qubits, it can represent 2ᵐ computational basis states, from 0 to 2ᵐ − 1.

The first register is prepared in a uniform superposition of these basis states using Hadamard gates. In simplified notation:

|ψ⟩ = (1/√Q) Σₓ |x⟩

Here, Q = 2ᵐ and the sum runs from x = 0 to Q − 1. The symbol |x⟩ represents a computational basis state, and the factor 1/√Q ensures that the total probability is 1.

The second register is prepared in a suitable initial state, commonly the state representing the integer 1.

7.3.5 Step 4: Perform modular exponentiation

The quantum circuit applies a reversible operation that computes aˣ mod N while preserving the exponent x. Its action can be represented as:

|x⟩|1⟩ → |x⟩|aˣ mod N⟩

For our example, the second register contains the corresponding remainders:

1, 2, 4, 8, 1, 2, 4, 8, ...

Since the first register contains a superposition of exponents, the quantum state now contains correlations between exponents and their modular results. This is a quantum superposition of correlated possibilities, not a classical list that can be read out all at once.

Modular exponentiation is often the most expensive part of the circuit. A practical implementation must perform arithmetic reversibly, manage temporary qubits and control errors.

7.3.6 Step 5: Extract periodic information using the QFT

The first register contains information related to the periodic function aˣ mod N. Applying the inverse Quantum Fourier Transform converts this periodic structure into measurement outcomes that are concentrated near particular fractions.

For an idealised description, suppose the first register has Q = 2ᵐ possible states. The measured integer y often provides information approximately described by:

y / Q ≈ s / r

Here, r is the unknown order and s is an integer associated with the particular measurement. The approximation is important because Q is usually chosen as a power of two, and it may not be an exact multiple of r.

Different measurements may produce different values of s. Some measurements give especially useful information, while others may not immediately reveal the order.

7.3.7 Step 6: Recover the order using continued fractions

Continued fractions provide a classical method for finding rational numbers that closely approximate a given fraction. After measuring y, we examine the continued-fraction expansion of y/Q and test suitable denominators as candidates for r.

The candidate must be checked. A denominator recovered from one measurement may be a divisor of the true order rather than the order itself. We can test a candidate r by checking whether:

aʳ mod N = 1

If this test fails, the candidate is not the order. Additional measurements or further classical checks may be required.

There is a subtle but important point here. A single measurement does not always determine the full order. For example, if s and r share a common factor, the fraction s/r may simplify to a fraction with a smaller denominator. Practical order-finding algorithms handle this possibility through repeated measurements and verification.

7.3.8 Step 7: Calculate the factors

For our example, suppose the order-finding stage gives:

r = 4

This is correct because:

2⁴ = 16 ≡ 1 (mod 15)

The order is even, so we calculate:

a^(r/2) = 2² = 4

Now compute the greatest common divisors.

gcd(4 − 1, 15) = gcd(3, 15) = 3
gcd(4 + 1, 15) = gcd(5, 15) = 5

We have successfully recovered both factors:

15 = 3 × 5

Notice the division of responsibility. The quantum circuit helps identify the period. Classical arithmetic then converts the period into the factors.

7.3.9 The complete algorithm in pseudocode

The following pseudocode summarises the standard approach. The quantum order-finding subroutine is shown as one operation; its implementation includes state preparation, modular exponentiation, the inverse QFT, measurement and classical post-processing. 

function ShorFactor(N):

    if N is even:
        return 2, N / 2

    if N is a prime power:
        return its non-trivial factors

    repeat:
        choose a random integer a such that 1 < a < N

        g = gcd(a, N)

        if g > 1:
            return g, N / g

        r = QuantumOrderFinding(a, N)

        if r is invalid:
            continue

        if r is odd:
            continue

        x = modular_power(a, r / 2, N)

        if x == N - 1:
            continue

        p = gcd(x - 1, N)
        q = gcd(x + 1, N)

        if 1 < p < N and 1 < q < N:
            return p, q

        otherwise, repeat

This is an explanatory outline rather than production-ready code. A real implementation must handle measurement statistics, candidate-order verification, modular arithmetic, quantum circuit errors and additional edge cases. If N is composite but not a prime power, the repeated procedure can find non-trivial factors with high probability.

7.3.10 Where does the speedup come from?

The speedup comes from the way the quantum circuit extracts global information about the periodic function. It does not come from simply storing many values in superposition.

If we measured the exponent register immediately after modular exponentiation, we would obtain only one outcome. The useful structure becomes accessible because the quantum operations preserve and manipulate relative amplitudes, allowing interference to reveal information about the period.

For an input N with n bits, Shor's algorithm has polynomial quantum circuit complexity under standard arithmetic-circuit models. The precise resource count depends on the implementation, the desired success probability and whether we count logical gates, physical gates or total execution time.

Connection to the previous chapter: The Quantum Fourier Transform is useful here because periodic structure in one representation becomes concentrated frequency information in another. Shor's algorithm applies that principle to modular arithmetic.

7.4 Resource Estimates for Breaking Real-World Cryptography

7.4.1 Why is a quantum algorithm not enough?

Learning that Shor's algorithm is efficient does not mean that an existing quantum computer can immediately break modern RSA. An algorithm is a mathematical procedure. Running it successfully requires hardware with enough reliable qubits, gates and execution time to complete the computation before errors overwhelm the result.

Today's quantum processors have limited numbers of usable qubits and are affected by noise. A long computation can fail because small errors accumulate during gate operations, measurements and storage of quantum information.

Large-scale factoring therefore requires fault-tolerant quantum computation. This means encoding logical qubits using multiple physical qubits and repeatedly detecting and correcting errors while the computation proceeds.

7.4.2 Logical qubits and physical qubits

A logical qubit is a qubit protected by an error-correcting code. It is not simply one physical qubit. Many physical qubits may be required to create a logical qubit with an acceptably low error rate, depending on the hardware quality, error-correction method and target reliability.

For this reason, resource estimates distinguish between logical and physical qubits.

Resource Meaning Why it matters
Logical qubits Error-protected units of quantum information Determine how much protected information the algorithm can hold.
Physical qubits Actual hardware qubits Must support the logical qubits and error-correction operations.
Logical gates Operations performed on protected qubits Determine the logical computation and part of its runtime.
Non-Clifford resources Special operations or states used to implement general quantum circuits Can dominate the cost of fault-tolerant computation.
Code distance A parameter related to the error-detection and correction strength of a code Higher protection generally requires more physical resources and time.

7.4.3 How large is RSA-2048?

RSA-2048 uses a modulus N that is 2048 bits long. This means that N can be represented using 2048 binary digits. It does not mean that the quantum circuit requires exactly 2048 qubits.

The circuit also needs qubits for arithmetic, temporary values, control operations and error correction. The number of physical qubits can be much larger than the number of logical qubits because error correction introduces substantial overhead.

Different published implementations use different arithmetic circuits and error-correction assumptions. Some optimise the number of logical qubits, while others optimise gate counts or estimated runtime. Consequently, a resource estimate must always be read alongside the assumptions under which it was calculated.

7.4.4 Estimating the scale of the computation

The time required for fault-tolerant factoring depends on several interacting quantities:

  • The logical gate count of modular exponentiation and the other quantum operations.
  • The speed at which the hardware can perform the required physical operations.
  • The error rate of physical qubits and gates.
  • The error-correction code, its cycle time and the number of physical qubits available.
  • The overhead for operations such as preparing and using special resource states in fault-tolerant circuits.

A rough way to think about runtime is that the number of dependent logical operations, multiplied by the time required for each relevant operation, places a lower bound on execution time. Error correction and parallel operations affect the final result.

The asymptotic running time of Shor's algorithm is polynomial in the input size, unlike the best known classical general-purpose factoring algorithms, whose cost grows much more rapidly. However, asymptotic complexity alone does not tell us the exact number of hours or physical qubits needed for a particular RSA key. Those figures depend on the architecture and implementation.

7.4.5 Why estimates differ

Research papers sometimes report strikingly different qubit counts or runtimes for factoring the same RSA modulus. This does not necessarily mean that one result is incorrect. Researchers may be making different assumptions about hardware, error correction, gate connectivity, arithmetic design and acceptable failure probability.

A resource estimate might assume a very large, highly connected quantum processor with rapid error-correction cycles. Another might assume slower operations but more parallel hardware. Neither estimate can be applied universally without considering the assumptions.

Important distinction: A theoretical demonstration of polynomial-time factoring is not the same as a practical demonstration of breaking RSA-2048. As of October 2026, RSA-2048 has not been publicly demonstrated as factored by a quantum computer. Estimates about when this may become possible remain uncertain.

7.4.6 What about smaller cryptographic keys?

Researchers can use small numbers to demonstrate Shor's algorithm on real quantum devices. Such experiments are useful for testing quantum circuits, measurements and classical post-processing. They do not establish that the same device can break a modern cryptographic key.

For a small number, the classical solution may be trivial. The educational value lies in demonstrating the quantum procedure, not in showing a meaningful security threat.

7.4.7 Is RSA the only system at risk?

No. Shor's algorithm also threatens cryptographic systems whose security depends on the difficulty of discrete logarithms. This includes widely used public-key systems based on finite fields and elliptic curves.

RSA relies on factoring, while elliptic-curve cryptography relies on the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem. These are different mathematical problems, but sufficiently powerful fault-tolerant quantum computers can attack both efficiently using Shor-type methods.

By contrast, symmetric encryption and cryptographic hash functions face a different quantum threat. Grover's algorithm can provide a quadratic speedup for certain search tasks, rather than the exponential-type improvement in the relevant security problem that Shor's algorithm provides for factoring and discrete logarithms.

7.5 Implications and Post-Quantum Cryptography

7.5.1 What is the quantum threat?

Much of today's digital security relies on public-key cryptography. It protects secure web connections, software updates, digital signatures, financial transactions and the exchange of secret keys.

If an attacker had access to a sufficiently powerful fault-tolerant quantum computer, Shor's algorithm could undermine RSA and traditional discrete-logarithm systems. This would affect not only encrypted communication but also the systems that use vulnerable public-key algorithms to establish trust.

The risk is not that quantum computers automatically reveal every password or decrypt every file. An attacker still needs the relevant cryptographic material, access to the communication or stored data, and a practical way to exploit the vulnerable system. The algorithm changes the security assumptions on which certain cryptographic systems depend.

7.5.2 The harvest-now, decrypt-later threat

Consider an attacker who records encrypted communication today. The attacker may be unable to decrypt it using current resources. However, if the communication uses vulnerable public-key cryptography and the attacker retains the necessary data, a future quantum computer might make decryption possible.

This is commonly called the harvest-now, decrypt-later threat. It matters especially for information that must remain confidential for many years, such as long-term government records, sensitive business plans and certain personal information.

The risk depends on the protocol and the cryptographic design. Recording an encrypted session does not guarantee that a future attacker can decrypt it. But organisations should assess whether their key-establishment mechanisms are vulnerable and how long their data needs protection.

7.5.3 What is post-quantum cryptography?

Post-quantum cryptography, often abbreviated as PQC, is the study and use of cryptographic algorithms designed to resist attacks from both classical and known quantum algorithms.

These algorithms are designed to run on ordinary computers. They do not require users to own a quantum computer. The aim is to replace vulnerable cryptographic mechanisms with alternatives whose underlying mathematical problems are believed to remain difficult even for quantum attackers.

Post-quantum cryptography is different from quantum cryptography. Quantum cryptography uses quantum physical systems to support cryptographic tasks, while PQC uses classical computation and mathematical constructions designed for a post-quantum threat model.

7.5.4 Important families of post-quantum algorithms

Family Basic idea Typical use or status
Lattice-based cryptography Uses mathematical problems involving structured high-dimensional lattices. Includes standardised algorithms for key establishment and digital signatures.
Hash-based signatures Builds signatures from cryptographic hash functions. Useful for digital signatures; some constructions have state-management requirements.
Code-based cryptography Uses problems related to decoding error-correcting codes. An important family for public-key encryption and key establishment.
Multivariate cryptography Uses systems of multivariate polynomial equations. Has been explored for signatures and other applications; security varies by construction.
Isogeny-based cryptography Uses relationships between certain elliptic curves. Some prominent proposals have been broken, illustrating why careful cryptanalysis is essential.

Not every proposal in these families is secure. Cryptographic systems must be evaluated through public analysis, rigorous implementation and ongoing research. A new mathematical idea should never be treated as secure simply because it is described as quantum-resistant.

7.5.5 NIST's post-quantum standards

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) finalised its first major set of post-quantum cryptographic standards in 2024. Three particularly important standards are:

  • FIPS 203 — ML-KEM: A lattice-based key encapsulation mechanism, based on CRYSTALS-Kyber, intended to establish shared secret keys.
  • FIPS 204 — ML-DSA: A lattice-based digital signature standard, based on CRYSTALS-Dilithium.
  • FIPS 205 — SLH-DSA: A stateless hash-based digital signature standard, based on SPHINCS+.

A key encapsulation mechanism is used to establish a shared secret between parties. That secret can then be used with symmetric cryptography to protect communication. A digital signature, on the other hand, allows a receiver to verify that a message was signed by the holder of the corresponding private key and has not been altered.

These standards are intended to address different needs. ML-KEM is used for key establishment, while ML-DSA and SLH-DSA provide digital signatures. The correct choice depends on the protocol, performance requirements, security requirements and applicable standards.

7.5.6 Does post-quantum cryptography solve everything?

Replacing RSA with a post-quantum algorithm is not as simple as changing one line of code. Cryptographic algorithms are embedded in network protocols, certificates, hardware devices, software libraries, identity systems and long-lived infrastructure.

A migration must consider public-key sizes, signature sizes, network bandwidth, memory use, processor performance, certificate limits and compatibility with older systems. Implementations must also avoid side-channel attacks, poor randomness, insecure key storage and mistakes in protocol design.

Organisations often need a cryptographic inventory: a record of which algorithms they use, where they are used, who operates them and how they can be replaced. Systems with long confidentiality requirements or difficult upgrade paths deserve particular attention.

7.5.7 What about symmetric encryption?

Shor's algorithm does not directly provide an efficient method for recovering every symmetric key. Grover's algorithm gives a quadratic speedup for idealised unstructured key search.

For example, a generic exhaustive search over a key space of size 2ⁿ takes on the order of 2ⁿ classical trials. In the ideal query model, Grover's algorithm can reduce the number of oracle queries to approximately 2^(n/2). This is still exponential in n, unlike the polynomial-time quantum factoring algorithm.

This is one reason larger symmetric key sizes can provide additional protection against quantum search. However, actual security depends on the algorithm, mode of operation, implementation and security requirements. Organisations should follow current cryptographic guidance rather than make changes based only on a simplified mathematical comparison.

7.5.8 A practical migration strategy

Preparing for quantum threats is a long-term engineering exercise. A sensible approach includes the following steps.

  1. Identify vulnerable cryptography. Find where RSA, traditional Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve algorithms are used for key establishment or signatures.
  2. Prioritise sensitive information. Consider the consequences if stored or intercepted data were decrypted years later.
  3. Review standards and vendor roadmaps. Use established standards and verify that libraries and platforms support the required algorithms.
  4. Test compatibility. Evaluate handshake sizes, certificates, latency, bandwidth, memory use and performance.
  5. Plan cryptographic agility. Design systems so that algorithms and keys can be replaced without rebuilding the entire application.
  6. Deploy and monitor carefully. Validate interoperability, maintain secure key management and track future standards and security advisories.

7.5.9 The broader lesson

Shor's algorithm is important for more than the specific problem of factoring integers. It demonstrates that a change in the model of computation can alter which mathematical problems are practical to solve.

For decades, cryptographic systems were designed around the capabilities of classical computers. Quantum computing requires us to revisit some of those assumptions. The response is not to abandon digital security, but to build cryptographic systems that remain secure under stronger computational models.

Chapter Summary

Shor's algorithm connects three ideas: integer factorisation, periodicity in modular arithmetic, and the Quantum Fourier Transform.

  • Factoring: Given N, find non-trivial integers whose product is N.
  • RSA: Uses a public modulus formed from secret prime factors. Recovering those factors allows an attacker to derive the private key.
  • Order finding: Find the smallest positive r for which aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N), assuming gcd(a, N) = 1.
  • Quantum Fourier Transform: Helps turn the periodic structure of modular exponentiation into measurement outcomes that reveal information about r.
  • Classical post-processing: Uses continued fractions and greatest common divisors to recover and verify candidate factors.
  • Resource requirements: Practical cryptographic attacks require large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computers, not merely a small quantum processor.
  • Post-quantum cryptography: Replaces vulnerable public-key mechanisms with carefully evaluated alternatives designed to resist quantum attacks.
