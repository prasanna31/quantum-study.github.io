Chapter 25 · Quantum Machine Learning

Classical Simulability and Dequantization

Understanding when quantum computers offer a genuine computational advantage, when classical computers can reproduce their results, and how to compare both fairly.

Introduction: Does using a quantum computer automatically make a task faster?

Imagine that two students are asked to solve the same difficult mathematical problem. One student uses a powerful computer based on quantum physics, while the other uses an ordinary computer. If the first student finishes earlier, we might conclude that quantum computing is better for the problem.

However, there is an important question we must ask first: Did the classical student have access to the best possible classical algorithm?

Perhaps the quantum student used a clever method, while the classical student used an unnecessarily slow one. If we improve the classical algorithm, both students might finish in nearly the same amount of time.

This is the central problem studied in classical simulability and dequantization. We want to understand whether a quantum algorithm provides a real computational advantage or whether an ordinary computer can reproduce its important results efficiently.

Classical simulability asks whether an ordinary classical computer can reproduce the behaviour or relevant outputs of a quantum computation without performing the same quantum computation on quantum hardware.
Dequantization refers to finding a classical algorithm that reproduces the useful computational result of a quantum algorithm, often removing the apparent quantum advantage under specified assumptions.

These ideas are especially important in quantum machine learning (QML). A proposed QML algorithm might claim to classify data, estimate a property, solve a linear system or train a model much faster than classical machine learning. Before accepting that claim, researchers must investigate whether a classical algorithm can achieve the same result just as efficiently.

25.1 When can classical computers efficiently simulate quantum circuits?

25.1.1 What does it mean to simulate a quantum circuit?

A quantum circuit is a sequence of operations applied to qubits. A qubit is a quantum system that can be in a superposition of the states represented by 0 and 1. Quantum gates change the state of these qubits, and measurements produce classical outcomes.

Consider a quantum circuit containing three qubits. The state of the system can be written as

|ψ⟩ = α₀₀₀|000⟩ + α₀₀₁|001⟩ + ... + α₁₁₁|111⟩

There are eight computational basis states for three qubits. Each coefficient, such as α₀₀₀, is a complex number describing the amplitude associated with one basis state. The squared magnitude of an amplitude gives the probability of observing that basis state when measured in the computational basis.

For n qubits, the number of basis states is 2n. Therefore, a general pure quantum state requires 2n complex amplitudes to represent it explicitly.

|ψ⟩ = Σx ∈ {0,1}n αx|x⟩
Σx |αx|² = 1

The second expression is the normalisation condition. It ensures that the probabilities of all possible measurement outcomes add up to one.

A straightforward classical simulator can store the amplitudes and update them after every gate. For a small circuit, this approach works well. For a large circuit, however, storing every amplitude can become impractical.

The key point is that the number of amplitudes grows exponentially with the number of qubits. Increasing the system from 30 to 60 qubits multiplies the number of amplitudes by 230, which is more than one billion.

Important distinction: a quantum computer does not give us a readable list of all its amplitudes simply because those amplitudes exist. Measurement provides sampled classical outcomes. The difficulty of classical simulation depends on what information we want to reproduce and on the structure of the circuit.

25.1.2 What does efficient simulation mean?

In computer science, an algorithm is commonly called efficient when its resource requirements grow polynomially with the size of the input. For example, a running time such as n² or n³ is polynomial.

Polynomial time: O(n²), O(n³), O(nk)
Exponential time: O(2n)

These expressions describe how the required work grows as the input becomes larger. They do not directly tell us the exact number of seconds on a particular machine.

Suppose the number of qubits is n. If a classical simulator requires a number of operations proportional to 2n, it may become infeasible as n increases. If it requires only polynomially many operations in n and achieves the required accuracy, it may remain practical for much larger instances.

Strictly speaking, efficient simulation also depends on the desired output. Computing one amplitude, estimating an expectation value, sampling measurement outcomes and reproducing an entire output distribution are different tasks. An algorithm may solve one of these tasks efficiently without solving the others efficiently.

25.1.3 Four different meanings of simulation

Simulation task What the classical computer must do Why it matters
State simulation Represent or calculate the quantum state. A full state vector may require exponentially many amplitudes.
Amplitude calculation Calculate the amplitude associated with a specified outcome. Some circuits permit efficient calculation of selected amplitudes even when full-state simulation is difficult.
Sampling Generate outcomes with approximately the same probabilities as quantum measurements. A model can sometimes be sampled efficiently without explicitly storing its full state.
Observable estimation Estimate a specified property, such as an average measurement value. The desired property may be much easier to estimate than the complete quantum state is to reconstruct.

This distinction is crucial in QML. A model might use thousands of parameters in a quantum circuit, but its final prediction could depend on only a few expectation values. If those expectation values can be calculated classically without simulating the entire circuit, the claimed quantum advantage may disappear for that task.

25.1.4 Why some quantum circuits are easy to simulate

The size of a circuit is not the only factor that matters. Its structure can be even more important.

A circuit containing many qubits may still be easy to simulate if its gates preserve a mathematical structure that a classical algorithm can exploit. Conversely, a circuit with fewer qubits may be difficult to simulate if its operations generate complicated entanglement and do not admit a useful compact representation.

Consider two circuits with the same number of qubits. The first contains mostly independent operations on individual qubits. The second repeatedly entangles many qubits and spreads correlations across the system. The second circuit may be significantly harder for a classical simulator, even though both circuits have the same number of qubits.

This leads to an important lesson: counting qubits alone is not enough to determine whether a quantum circuit is difficult to simulate classically.

25.1.5 Classical simulability does not mean quantum computers are useless

Suppose researchers discover an efficient classical algorithm for a particular family of quantum circuits. This result does not imply that every quantum circuit can be simulated efficiently. It also does not imply that quantum computers provide no advantage for other tasks.

The conclusion applies to the circuit family, computational task and assumptions covered by the classical algorithm. A different circuit family may have different properties.

Section takeaway: Classical simulation is not simply a question of whether the number of qubits is large. It is a question of whether the required output can be calculated with acceptable time, memory and accuracy by exploiting the structure of the computation.

25.2 Tensor networks and Clifford simulation

Two important examples of classical simulation techniques are tensor networks and stabiliser-based simulation of Clifford circuits. They exploit different mathematical structures, but they share the same idea: do not represent or calculate more information than the computation actually requires.

25.2.1 Understanding tensor networks

A tensor is a mathematical object that generalises familiar objects such as numbers, vectors and matrices. A number has no indices, a vector has one index, a matrix has two indices, and a higher-order tensor can have three or more indices.

For example, a vector might contain the values (2, 4, 6), while a matrix might contain several rows and columns of values. A tensor can be used to represent relationships among several indices at once.

Quantum circuits can be represented as collections of tensors. Each quantum gate is converted into a tensor, and the connections between gates describe how their indices are combined. The resulting calculation is called a tensor network contraction.

A simple analogy

Imagine a large jigsaw puzzle. Solving the entire puzzle at once may be difficult. However, if it can be divided into small connected sections, we can solve those sections and combine their results.

A tensor network works in a somewhat similar way. It breaks a large mathematical expression into smaller components and combines them in a carefully chosen order. The analogy is not exact, because tensor contraction is a numerical operation rather than a puzzle-solving process.

25.2.2 Why the contraction order matters

Suppose a calculation contains many connected tensors. We can combine the first two tensors, then combine their result with a third tensor. Alternatively, we can combine another pair first.

These choices may produce the same final mathematical result, but their intermediate calculations can have very different sizes. One order may create a small intermediate tensor, while another may create an enormous one.

The computational cost depends heavily on the sizes of the intermediate tensors. In a quantum circuit, these sizes are closely related to the network structure and the amount of correlation represented across its connections.

One useful structural quantity is the treewidth of an associated graph. Informally, treewidth measures how closely a graph can be decomposed into small, tree-like connected pieces. For suitable tensor-network representations, low treewidth can enable efficient contraction.

A related quantity is bond dimension, which measures the number of values carried by a connection between tensor components. Large bond dimensions can increase memory and computational requirements.

Larger intermediate tensors → more memory and computation
Suitable low-complexity structure → potentially efficient simulation

The word "potentially" is important. Low treewidth, modest bond dimensions and suitable contraction strategies can make simulation efficient, but the precise cost also depends on the network, the requested output and the required numerical accuracy.

25.2.3 Matrix product states

One well-known tensor-network representation is the matrix product state (MPS). It expresses a quantum state using a sequence of smaller tensors rather than one enormous state vector.

For a system of n qubits, the MPS representation connects tensors associated with the individual qubits. The dimensions of the internal connections are called bond dimensions. If these dimensions remain small, the state can often be stored and manipulated efficiently.

MPS methods are particularly useful for certain one-dimensional systems and circuits whose entanglement remains limited across suitable partitions of the qubits.

Entanglement is a quantum relationship in which the state of a combined system cannot be described as a simple product of independent states of its parts. Strong entanglement across a partition can require larger bond dimensions for an accurate MPS representation.

Therefore, an MPS simulator may work extremely well for one circuit and poorly for another. A large number of qubits does not automatically make the first circuit difficult, and a smaller number does not guarantee that the second will be easy.

25.2.4 Exact simulation versus approximate simulation

Tensor-network methods can sometimes calculate results exactly, up to numerical precision, but they are also often used for approximate simulation.

An approximate simulator may discard small contributions, limit bond dimensions or truncate intermediate calculations. This reduces the cost, but it introduces an approximation error that must be measured.

If the approximation error is small for the particular observable or prediction being studied, the simulator may still be useful. If the discarded information strongly affects the result, the approximation may be unreliable.

Important: An approximate classical simulation should not be described as an exact reproduction of the quantum circuit. Researchers must report the approximation method, its accuracy and the conditions under which it works.

25.2.5 What are Clifford gates?

Clifford gates form a special family of quantum gates that transform Pauli operators into other Pauli operators under conjugation.

To understand this statement, we first need to know about Pauli operators. The three familiar single-qubit Pauli matrices are X, Y and Z. The X operator exchanges the computational basis states, while Z changes the relative phase of the state |1⟩. The Y operator combines related changes in amplitude and phase.

These operators, together with identity operations and appropriate phase factors, form the Pauli group. Clifford gates preserve the structure of this group under conjugation.

Common Clifford gates include the Hadamard gate H, the phase gate S and the controlled-NOT gate CNOT.

Gate Main action Why it is useful
Hadamard (H) Interchanges the computational and phase-related descriptions of a qubit. Creates superpositions and changes measurement basis.
Phase (S) Applies a relative phase to the |1⟩ component. Provides a basic phase operation within the Clifford family.
CNOT Flips a target qubit when the control qubit is 1. Creates entanglement and enables multi-qubit Clifford operations.

25.2.6 Why Clifford circuits can be simulated efficiently

The key result is the Gottesman–Knill theorem. It establishes that circuits built from Clifford operations, stabiliser states and computational-basis measurements can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer under the standard circuit model.

This is surprising because Clifford circuits can create superposition and entanglement. Entanglement alone, therefore, is not enough to guarantee that a circuit is difficult to simulate classically.

Instead of storing every amplitude, a stabiliser simulator tracks a compact description of the state through a set of stabiliser generators. These generators provide enough information to update the state under Clifford operations and calculate measurement statistics efficiently.

This illustrates a broader principle: a quantum state may have an exponentially large state-vector representation while still possessing a compact mathematical description.

25.2.7 Why non-Clifford gates matter

Clifford gates alone do not provide the full computational power associated with universal quantum computation. Universal gate sets require additional operations, commonly including a non-Clifford gate such as the T gate.

Adding non-Clifford gates can make classical simulation more difficult, but it does not automatically make every circuit hard to simulate. The cost may depend on how many non-Clifford operations are present and how they are arranged.

Specialised algorithms can simulate some circuits containing non-Clifford gates efficiently, while other methods become more expensive as the non-Clifford resources increase. Consequently, a circuit with many Clifford gates and a small number of non-Clifford gates may remain amenable to useful classical simulation.

Method Structure it exploits Main limitation
State-vector simulation Direct numerical representation of the complete pure state. Memory grows exponentially with qubit count.
Tensor networks Connectivity, contraction order and limited complexity across partitions. Large intermediate tensors or bond dimensions can become expensive.
Stabiliser simulation Clifford operations and stabiliser-state structure. General non-Clifford circuits are not covered by the basic efficient-simulation result.
Hybrid specialised simulation Combinations of exploitable circuit properties. Efficiency depends on the particular circuit and output task.
Section takeaway: Tensor networks exploit the structure of a quantum state and circuit. Clifford simulation exploits algebraic structure in a special family of quantum operations. Both demonstrate why quantum circuits must be examined individually rather than judged by qubit count alone.

25.3 Dequantization of QML algorithms

25.3.1 What problem does dequantization solve?

A QML algorithm uses quantum operations as part of a machine learning task. It might encode classical data into quantum states, apply a parameterised circuit, measure an observable and use the result to make a prediction.

Researchers may argue that this procedure is faster than a classical machine learning method. Dequantization asks whether the same useful result can be obtained by a classical algorithm using a more careful representation or a better mathematical method.

A dequantized algorithm does not need to copy every physical step of the quantum computation. It needs to reproduce the relevant computational outcome under comparable assumptions.

Quantum algorithm
→
Identify the useful output
→
Find exploitable structure
→
Construct a classical method

The important question is not whether the classical algorithm looks like the quantum algorithm. The important question is whether it solves the same problem with comparable accuracy and resource requirements.

25.3.2 The role of data loading

Many QML proposals begin with a classical dataset. Suppose a dataset contains a vector of numerical features. A quantum algorithm may encode those features into the amplitudes of a quantum state.

For a normalised vector x, amplitude encoding has the form

|x⟩ = Σi=0N−1 xi|i⟩
Σi |xi|² = 1

Since N basis states can be represented using approximately log₂(N) qubits, amplitude encoding appears very compact. However, preparing that state from arbitrary classical data may itself require substantial work.

The existence of a compact quantum representation does not guarantee that the representation can be prepared efficiently. If the input must be loaded repeatedly, state preparation costs may dominate the total running time.

Some proposed speedups assume a special data-loading mechanism, such as quantum random access memory (QRAM), or assume that the required quantum states are already available. Such assumptions must be stated explicitly.

Common mistake: Counting only the operations performed after data loading can make a QML algorithm appear faster than it really is. A fair comparison includes the cost of obtaining and preparing the input in both approaches.

25.3.3 Example: quantum linear algebra for machine learning

Linear algebra is central to machine learning. Linear regression, least-squares fitting, optimisation and several dimensionality-reduction methods all involve matrices and vectors.

Consider a linear system

A x = b

Here, A is a matrix, b is a known vector and x is the unknown solution. A quantum linear-system algorithm aims to prepare a quantum state related to the solution x, rather than necessarily printing every entry of x.

Under suitable assumptions, quantum linear-system methods can offer advantages for particular tasks. The assumptions can include favourable matrix conditioning, efficient state preparation, suitable access to the matrix and a requirement to estimate only selected properties of the solution.

The final point is especially important. If a user needs every component of x as an ordinary list, the output itself may contain N values. Writing down all those values already requires work proportional to N.

A quantum algorithm may instead be useful when the goal is to estimate a particular quantity related to x, and that quantity can be extracted without reconstructing the full vector.

25.3.4 How classical sampling can reproduce quantum-inspired results

A common feature of some quantum algorithms is their use of sampling. A quantum device prepares a state and measurements provide samples from a probability distribution.

Classical algorithms also use sampling. If the relevant distribution has exploitable structure, a classical method may sample from it without constructing the complete quantum state.

For example, consider a learning task that depends on estimating a collection of inner products or matrix-related quantities. A quantum algorithm may access those quantities through carefully prepared states and measurements. A classical algorithm may instead use random sampling, low-rank approximations or specialised data structures.

Whether this works depends on the problem. Sampling does not automatically remove quantum advantage. It provides a possible route for discovering a more efficient classical algorithm.

25.3.5 Dequantization and low-rank structure

Many datasets contain redundancy. For example, thousands of features might be strongly related to a much smaller number of underlying patterns. In such cases, a matrix can sometimes be approximated by a low-rank matrix.

A low-rank approximation represents the important information in a large matrix using a smaller number of components. If the approximation is accurate enough for the task, the classical algorithm may avoid processing every matrix entry in the same way.

This is relevant to dequantization because some quantum linear-algebra algorithms exploit efficient access to quantum states or particular forms of matrix access. Researchers have shown that, for certain algorithms and input assumptions, classical sampling methods can reproduce similar computational results.

Such work is often called quantum-inspired classical algorithms. They are inspired by the mathematical structure of quantum algorithms but run on ordinary computers.

Example: finding the important features

Imagine a dataset containing 10,000 measurements per example. A quantum algorithm may attempt to process a compressed representation of these measurements. A classical algorithm might discover that most of the useful variation can be represented by a small number of components.

If both algorithms can predict the target accurately, and the classical method uses fewer resources, then the proposed quantum advantage has not been demonstrated for that particular dataset and task.

This does not mean the quantum algorithm is always inferior. The result depends on whether the low-rank structure exists, how the data is accessed and what accuracy the application requires.

25.3.6 Classical data access is part of the problem

Two algorithms cannot be compared fairly if they receive fundamentally different forms of access to their inputs. One algorithm might receive a complete matrix in ordinary memory, while another receives a quantum state prepared from that matrix. Alternatively, one method may assume specialised sampling access that the other does not have.

Researchers must therefore specify the input model. Is the data stored explicitly? Is it available through queries? Must a quantum state be prepared? Is there an oracle that answers particular questions efficiently?

An oracle is an abstract computational resource that provides specified information when queried. An algorithm's complexity may look impressive if oracle calls are counted but the cost of constructing or implementing the oracle is ignored.

25.3.7 A practical test for dequantization

When examining a proposed QML algorithm, ask the following questions:

  1. What exact mathematical quantity or prediction does the algorithm produce?
  2. Does it need to reconstruct an entire quantum state, or only estimate a particular property?
  3. Can the output be calculated using classical sampling or approximation methods?
  4. Does the classical method exploit low-rank structure, sparsity, symmetry or another useful property?
  5. Are both methods given comparable access to the input data?
  6. Do both methods achieve the same accuracy and success probability?
  7. Does the proposed advantage remain after including data loading, preprocessing and output costs?
Section takeaway: Dequantization tests the complete computational claim, not merely the quantum circuit. A quantum representation can be compact, but input preparation, output extraction and classical structure may determine whether the overall method is actually faster.

25.4 Implications for claims of advantage

25.4.1 What is quantum advantage?

Quantum advantage means that a quantum computer performs a specified computational task better than the best relevant classical methods, according to a clearly defined measure. That measure might be running time, memory, query complexity, accuracy for a fixed budget or another resource.

There is no single definition that covers every application. A quantum algorithm may offer a theoretical asymptotic speedup but provide no practical benefit on currently available hardware. Another algorithm may achieve a useful practical improvement on a specialised problem without establishing a general complexity-theoretic separation.

Therefore, researchers should state exactly what kind of advantage they claim.

Type of advantage Meaning Question to ask
Theoretical advantage A mathematical analysis predicts better scaling under stated assumptions. Are the assumptions realistic, and are all relevant resources included?
Practical advantage A quantum method performs better in an actual application or experiment. Does it outperform strong classical methods at a useful scale?
Sampling advantage A quantum device samples from a specified distribution more efficiently than known classical approaches under a defined model. How strong is the evidence that classical sampling cannot match it efficiently?
Learning advantage A quantum learning procedure achieves better learning performance or resource scaling. Does the improvement remain for comparable data access, accuracy and generalisation?

25.4.2 Be careful with the word "exponential"

A paper may state that a quantum algorithm uses exponentially fewer operations than a particular classical algorithm. This statement is meaningful only if the classical algorithm used for comparison is sufficiently strong and both algorithms solve the same problem under comparable assumptions.

Suppose a quantum method has a running-time bound that grows like a polynomial in log N, while an initial classical method grows like N. It may appear that the quantum method has an exponential advantage.

However, another classical algorithm might exploit sparsity, low-rank structure, fast matrix operations or a specialised data representation. If that improved method also has favourable scaling, the original comparison no longer establishes the claimed advantage.

A quantum algorithm may still be superior, but that conclusion requires a stronger argument than comparing it against one weak baseline.

25.4.3 The output problem

One of the most important issues in quantum computing is the distinction between preparing a useful quantum state and extracting a large amount of classical information from it.

Consider a system with N numerical outputs. A quantum algorithm may prepare a state that encodes information about all N values. Yet measuring that state does not reveal every encoded value in one operation.

If the application needs all N values, reading the complete output can erase much of the expected speedup. If the application needs only a particular summary statistic, a quantum approach may have a better chance of providing an advantage.

Example: a recommendation system

Suppose a model scores one million products. One task is to print every score. Another task is to identify a small number of suitable products or estimate a particular statistic about the scores.

These tasks have different output requirements. An algorithm that helps with the second task does not automatically offer the same advantage for the first. The comparison must use the actual output required by the application.

25.4.4 Complexity theory and what it can establish

Computational complexity theory studies how the resources required to solve problems grow with input size. It helps researchers distinguish a rigorous speedup result from an empirical observation.

Some quantum advantages are established for specific computational models or restricted problem families. Other claims rely on assumptions about the difficulty of classical simulation. These assumptions may be reasonable and well motivated, but they must still be stated clearly.

Demonstrating that no efficient classical algorithm exists is often much harder than finding a fast quantum algorithm. In many cases, researchers do not have an unconditional proof that every possible classical method must be slow.

Consequently, a paper may establish that a quantum algorithm beats known classical methods or that a separation follows from a particular complexity assumption. These are useful results, but they should not be described as a proof of universal quantum superiority.

25.4.5 Why dequantization is scientifically valuable

Discovering a classical algorithm that matches a quantum method is not a failure. It improves our understanding of the problem.

First, it identifies the mathematical structure that makes the task easier than expected. Second, it helps researchers design better classical algorithms. Third, it narrows the conditions under which a quantum computer might provide a genuine advantage.

Dequantization can also reveal that a proposed QML benefit comes from a good modelling idea rather than from uniquely quantum computation. That is a useful discovery because the modelling idea may then be deployed on widely available classical hardware.

Section takeaway: A quantum advantage claim is only as strong as its problem definition, assumptions, output requirements and classical comparison. Dequantization makes these claims more precise rather than making quantum computing less important.

25.5 How to benchmark QML fairly against classical baselines

25.5.1 Why benchmarking is difficult

Benchmarking means measuring the performance of different methods on a well-defined task. In QML, it is not enough to run a quantum model and a classical model on a dataset and compare their final accuracy.

The two methods might use different amounts of training data, different preprocessing, different optimisation budgets or different access to the input. They may also be measured using different definitions of running time.

A fair benchmark must control these differences or report them clearly. Otherwise, the experiment may measure the advantages of the experimental setup rather than the advantages of the algorithm.

25.5.2 Start by defining the exact task

Before selecting models, write down what the system must accomplish. For a classification task, this may mean predicting a label from a feature vector. For regression, it may mean estimating a numerical value. For a generative model, it may mean sampling from a distribution with particular statistical properties.

Both methods must solve the same task. If the quantum model predicts a small summary of the data while the classical model must generate a much larger output, their performance numbers cannot be interpreted as a direct comparison.

25.5.3 Use strong classical baselines

A baseline is a method used as a reference point. In a scientific comparison, a baseline should be appropriate for the task and reasonably well tuned.

For a small tabular dataset, a logistic regression model, a support-vector machine, a tree-based model or a well-tuned neural network may be useful baselines, depending on the task. For a linear-algebra problem, optimised classical numerical algorithms may be more relevant than a generic neural network.

Researchers should not select a deliberately weak classical method simply because it makes the quantum model look better. Nor should they compare against a poorly tuned model and treat the result as a general conclusion about classical machine learning.

25.5.4 Compare equal conditions, not just equal model sizes

A quantum circuit with 100 trainable parameters and a classical network with 100 trainable parameters are not necessarily equally expensive. Each parameter may have a different computational cost, and the quantum model may require many repeated measurements to estimate its predictions.

Likewise, giving both models the same number of training epochs does not guarantee a fair comparison. One epoch may involve very different amounts of computation.

Researchers should report multiple resource measures wherever possible, including wall-clock time, total training cost, memory, energy if available, inference cost and hardware usage.

25.5.5 Include the complete quantum workflow

The total cost of a QML method can include several stages. Some are shared with classical methods, while others are specific to quantum hardware.

Data preparation
→
Quantum state preparation
→
Circuit execution
→
Repeated measurements
→
Classical post-processing

The end-to-end cost includes the stages required to obtain the final answer, not only the time spent executing the quantum gates.

For example, a variational quantum model may require repeated circuit executions for each training step. If its cost function is estimated using many measurement shots, the number of shots can substantially affect total training time.

Hardware queue times, calibration, communication with the device and classical optimisation may also matter in practical settings. Researchers should report these separately when they depend on the experimental environment, rather than presenting one misleading timing number.

25.5.6 Account for data loading and output extraction

Classical datasets normally begin as classical information. Converting them into quantum states can be costly. If a new quantum state must be prepared for every training example or every model evaluation, this cost can be substantial.

On the other hand, a particular application might already have quantum data produced by a physical experiment. In that setting, the data-loading comparison is different: the input may naturally arrive as a quantum state rather than as a list of classical numbers.

Therefore, the benchmark must describe the origin of the data. A QML algorithm trained on naturally available quantum data should not be evaluated using exactly the same assumptions as an algorithm that must encode a large classical dataset into quantum states.

The output cost is equally important. If the quantum algorithm estimates one expectation value, compare it against a classical method estimating the same value. If it is supposed to return an entire vector, include the cost of producing that vector.

25.5.7 Compare accuracy and statistical uncertainty

Quantum measurements are probabilistic. A measured expectation value is estimated from repeated samples, so the result has statistical uncertainty.

Suppose an observable has outcomes between -1 and 1. Repeated independent measurements can be used to estimate its expectation value. In the usual bounded-outcome setting, the number of samples required for a fixed additive accuracy generally scales inversely with the square of the target error, with additional dependence on the desired confidence.

Typical sampling dependence:
Number of samples ∝ 1 / ε²
for fixed confidence and bounded measurement outcomes

Here, ε represents the target additive estimation error. This relationship is a general sampling guideline, not a universal cost formula for every quantum observable or measurement strategy.

The practical lesson is that demanding higher accuracy can require many more measurements. A benchmark that ignores measurement uncertainty may overstate the quality or speed of a quantum prediction.

For learning tasks, report appropriate measures such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score or regression error. For probabilistic and generative tasks, use metrics suited to the distribution being learned. Include uncertainty estimates where possible and evaluate on held-out data.

25.5.8 Separate training time from inference time

Training and inference are different stages. Training adjusts model parameters using data, while inference uses the trained model to produce predictions.

A quantum model might take a long time to train but require relatively little work for a particular prediction. Alternatively, it might require many measurements for each prediction.

Classical models also have different training and inference costs. For a fair benchmark, researchers should report these stages separately and explain the intended deployment pattern.

If a model will make millions of predictions after one training run, inference efficiency may be especially important. If the model must be retrained frequently, training cost may dominate.

25.5.9 Test generalisation rather than memorisation

A model can perform well on its training examples without learning patterns that transfer to new examples. This is called overfitting.

Both quantum and classical models must be evaluated on unseen test data. The same data splits, leakage controls and evaluation rules should be used for both.

For a small dataset, results can vary substantially depending on the split. Repeating experiments across several random seeds or using a suitable cross-validation procedure can help reveal whether a reported improvement is stable.

Hyperparameter tuning also consumes resources. Researchers should account for the tuning budget rather than reporting only the final training run of one model while charging the competing method for extensive experimentation.

25.5.10 Account for hardware differences

A classical algorithm may run on a modern GPU, while the quantum method runs on a remote quantum processor. Comparing the two can still be useful, but the hardware conditions must be described.

Current quantum devices may be affected by gate errors, limited connectivity, noise and device-specific execution overheads. A simulator running on a classical computer can help study a circuit, but it does not demonstrate that the circuit has a practical advantage on quantum hardware.

Similarly, an experimental quantum result that beats one particular classical implementation does not automatically establish that it beats all possible classical implementations.

25.5.11 A practical benchmarking checklist

  • Define the exact problem and the output that both algorithms must produce.
  • Use the same dataset, data splits and evaluation criteria.
  • Include strong, task-appropriate classical baselines.
  • Give each method a reasonable and transparently reported tuning budget.
  • Report training and inference costs separately.
  • Include state preparation, data loading, measurements and post-processing where relevant.
  • Report accuracy, statistical uncertainty and the cost of reaching the required accuracy.
  • Use the same assumptions about input access and output requirements.
  • State the hardware, software, circuit depth and other relevant implementation details.
  • Test on unseen data and repeat experiments where appropriate.
  • Distinguish theoretical complexity claims from measured hardware performance.
  • Release code, configurations and experimental details where possible so that others can reproduce the result.
Section takeaway: A fair QML benchmark measures the cost of solving the whole problem at comparable accuracy. It does not compare only gate counts, parameter counts or isolated execution times.

Putting the ideas together

Classical simulability, dequantization and benchmarking are three connected parts of the same scientific question: what can a quantum computer do that a classical computer cannot do as efficiently under clearly stated conditions?

Classical simulability begins by asking whether the behaviour of a quantum circuit can be reproduced using an ordinary computer. Tensor networks and stabiliser methods demonstrate that special circuit structures can make this possible, even when a direct state-vector calculation would be prohibitively expensive.

Dequantization extends this investigation to quantum algorithms for machine learning. It asks whether the useful output of a QML method can be calculated by a classical algorithm using techniques such as sampling, low-rank approximation or efficient data structures.

Benchmarking then checks the practical claim. Both approaches must solve the same task, receive comparable input access, meet the same accuracy requirements and account for the resources needed to produce the final answer.

Can we reproduce the circuit?

Dequantization

Fair benchmarking

The five central lessons

  1. More qubits do not automatically mean a hard simulation. Circuit structure, entanglement, algebraic properties and the required output all affect simulation difficulty.
  2. A compact quantum representation is not the same as a cheap computation. Preparing the state and extracting the desired information can be expensive.
  3. Quantum-inspired classical algorithms deserve serious consideration. They can reveal that a problem thought to require quantum computation has a more efficient classical solution under certain assumptions.
  4. A dequantized algorithm does not disprove all quantum advantage. It addresses a particular problem, algorithm family and input model.
  5. Quantum advantage must be demonstrated through a fair comparison. Strong baselines, complete resource accounting, equivalent accuracy and reproducible experiments are essential.

Final perspective: Quantum computing is not about assuming that every quantum algorithm must be better than its classical counterpart. It is about discovering where quantum mechanics enables a real computational benefit. Classical simulation, dequantization and fair benchmarking help researchers identify that boundary with mathematical care and experimental honesty.
