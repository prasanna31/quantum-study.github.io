Chapter 26: Advanced Topics and Emerging Directions

Quantum machine learning brings together three important fields: quantum physics, computer science and machine learning. The earlier chapters introduced the basic ideas behind quantum data, quantum circuits, variational algorithms, quantum neural networks and quantum learning theory. In this chapter, we move towards the questions that researchers are exploring today: Can quantum computers help us understand molecules? Can ideas from modern AI be adapted to quantum systems? How will machine learning change when quantum computers become more reliable? Can quantum methods support privacy, optimisation and cryptography?

These questions do not all have the same answer. Some applications already have useful mathematical foundations, while others remain experimental research directions. Understanding the difference is essential. Quantum computing is not automatically better than classical computing; its value depends on the problem, the algorithm, the hardware and the resources required to obtain a useful answer.

26.1 Quantum Machine Learning for Physics and Chemistry

26.1.1 Why physics and chemistry are natural areas for quantum computing

Everything around us is made of matter, and matter behaves according to the laws of physics. Electrons occupy atomic orbitals, atoms form chemical bonds, and molecules change their behaviour depending on their structure and environment. At very small scales, these phenomena are governed by quantum mechanics.

A classical computer can calculate many properties of molecules with impressive accuracy. However, accurately representing the full quantum state of a large interacting system can become difficult. The challenge comes partly from the fact that quantum systems can exhibit superposition and entanglement, which create correlations that are difficult to represent compactly using ordinary classical data structures.

Quantum computers offer a different way to represent and manipulate such states. Rather than always trying to imitate a quantum system using classical calculations, a quantum computer can use quantum states and quantum operations directly.

Central idea: Quantum computers are especially interesting for studying quantum systems because the mathematical language used to describe the computer is closely related to the language used to describe the physical system being studied.

This does not mean that every chemistry problem requires a quantum computer. Many chemical calculations can be handled effectively by classical methods. The opportunity is to find problems where quantum methods can provide more accurate results, reduce computational costs or make calculations possible that would otherwise be impractical.

26.1.2 The role of machine learning

A quantum simulation may produce valuable results, but running a detailed simulation for every possible molecule, atomic arrangement or experimental condition can be expensive. Machine learning offers a way to learn patterns from previously calculated or measured examples.

Suppose researchers calculate the energies of thousands of molecular structures. They can use those results to train a model that predicts the energy of a new structure. Once trained, the model may make predictions much faster than repeating the original expensive calculation every time.

The same general idea can be used with data produced by quantum experiments. A quantum device can prepare states, perform measurements and generate results. A classical or quantum learning model can then learn relationships between experimental settings and observed outcomes.

This gives rise to several different approaches:

  • Classical machine learning for quantum science: A classical model predicts molecular properties or helps analyse physical experiments.
  • Quantum machine learning for scientific data: A quantum circuit or quantum learning model processes information obtained from a scientific problem.
  • Quantum-assisted machine learning: A quantum computer performs a selected calculation while a classical computer handles data preparation, optimisation and interpretation.

These approaches are related, but they are not interchangeable. A machine-learning model used in chemistry does not automatically become a quantum machine-learning model merely because it predicts quantum-mechanical properties.

26.1.3 Variational quantum eigensolvers and molecular energy

One important application is estimating the lowest energy of a molecule. In quantum mechanics, the energy levels of a system are associated with its Hamiltonian, an operator that describes the system's energy.

For a molecule, finding the ground-state energy helps us understand its stability and chemical behaviour. The Variational Quantum Eigensolver, or VQE, is a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm designed to estimate such energies.

  1. A classical program chooses parameters for a quantum circuit.
  2. The quantum circuit prepares a trial state representing a possible electronic configuration.
  3. Measurements estimate the expected energy of that state.
  4. A classical optimiser adjusts the parameters to seek a lower energy.
  5. The process repeats until a satisfactory result is obtained or the computational budget is exhausted.

The important connection to machine learning is that the circuit contains trainable parameters, measurements provide an objective value, and an optimiser searches for better parameters. This resembles the training of a machine-learning model, although the underlying objective and physical meaning are different.

E(θ) = ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩

Here, H is the molecular Hamiltonian, and ψ(θ) is the quantum state prepared by a circuit with parameters θ. The goal is to find parameters that make the expected energy as low as possible.

Machine-learning techniques can help choose circuit structures, initialise parameters, estimate useful configurations and reduce the number of expensive measurements. However, optimising a circuit can be difficult, especially when the circuit is deep or its gradients become very small.

26.1.4 Learning potential energy surfaces

Molecules are not fixed objects. Their atoms move, vibrate and interact. To understand chemical reactions or molecular motion, researchers need to know how the energy changes when the positions of the atoms change.

A potential energy surface describes the relationship between molecular geometry and energy. If the positions of all atoms are represented by a coordinate vector R, the energy can be written as E(R).

Molecular geometry R → Energy E(R) → Forces F(R)

Forces can be obtained from how the energy changes with the atomic coordinates. A model that predicts both energy and reliable forces can help simulate molecular motion, study reaction pathways and explore candidate materials.

Quantum calculations or experiments may provide high-quality training data. Machine learning then learns an approximation to the energy surface. Quantum machine learning is one possible modelling approach, but classical neural networks and specialised geometric models are also important and often highly competitive.

26.1.5 Applications in materials science and physics

Beyond chemistry, researchers investigate quantum methods for analysing condensed-matter systems, magnetic materials, phase transitions and quantum many-body phenomena. In these problems, the objective may be to identify a phase of matter, estimate an observable or infer properties of a system from experimental data.

Consider an experiment in which a physical system is measured under different temperatures, magnetic fields or control settings. A learning model may help classify the resulting states or predict how the system responds to a new setting. Quantum circuits may be used as part of the model or as a component of the simulation.

Example: Searching for a better battery material

Researchers may want to identify a material that conducts ions efficiently and remains stable over many charging cycles. A practical workflow could combine classical screening, quantum calculations for selected candidates, machine-learning predictions and laboratory testing. A quantum computer might eventually improve particular calculations, but it would be only one component of the overall research process.

26.1.6 What remains difficult?

Quantum scientific applications face several challenges. Preparing the correct state can be difficult. Measurements are noisy, and estimating an expectation value accurately may require many circuit executions. Molecular calculations may also demand more qubits, better connectivity and deeper circuits than current devices can reliably support.

Another challenge is data loading. If a large classical dataset must be encoded into a quantum state, the preparation cost may remove the benefit of the quantum computation. The cost of transferring information into and out of the quantum device must therefore be included when comparing algorithms.

The long-term goal is not to replace all classical chemistry or physics software. It is to develop a scientific computing toolkit in which classical and quantum methods each perform the tasks for which they are best suited.

26.2 Quantum Transformers and Large-Model Ideas

26.2.1 Why transformers matter

Transformers have become central to modern artificial intelligence. They are used in language models, image systems, speech processing and many other applications. Their success comes partly from the attention mechanism, which allows a model to decide which parts of an input are important when processing another part.

Consider the sentence, "The student placed the book on the table because it was stable." To interpret the word "it", a language model must consider the surrounding context. Attention provides a learned way to compare representations of different tokens and combine information from relevant positions.

A standard transformer is a classical neural network. Its attention operations, embeddings and feed-forward layers are normally implemented using classical hardware, even when the model is extremely large.

Quantum transformers explore whether some of these ideas can be combined with quantum circuits, quantum states or quantum information-processing techniques.

26.2.2 Understanding classical attention first

Before discussing quantum versions, it is useful to understand the main parts of classical attention. Each token is converted into a numerical representation. Learned transformations produce three vectors: a query, a key and a value.

  • Query: Represents the information a token is looking for.
  • Key: Represents the information a token offers for comparison with other tokens.
  • Value: Contains the information that can be combined with information from other tokens.

The model compares queries with keys to calculate attention scores. A normalisation operation converts these scores into weights, and the weights determine how the values are combined.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKT / √dk)V

Here, Q, K and V are matrices containing query, key and value vectors, while dk is the key-vector dimension. The division by its square root helps keep the scores at a useful numerical scale.

Attention does not simply select one token and ignore everything else. It can combine information from several tokens with different weights. Multiple attention heads allow the model to learn different kinds of relationships in parallel.

26.2.3 What makes a transformer quantum?

The term "quantum transformer" does not refer to one universally accepted architecture. It describes a family of research ideas that incorporate quantum processing into parts of a transformer-like system.

For example, a quantum circuit could be used to transform an input representation into a quantum state. Measurements could then provide scores or features used by a classical attention layer. Alternatively, a model could use quantum circuits to represent certain interactions among features.

One possible hybrid design follows this pattern:

  1. A classical tokenizer divides the input into tokens.
  2. Classical embeddings convert tokens into numerical vectors.
  3. A quantum circuit encodes selected features and processes them.
  4. Measurements produce classical values that feed into an attention or prediction layer.
  5. A classical optimiser updates the trainable parameters during training.

Such a model is a hybrid quantum-classical model. It does not imply that the entire transformer runs on a quantum computer.

Important distinction: Putting a quantum circuit inside a transformer does not automatically make the model faster or more capable. The circuit must provide a measurable benefit that outweighs its execution, measurement and data-transfer costs.

26.2.4 Quantum attention

Researchers can investigate several ways of introducing quantum ideas into attention. One approach is to encode token features into quantum states and use measurements to estimate similarities between those states. The estimated similarities could influence attention weights.

Another approach uses parameterised quantum circuits as feature maps. These circuits transform inputs into representations whose measured properties may be useful for classification or prediction. The resulting features can be combined with ordinary attention mechanisms.

A third possibility is to study whether quantum algorithms can accelerate particular linear algebra, sampling or kernel calculations associated with attention. Such proposals must be evaluated carefully because a speedup for one mathematical subroutine does not necessarily translate into a faster complete model.

Quantum measurement introduces a particular difficulty. The attention mechanism needs numerical scores, but a quantum device generally produces measurement outcomes rather than revealing every property of its state at once. Estimating accurate scores may require repeated circuit executions.

26.2.5 From quantum transformers to large models

Large language models are trained on enormous datasets using extensive computational resources. Their performance depends on the model architecture, data quality, optimisation method, available compute and the way they are evaluated.

Bringing quantum circuits into large models raises several questions. How should classical token embeddings be encoded into qubits? How many qubits are needed? Can the circuit be trained reliably? How much information is lost during measurement? Can the model be scaled without making the quantum portion too expensive?

The data-loading problem is especially important. A classical token may be represented by hundreds or thousands of numerical values. Encoding all those values into a quantum state is not necessarily free. An algorithm that assumes instant access to a quantum representation of a large vector may overlook a substantial part of the actual computational cost.

26.2.6 Quantum models and generative AI

Generative models learn patterns in data and use those patterns to produce new samples. A language model generates tokens, while an image model may generate pixels or latent representations. Quantum generative models explore whether quantum circuits can learn and sample useful probability distributions.

In principle, a hybrid generative system could use a quantum circuit to generate features or samples while classical neural networks handle token processing, large-scale memory and output generation. This is a research possibility rather than evidence that quantum models can currently replace large classical foundation models.

Research caution: A small quantum model performing well on a carefully selected dataset does not establish an advantage over a large language model. Fair comparisons require strong classical baselines, comparable resources, appropriate datasets and measurements of end-to-end cost.

26.2.7 What would count as progress?

Meaningful progress would include a reproducible improvement in accuracy, training cost, sampling quality or another useful measure on a well-defined task. Researchers would also need to explain whether the benefit comes from quantum processing, a different architecture, additional resources or an unfair comparison.

For the foreseeable future, hybrid experiments and carefully selected subproblems are more realistic research directions than replacing every component of a large AI system with quantum hardware.

26.3 Equivariant and Geometric Quantum Machine Learning

26.3.1 Why geometry matters in machine learning

Many machine-learning problems contain more structure than a simple list of unrelated numbers. Images contain spatial relationships. Molecules contain atoms and bonds. A robot has positions and orientations. A physical system may obey laws that remain unchanged when the entire experiment is rotated or translated.

A model that ignores this structure may need a large amount of training data to learn relationships that could have been built into its design from the beginning.

Geometric machine learning studies how to build models that respect the structure of the data. Examples include graph neural networks for molecules, convolutional neural networks for images and specialised models for three-dimensional objects.

26.3.2 Invariance and equivariance

Two related ideas are invariance and equivariance. They describe how a model should respond when its input is transformed.

An invariant prediction remains unchanged under a transformation. For example, the total mass of a molecule does not change when the molecule is rotated in space.

An equivariant prediction changes in a predictable way. For example, if a molecule is rotated, its predicted force vectors should rotate by the same amount.

Invariance: f(Tx) = f(x)

Equivariance: f(Tx) = T'f(x)

In these expressions, x is the input, T transforms the input, and T' describes the corresponding transformation of the output. For an invariant model, the output is unchanged. For an equivariant model, the output transforms consistently with the input.

Example: A molecule viewed from a different direction

Imagine taking two photographs of the same molecule, one before rotating the model and one after. Its total energy should not depend on which direction the camera faces. However, the directions of its forces should change with the rotation. A model that respects these relationships can learn the underlying chemistry more efficiently than one that must discover them from examples alone.

26.3.3 Symmetry and group theory

Mathematicians describe many transformations using groups. A group is a collection of operations that can be combined according to specific rules. Rotations, translations and permutations provide important examples of transformations relevant to physical and geometric problems.

Symmetry is useful because it tells us which aspects of a problem change and which remain the same. If a physical law is symmetric under a particular transformation, a learning model can be designed to respect that symmetry.

In quantum mechanics, symmetries also influence the structure of quantum states and operators. They are connected to conserved quantities, allowed transitions and the way a physical system responds to transformations.

26.3.4 What is geometric quantum machine learning?

Geometric quantum machine learning combines geometric structure with quantum representations and quantum computation. The aim is to design quantum models that respect important symmetries of the problem rather than treating every input as an unrelated collection of numbers.

A quantum circuit can be designed to behave consistently under a chosen family of transformations. One way to achieve this is to use gates and circuit structures that respect a symmetry. Another is to transform the input, apply a shared model and combine the results in a controlled manner.

Such designs may be useful when the data comes from physical systems, molecules, particle interactions or spatial configurations. The model can incorporate known scientific relationships into its architecture.

26.3.5 Symmetry-aware quantum circuits

Suppose a physical system has a symmetry represented by an operation U. A quantum circuit may be designed so that its transformation respects that symmetry. In some settings, the circuit may commute with the symmetry operation.

[V, U] = VU - UV = 0

Here, V represents a circuit operation and U represents a symmetry operation. The commutator is zero when applying the two operations in either order gives the same result.

This is one possible way of expressing symmetry compatibility. It is not a universal requirement for every equivariant model: more general architectures may transform outputs according to a representation of the symmetry group rather than simply commuting with every transformation.

The benefit of respecting symmetry is that the model does not have to learn the same physical relationship independently for every equivalent orientation or configuration. This can improve data efficiency and make predictions more consistent.

26.3.6 Graphs, molecules and quantum learning

Molecules can be represented as graphs. Atoms become nodes, bonds become edges, and node or edge features describe chemical properties. Graph-based learning methods are useful because changing the order in which the atoms are listed should not change the identity of the molecule.

This property is related to permutation symmetry. A model should produce consistent results if the same atoms are presented in a different order, provided their relationships remain unchanged.

Quantum machine-learning models can use graph information when constructing circuits, encoding features or defining interactions. The challenge is to create a quantum representation that respects the relevant graph structure without introducing excessive circuit depth or difficult training problems.

26.3.7 Why equivariance is not automatically a quantum advantage

Symmetry-aware architecture can improve a model, but the benefit does not necessarily come from quantum computation. Classical geometric neural networks already exploit many forms of symmetry very effectively.

To demonstrate a quantum advantage, a quantum geometric model must be compared with strong classical models that use the same scientific knowledge. Otherwise, the result may show only that incorporating symmetry is helpful.

The central research question is whether quantum representations offer additional useful expressive power or computational benefits after the advantages of geometry have been fairly accounted for.

26.4 Quantum Machine Learning on Error-Corrected Hardware

26.4.1 Why quantum computers make errors

A classical computer stores information using physical states that are engineered to be stable enough for reliable computation. Quantum computers use delicate quantum states. Their information can be disturbed by interactions with the environment, imperfect control operations and measurement errors.

These disturbances are commonly described as noise. A small error in one quantum operation may spread through a circuit, changing its final measurement outcomes. As circuits become deeper, controlling the accumulated effects of noise becomes increasingly important.

This is a serious challenge for quantum machine learning because training may require repeated execution of a parameterised circuit. If the circuit's output is too noisy, it becomes difficult to estimate its predictions or determine which parameter changes would improve the model.

26.4.2 What quantum error correction does

Quantum error correction aims to protect quantum information by encoding logical information across multiple physical qubits. The physical qubits are the actual hardware components. Logical qubits are the protected units of information used by a fault-tolerant computation.

A quantum error-correcting code introduces structured redundancy. The system measures carefully chosen error indicators, often called syndrome information, to learn which types of errors may have occurred without directly measuring and destroying the logical information being protected.

A classical decoder processes the syndrome data and determines a suitable correction or equivalent interpretation of the error. The process can be repeated throughout a computation.

Physical versus logical qubits: A physical qubit is a hardware component. A logical qubit is information protected by an error-correction scheme. Constructing one reliable logical qubit generally requires multiple physical qubits, and the overhead depends on the code, hardware noise and target error rate.

26.4.3 Fault tolerance

A fault-tolerant quantum computer is designed so that errors can be managed while the computation continues. It does not mean that errors become impossible. Rather, the architecture, encoding and operations are arranged so that error rates can be reduced to levels suitable for longer computations.

In suitable fault-tolerant architectures, increasing the protection level can reduce the logical error rate, but it requires additional physical resources and reliable control. The exact relationship depends on the hardware and error correction code.

This distinction matters for machine learning. A model that works on a small noisy device may not behave the same way on a larger error-corrected system. The available gate set, circuit depth, logical qubit count and measurement procedures all affect which algorithms are practical.

26.4.4 How error correction changes quantum machine learning

Many current quantum-learning experiments use relatively shallow circuits because present devices have limited coherence and imperfect operations. Error-corrected hardware could allow more complex algorithms to run reliably, provided the cost of encoding and correcting the quantum information remains manageable.

Potential research directions include:

  • Training quantum models that require deeper circuits.
  • Running quantum optimisation procedures for more iterations.
  • Evaluating algorithms that are too sensitive to noise on present hardware.
  • Studying quantum kernels or feature maps that require more complex quantum operations.
  • Combining error-corrected quantum computation with classical machine-learning systems.

However, error correction does not automatically make a model easier to train. It protects quantum information from errors; it does not guarantee that the model has a useful architecture, that its loss function is easy to optimise or that it will outperform a classical alternative.

26.4.5 The connection between noise and training

A parameterised quantum model produces predictions based on measurements. When the circuit is noisy, the measured expectation values may differ from those predicted by the ideal circuit. Some noise processes introduce bias, while finite measurement counts introduce statistical uncertainty.

During training, these effects can make it harder to estimate gradients. The optimiser may respond to measurement fluctuations rather than genuine improvements in the model.

Error mitigation techniques attempt to reduce the effect of noise without fully correcting the quantum information. They can be useful in some settings, but they often add sampling or computational overhead and do not offer the same general protection as full fault-tolerant error correction.

26.4.6 Logical qubits and resource estimation

A practical quantum-learning algorithm must be assessed using realistic resource estimates. Researchers need to estimate the number of logical qubits, the number and type of logical gates, the circuit depth, the number of measurements and the overhead required for error correction.

A circuit that appears small when counted in physical gates may be expensive when implemented fault-tolerantly. Some operations require considerably more resources than others, depending on the error-correcting code and architecture.

For this reason, claims about future quantum machine learning should distinguish between an algorithm that is mathematically promising and one that can be executed within a realistic hardware budget.

Example: Training a deeper quantum model

Imagine a model whose useful circuit requires hundreds of sequential quantum operations. A noisy device may produce unreliable outputs before the circuit finishes. An error-corrected device could protect the computation, but it might require many physical qubits to construct the necessary logical qubits. The real question is whether the resulting reliable computation is worth that resource cost.

26.4.7 The longer-term direction

Error-corrected hardware may broaden the range of quantum algorithms available to researchers. It could make it possible to explore more complex quantum learning models and more demanding scientific applications.

Nevertheless, the most useful models may still be hybrid. Classical computers are well suited to storing large datasets, performing ordinary matrix operations, managing experiments and updating many model parameters. Quantum hardware may eventually handle selected computations that benefit from reliable quantum processing.

26.5 Quantum-Enhanced Optimisation for AI Training

26.5.1 Why optimisation is central to AI

Training a machine-learning model usually means finding parameters that make its predictions fit the training data according to a chosen objective. A loss function measures how poorly the model performs, and an optimiser changes the parameters in an attempt to reduce that loss.

If a model has parameters represented by θ, its training objective can be written as:

θ* = arg minθ L(θ)

Here, L is the loss function, and θ* represents a parameter setting that minimises the objective, ideally subject to the relevant constraints.

In practice, modern AI training often uses gradient-based algorithms. These algorithms estimate how the loss changes when the parameters change, then update the parameters accordingly.

Optimisation becomes challenging when a model has many parameters, the loss surface is complicated, training data is large or evaluations are expensive. These challenges motivate researchers to explore new optimisation methods, including quantum approaches.

26.5.2 What does quantum-enhanced optimisation mean?

Quantum-enhanced optimisation refers to approaches in which quantum computation is used to help solve an optimisation problem. The quantum component might search through candidate solutions, sample from a distribution, estimate an objective or perform a subroutine used by a larger optimisation process.

It is important to distinguish quantum optimisation algorithms from quantum machine learning itself. A quantum optimiser can be used to train a classical neural network. Conversely, a quantum neural network can be trained using an ordinary classical optimiser.

Combining the two is possible, but it introduces extra questions about how information flows between the classical and quantum parts and whether the combined process is computationally worthwhile.

26.5.3 Variational quantum optimisation

Variational quantum algorithms use a parameterised circuit to represent candidate states or solutions. A classical optimiser updates the circuit parameters according to measured results.

One example is the Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm, or QAOA. It was developed for combinatorial optimisation problems that can be expressed in terms of an objective over discrete choices.

Suppose a problem asks us to select a set of items subject to certain constraints. The quality of a solution can be represented by a cost function. QAOA encodes that cost into a quantum operation and alternates it with operations intended to explore different combinations of choices.

A quantum circuit then produces candidate solutions through measurement. A classical optimiser adjusts the circuit parameters to increase the likelihood of obtaining high-quality candidates.

QAOA is not a general-purpose method guaranteed to outperform classical optimisation. Its performance depends on the problem structure, circuit depth, hardware and quality of the classical alternatives.

26.5.4 Could quantum optimisation improve neural-network training?

Neural-network training involves large and often non-convex optimisation problems. Non-convex means that the loss function can contain many local minima, flat regions, saddle points and other structures that complicate the search for good parameters.

Researchers have considered several ways quantum computation might help:

  • Parameter search: Explore candidate parameter settings for selected optimisation problems.
  • Sampling: Generate samples from distributions that may be useful in probabilistic training methods.
  • Combinatorial subproblems: Address discrete decisions that arise in scheduling, architecture search or resource allocation.
  • Quantum subroutines: Investigate whether a well-defined part of an optimisation algorithm can be accelerated by a quantum algorithm.

These are different research directions. A method that helps with scheduling a training job does not necessarily accelerate the gradient calculations needed to train a neural network.

26.5.5 Quantum annealing and discrete optimisation

Some optimisation problems can be expressed using binary variables. For example, a variable might indicate whether a particular item is selected or whether a particular connection is enabled.

Certain discrete optimisation objectives can be represented as a quadratic unconstrained binary optimisation problem, often called QUBO. Constraints can sometimes be incorporated through penalty terms.

E(x) = Σi aixi + Σi<j bijxixj
xi ∈ {0, 1}

Quantum annealing devices are designed to seek low-energy configurations of a physical system whose energy function represents the optimisation objective. Such methods can be investigated for discrete problems arising in AI workflows.

However, converting a real-world problem into QUBO may require additional variables and penalty terms. Embedding the resulting problem into the device may also be expensive. Classical solvers remain very strong on many practical optimisation tasks.

26.5.6 The measurement bottleneck

A quantum optimiser does not simply reveal an ideal answer after one circuit execution. Measurements produce samples, and repeated executions may be needed to estimate expected costs or gradients accurately.

In a hybrid training loop, each iteration may involve sending parameters to the quantum device, executing a circuit many times, collecting measurements and sending the results back to the classical optimiser. Communication and queueing delays can make this process slow.

A proposed speedup must therefore account for the complete process, including state preparation, circuit execution, repeated measurements, classical processing and data transfer.

26.5.7 Comparing fairly with classical optimisation

The correct baseline depends on the problem. For neural-network training, researchers should compare against established optimisers and strong practical training procedures. For discrete optimisation, they should compare against suitable classical algorithms, including specialised solvers when appropriate.

Useful measurements include solution quality, wall-clock time, energy consumption, total computational resources and reliability across multiple runs. The comparison should also account for preprocessing and the cost of translating the problem into a form suitable for the quantum hardware.

Key lesson: Quantum optimisation is a possible tool for selected problems, not a universal replacement for gradient descent, Adam or classical combinatorial optimisation.

26.5.8 Where the research may lead

The most promising opportunities may arise when a problem has a structure that a particular quantum algorithm can exploit. This may include carefully chosen sampling tasks, quantum-native objectives or discrete optimisation problems with suitable mathematical properties.

For large-scale AI training, it remains an open research question whether quantum methods can deliver a reliable end-to-end advantage over highly optimised classical systems.

26.6 Federated and Privacy-Preserving Quantum Learning

26.6.1 Why privacy matters in machine learning

Machine learning often depends on data collected by different people or organisations. A hospital may hold medical records, a bank may hold transaction histories, and a research laboratory may hold sensitive experimental measurements.

Combining these datasets could improve a model, but moving all the raw data to one location may create legal, commercial or privacy concerns. In some cases, the organisations are not permitted to share their records directly.

Federated learning is one approach to this problem. It allows multiple participants to train a shared model while keeping their raw datasets at their respective locations.

26.6.2 How federated learning works

Consider several hospitals that want to train a model to recognise patterns in medical images. Each hospital holds its own dataset and cannot freely transfer its patients' records to another institution.

  1. A coordinating server distributes the current model parameters to participating hospitals.
  2. Each hospital trains the model using its local data.
  3. The hospitals send model updates, rather than the original medical records, to the coordinator.
  4. The coordinator combines the updates to produce a new shared model.
  5. The process repeats until the model reaches an acceptable result or the training process stops.

This general approach can reduce the need to centralise sensitive data. However, keeping raw data local does not automatically guarantee privacy. Model updates can sometimes reveal information about individual training examples.

26.6.3 What changes when quantum learning is introduced?

Quantum federated learning explores how multiple participants can collaboratively train quantum models or combine quantum-related information while limiting the movement of sensitive data.

There are several possible arrangements. Each participant might own a quantum processor and train a local quantum model. Alternatively, participants may use local classical datasets to train hybrid quantum-classical models, with a central server coordinating selected parameters.

In another setting, a quantum service may be accessed remotely. Participants submit authorised computations without directly controlling the hardware. This raises questions about data confidentiality, the information revealed by submitted circuits and the trust placed in the service provider.

These arrangements have different technical requirements. A system with a quantum processor at every participant is substantially different from one in which all quantum operations are performed by a shared remote service.

26.6.4 Challenges in federated quantum learning

Classical federated learning already faces challenges such as communication cost, unequal dataset sizes, differences in local data distributions and unreliable participants. Quantum systems introduce further complications.

  • Parameter compatibility: Participants need compatible models or a well-defined method for combining their updates.
  • Quantum hardware differences: Devices may have different noise characteristics, gate sets and qubit connectivity.
  • Measurement costs: Local training may require repeated circuit executions to estimate predictions and gradients.
  • Communication overhead: Sharing model parameters and measurement information can be expensive, especially when frequent updates are required.
  • Data imbalance: Participants may hold different amounts and types of data, which can make a shared model difficult to train.

26.6.5 Privacy techniques

Federated learning is a way of organising training, not a complete privacy guarantee. Additional techniques may be needed to reduce information leakage.

Secure aggregation allows a coordinator to obtain a combined update without directly observing every participant's individual update, subject to the protocol's assumptions.

Differential privacy provides a formal framework for limiting how much a model's released information can depend on any one individual's data. It commonly uses carefully calibrated noise and may require limiting the influence of individual examples.

Encryption and secure computation can protect data or intermediate values while they are transferred or processed. Their usefulness depends on the threat model, implementation and computational overhead.

Quantum communication and quantum cryptographic techniques may offer additional tools in specialised settings, but they do not remove the need for a careful privacy design.

26.6.6 Quantum communication and distributed learning

Quantum communication allows quantum states or other information to be transmitted using quantum channels. In principle, distributed quantum systems can share entanglement and coordinate certain quantum operations across different locations.

Such systems could support research into distributed quantum computation and learning. However, transmitting quantum states is technically demanding. Quantum communication channels suffer from loss and noise, and long-distance operation may require specialised infrastructure such as quantum repeaters.

It is also important to distinguish quantum communication from ordinary federated learning. A federated learning system can operate entirely over classical communication channels, even if its local models use quantum circuits. Quantum communication is not a necessary component of every quantum federated-learning proposal.

26.6.7 A practical example

Example: Several laboratories studying a rare disease

Each laboratory has a small collection of patient records. No laboratory has enough data to train a reliable model alone, but regulations prevent them from sharing raw records freely. A federated system allows each laboratory to train locally and contribute model updates. If a quantum model is being investigated, a quantum circuit may process selected features at each site, while classical systems coordinate the training.

Before deployment, the researchers must still test for information leakage, bias, differences between institutions and the reliability of the model. Quantum processing does not by itself solve these problems.

26.6.8 What should be evaluated?

A useful evaluation should measure model quality, communication volume, training time, hardware usage, robustness to different local datasets and the strength of the privacy guarantees.

Researchers must also identify their threat model. Are participants honest? Can the central server be trusted? Could one participant try to infer another participant's data? Could an attacker manipulate model updates?

Answering these questions is necessary before claiming that a system provides privacy-preserving quantum learning. A quantum model, a federated protocol and a privacy guarantee are three separate components that must be designed to work together.

26.7 Quantum AI and Cryptographic Aspects

26.7.1 Why AI and cryptography intersect

Cryptography protects information by controlling who can read it, verify it or change it. It supports secure communication, digital signatures, authentication and many other essential computer systems.

Artificial intelligence introduces new opportunities and risks for these systems. Machine learning can help detect suspicious activity, classify malicious traffic or identify unusual patterns. At the same time, attackers can use AI to discover weaknesses, automate analysis and create more convincing attacks.

Quantum computing adds another dimension. Some quantum algorithms can solve particular mathematical problems more efficiently than known classical methods. Certain cryptographic systems rely on the difficulty of precisely such problems.

The intersection of these fields includes the security of AI systems, quantum-resistant cryptography, quantum algorithms for cryptanalysis and the possible use of learning methods in cryptographic research.

26.7.2 How quantum computing affects cryptography

Modern public-key cryptography often relies on mathematical problems that are believed to be difficult for classical computers to solve efficiently. Two well-known examples are integer factorisation and the discrete logarithm problem.

Shor's algorithm is a quantum algorithm that can solve integer factorisation and discrete logarithm problems in polynomial time under the standard algorithmic model. If a sufficiently large, reliable, fault-tolerant quantum computer becomes available, this could threaten widely used public-key systems based on these problems.

RSA relies on the difficulty of factoring large integers. Traditional elliptic-curve cryptography relies on discrete-logarithm problems over suitable groups. These systems therefore need to be considered in planning for a future with powerful quantum computers.

This does not mean that today's noisy quantum devices can immediately break all deployed cryptographic systems. Large-scale cryptanalysis would require substantial reliable quantum resources, and the resource requirements depend on the target and implementation.

26.7.3 What about symmetric cryptography?

Symmetric cryptography uses the same secret key, or closely related secret keys, for encryption and decryption. Examples include AES and cryptographic hash functions used in security protocols.

Grover's algorithm provides a quantum search speedup for certain unstructured search problems. In an idealised key-search setting, it can reduce the number of oracle queries required from a quantity proportional to N to one proportional to the square root of N.

Classical unstructured search: O(N)

Quantum unstructured search: O(√N)

This is a quadratic improvement in query complexity, not an instant solution to every search problem. Practical costs, circuit depth, error correction and the structure of the cryptographic target matter. Increasing key sizes can also help maintain security margins for symmetric encryption against this type of quantum attack.

26.7.4 Post-quantum cryptography

Post-quantum cryptography refers to cryptographic methods designed to resist attacks by both classical computers and quantum computers, including attacks using known quantum algorithms.

These methods generally run on ordinary classical computers. They do not require every user to own a quantum computer. Instead, they rely on mathematical problems for which no efficient classical or quantum attack is currently known under the relevant security assumptions.

Important families include lattice-based cryptography, hash-based signatures, code-based cryptography and other approaches. Security standards and implementations continue to develop, so real deployments should follow current guidance from recognised standards organisations.

A related field is quantum key distribution, which uses quantum communication to establish secret keys under particular physical and security assumptions. Quantum key distribution and post-quantum cryptography are different approaches: the former uses quantum communication, while the latter uses classical computation with quantum-resistant mathematical constructions.

26.7.5 Can AI help cryptanalysis?

Cryptanalysis studies methods for evaluating or breaking cryptographic systems. Machine learning can help researchers analyse data produced by cryptographic implementations, search for patterns and evaluate certain forms of leakage.

For example, a physical device may leak information through its execution time, power consumption or electromagnetic emissions. These are side channels: they reveal information through the behaviour of the implementation rather than directly through the intended mathematical interface.

Machine-learning models can sometimes classify side-channel measurements and infer information about secret-dependent operations. This research can be used to test and improve defensive implementations.

AI can also support anomaly detection in security logs and help prioritise suspicious events. However, a learned pattern is not automatically a cryptographic break. Strong cryptographic claims require rigorous analysis, reproducible evidence and a clear understanding of the threat model.

26.7.6 Could quantum machine learning help cryptanalysis?

Researchers may investigate whether quantum learning algorithms can improve particular tasks involving cryptographic data. These tasks might include classifying structured instances, learning a property of a distribution or assisting a search over candidate hypotheses.

The theoretical possibility of using quantum machine learning does not imply that it can efficiently break modern cryptography. A useful result must identify the exact task, specify the input model, account for data access and demonstrate a benefit over classical algorithms.

For example, if a quantum algorithm assumes that it can query a carefully constructed quantum oracle, the result may not apply when the real-world input consists of ordinary encrypted files. Building that oracle or loading the data into the required quantum representation may be expensive or impossible in the intended setting.

26.7.7 Cryptography for protecting AI models

The relationship also works in the opposite direction: cryptography can help protect machine-learning systems. AI services may contain valuable model parameters, sensitive training data and private user queries.

Several security techniques are relevant:

  • Secure multiparty computation: Allows multiple parties to compute a function together while limiting what each party learns about the others' private inputs.
  • Homomorphic encryption: Supports selected computations on encrypted data, allowing results to be decrypted after processing without first exposing the original data to the computing service.
  • Digital signatures: Help verify the authenticity and integrity of software, model artefacts and updates.
  • Zero-knowledge proofs: Can allow a party to demonstrate that a statement is true without revealing all the underlying information.

These techniques can be combined with machine-learning systems, although their computational overhead, supported operations and security assumptions vary. Quantum-resistant versions of relevant cryptographic components may also be important for systems expected to remain secure for many years.

26.7.8 Quantum cryptography and the limits of security claims

Quantum physics provides some unusual security properties, including the fact that measuring an unknown quantum state can disturb it. Quantum cryptographic protocols use physical principles to establish security under specified assumptions.

Nevertheless, a real system is more than its mathematical protocol. Hardware flaws, poor random number generation, insecure key storage, software vulnerabilities and implementation mistakes can undermine security.

Similarly, AI-based security tools may identify patterns that human analysts miss, but they can also make mistakes, produce false alarms or be manipulated by adversarial inputs. Strong security requires layers of protection rather than reliance on a single algorithm or model.

Example: Protecting a financial AI service

A bank may use an AI model to identify unusual transactions. Cryptographic protocols protect communications and stored credentials, while access controls restrict who can inspect the data. AI helps identify suspicious behaviour, but it does not replace encryption or secure authentication. As quantum computing advances, the bank may also need to migrate vulnerable public-key cryptographic components to standardised post-quantum alternatives.

26.7.9 The long-term relationship between quantum AI and security

Quantum computing and AI may influence security in several ways. Quantum algorithms can change the assumptions behind some cryptographic systems. Machine learning can improve security monitoring and help test implementations. Quantum learning may eventually contribute to specialised analysis tasks, while privacy-preserving cryptography can help protect AI models and data.

The important principle is to separate established results from speculation. Shor's algorithm has well-established theoretical implications for certain cryptographic systems. The practical performance of quantum machine learning for cryptanalysis, however, depends on the specific problem and remains an active research question.

26.8 Connecting the Research Directions

The seven areas in this chapter may appear unrelated at first. In reality, they are connected by a common question: how can quantum information processing be combined with the mathematical structure, computational requirements and practical constraints of modern learning systems?

Scientific applications begin with a physical problem. Geometric methods exploit the symmetries of that problem. Quantum circuits provide one possible computational representation. Optimisation methods train the model or search for useful solutions. Error correction may eventually make more demanding circuits reliable, while federated learning and cryptographic methods address the privacy and security of the overall system.

The broader picture:
  • Physics and chemistry supply important problems whose underlying behaviour is quantum mechanical.
  • Transformer research asks how quantum processing might contribute to modern representation learning.
  • Geometric learning builds physical symmetries into the model.
  • Error correction aims to make quantum computations more reliable.
  • Quantum optimisation explores alternative ways to solve selected search and training problems.
  • Federated learning and privacy techniques support collaboration without unrestricted sharing of raw data.
  • Cryptography addresses the security implications of both quantum computing and AI.

None of these directions removes the need for careful experimental design. A quantum model should be compared with strong classical alternatives, evaluated using suitable metrics and assessed with all relevant resource costs included.

26.9 How to Evaluate an Emerging Quantum AI Idea

New research proposals often begin with an attractive possibility: perhaps a quantum circuit can represent a complicated distribution, perhaps a quantum optimiser can find a better solution, or perhaps a quantum model can learn a scientific relationship using fewer examples.

The next step is not to assume that the proposal works. It is to turn the idea into a testable scientific question.

Step 1: Define the task precisely

Researchers must specify the input, the desired output and the conditions under which the task is performed. Predicting molecular energy, classifying images and searching a combinatorial space are different tasks with different computational requirements.

Step 2: Identify the quantum component

The proposal should explain exactly which operation uses quantum processing. If the entire task can be performed classically, the researchers should clarify why the quantum component is expected to help.

Step 3: Choose a strong classical baseline

The baseline should reflect the best reasonable classical method for the task. It is not sufficient to compare a new quantum model against a weak or poorly tuned classical model.

Step 4: Count the complete computational cost

The evaluation should include data encoding, circuit executions, measurements, classical optimisation, communication, error correction where relevant and the process of obtaining the final answer.

Step 5: Evaluate reliability

A result obtained once may be misleading. Researchers should repeat experiments, account for statistical uncertainty and investigate sensitivity to noise, initialisation and dataset variation.

Step 6: State the limits of the conclusion

A model that performs well on one small dataset demonstrates a result for that experiment. It does not automatically establish an advantage for all datasets, all hardware platforms or all machine-learning tasks.

Following this process makes quantum machine-learning research more useful. It helps distinguish a genuinely promising result from an interesting idea whose practical value remains uncertain.

26.10 Chapter Summary

Quantum machine learning for physics and chemistry combines scientific simulation, quantum computation and learning models to investigate molecular energies, material properties and physical systems.

Quantum transformers investigate ways to combine quantum processing with attention, feature representations and generative-model ideas. Their usefulness depends on the cost and benefit of the quantum components.

Equivariant and geometric quantum machine learning uses symmetries and geometric relationships to build models that behave consistently when inputs are transformed.

Error-corrected quantum machine learning explores how logical qubits and fault-tolerant operations may enable deeper and more reliable computations, at the cost of substantial hardware resources.

Quantum-enhanced optimisation investigates whether quantum algorithms can help with particular training, sampling or combinatorial optimisation problems. A general advantage for AI training has not been established.

Federated and privacy-preserving quantum learning studies how organisations can collaborate on learning while protecting sensitive data, and why local data storage alone is not a complete privacy guarantee.

Quantum AI and cryptography examines the security consequences of quantum algorithms, the role of AI in security analysis and the use of cryptographic techniques to protect learning systems.

Across all seven topics, the central lesson is the same: quantum computing creates new computational possibilities, but turning those possibilities into useful AI requires sound mathematics, suitable algorithms, reliable hardware and honest comparisons with classical methods.
