Quantum Machine Learning · Chapter 20

Trainability and Barren Plateaus

Why can a quantum model have millions of possible configurations and still be extremely difficult to train? This chapter connects quantum circuits, gradients, entanglement, optimisation, geometry and classical computation to answer that question.

The central idea: a quantum model is useful only when we can find circuit parameters that produce the desired behaviour. A circuit may be mathematically expressive enough to represent a good solution, yet its training process may provide almost no useful information about how to reach that solution. This is the problem of trainability, and barren plateaus are one of its most important obstacles.

20.1 What Are Barren Plateaus?

20.1.1 Training a quantum model

In classical machine learning, a model receives input data, performs calculations using adjustable parameters, and produces a prediction. Training changes those parameters until the predictions become useful. A neural network, for instance, may adjust millions of weights using gradient descent.

A variational quantum model follows a related process. A classical computer supplies parameters to a quantum circuit. The circuit applies parameter-dependent quantum gates, and measurements provide information about the model's output. A classical optimiser then uses that information to update the parameters.

Let the adjustable parameters be collected into a vector θ = (θ₁, θ₂, ..., θₚ). The quantum circuit prepares a state |ψ(θ)⟩, and a cost function measures how well the circuit performs a task. We want to minimise that cost.

θ* = arg minθ C(θ) The goal is to find the parameter values θ* that give the smallest cost C(θ).

The optimiser needs to know how the cost changes when a parameter changes. This information is supplied by a gradient. For the parameter θᵢ, the corresponding gradient component is

gᵢ = ∂C(θ) / ∂θᵢ

If the gradient is positive, a small decrease in that parameter may reduce the cost. If it is negative, a small increase may help. The exact update depends on the optimiser, but the important point is that the gradient tells us which direction is locally promising.

20.1.2 The plateau problem

Imagine walking through a hilly landscape while trying to reach its lowest point. On a normal slope, the ground tells you which direction goes downhill. On a completely flat plain, moving a little to the left or right gives you almost no such information.

A barren plateau is a region of parameter space in which the cost function has gradients that are extremely small, often becoming exponentially small as the number of qubits increases. The optimiser then struggles to identify a useful direction for improvement.

Figure 20.2: Schematic cost landscapes. A barren plateau does not necessarily mean the cost is optimal. It means local gradient information is too weak to guide training effectively.

The word "barren" is important. The landscape may contain good solutions, but ordinary gradient-based training cannot easily find them because the gradients are too small to be useful.

20.1.3 Why very small gradients are troublesome

Consider a gradient-descent update:

θᵢ ← θᵢ − ηgᵢ

Here, η is the learning rate. Suppose η is 0.1 and the gradient is 0.5. The update has magnitude 0.05. If the gradient is only 10−8, the update has magnitude 10−9. The second update is so small that it may have little practical effect.

Simply increasing the learning rate is not a general solution. A large learning rate can cause instability in regions where gradients are informative, and it cannot recover useful information that is absent from noisy measurements.

20.1.4 A flat gradient is not always a barren plateau

A gradient can vanish at an ordinary local minimum, at a maximum, at a saddle point or at a symmetry-protected stationary point. Those situations are not automatically barren plateaus.

The term is particularly useful when discussing a statistical phenomenon: for a suitable family of random quantum circuits and cost functions, the typical gradient variance decreases rapidly as the circuit size grows. This behaviour can make large circuits difficult to train even when their parameter counts are substantial.

Key distinction: expressivity asks whether a model can represent a desired function. Trainability asks whether an optimisation procedure can find useful parameters. A model can be expressive without being easy to train.

20.2 Causes: Expressivity, Entanglement, Noise and Global Cost Functions

Barren plateaus do not have just one cause. Different mechanisms can produce similar symptoms, and several mechanisms may appear together in the same circuit. Understanding the cause matters because each cause suggests a different remedy.

20.2.1 Expressivity: when a circuit becomes too random

In Chapter 17, parameterised quantum circuits were introduced as models whose behaviour changes with adjustable gate parameters. The collection of states or transformations a circuit can represent is related to its expressivity.

A simple circuit with a few gates may explore only a restricted set of quantum states. Adding layers and more adjustable gates generally expands what the circuit can represent. This is useful because a model must be sufficiently flexible to capture the structure of a task.

However, adding layers without considering their structure can make a circuit behave like a highly random unitary transformation. If the resulting circuit samples states that are sufficiently close to certain uniform distributions over quantum states or unitaries, many observables can have nearly the same expectation value across typical parameter settings.

When the output becomes insensitive to small changes in individual parameters, the gradient becomes small. Thus, increasing expressivity can eventually make optimisation harder rather than easier.

The lesson is not that expressive models are bad. It is that expressivity must be balanced with useful structure. A circuit should have enough freedom to learn the task without behaving like a random transformation that hides meaningful directions from the optimiser.

20.2.2 Entanglement: useful correlations, but not a guarantee of trainability

Entanglement is one of the features that distinguishes quantum systems from ordinary classical probability models. Two qubits are entangled when their joint state cannot be represented as a product of independent states for those qubits.

For example, the two-qubit state

|Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩) / √2

is entangled. Neither qubit alone has a definite pure state, yet their measurement outcomes are strongly correlated.

Entangling gates allow a quantum model to represent relationships between qubits. This can be essential for learning correlations that a circuit without suitable entangling operations cannot capture. But deep, broadly connected entangling circuits can also spread information across the system and make local parameter changes difficult to detect through a chosen measurement.

In many random-circuit settings, sufficient depth and entanglement help the circuit approach highly random unitary behaviour. Under appropriate mathematical assumptions, this can contribute to a barren plateau.

Entanglement alone is not the enemy. A shallow circuit can be entangled and trainable. A carefully structured deep circuit can also be trainable. What matters is the relationship between entanglement, circuit depth, parameter placement, observables and the cost function.

Useful principle: do not maximise entanglement merely because quantum computers can create it. Use entanglement where the task needs it, and preserve a meaningful relationship between adjustable gates and measurable outputs.

20.2.3 Global cost functions

A cost function is the quantity that training attempts to minimise. It translates the task into a number that can be estimated from measurements.

A global cost function depends on a property of the entire quantum system. One example is a cost based on the overlap between the final state and a target state:

C(θ) = 1 − |⟨ψtarget|ψ(θ)⟩|²

If the circuit prepares the target state perfectly, the overlap has magnitude one and the cost is zero. If the overlap is small, the cost is larger.

This cost can be useful, but it asks the circuit to coordinate its behaviour across all qubits at once. For sufficiently random circuits, a local parameter change may have an exponentially small effect on the expected value of such a global quantity.

A local cost function, by comparison, can depend on one qubit or a small group of neighbouring qubits. Such a cost may provide a more direct training signal for individual parts of the circuit.

Local costs are not automatically better in every respect. They may fail to capture the global objective, and minimising each local part does not always guarantee that the full system reaches the desired state. Their advantage is that they can avoid some of the gradient-concentration mechanisms associated with global objectives.

20.2.4 Noise: when useful information disappears

Real quantum hardware is imperfect. A qubit can lose coherence, gates can introduce errors, and measurements can be inaccurate. These effects are often grouped under the term quantum noise.

Noise can affect trainability in several ways. It may reduce the difference between outputs produced by nearby parameter values. It may increase uncertainty in gradient estimates. In some architectures, noise can also create a barren plateau even when the corresponding ideal circuit does not exhibit the same problem.

Consider two nearby parameter settings, θ and θ + δ. Suppose their ideal costs differ by a small amount. If hardware noise causes fluctuations larger than this difference, the observed measurements may not reliably tell us which setting is better.

The problem is therefore not only the size of the mathematical gradient. It is also the size of the gradient relative to the uncertainty of the estimate.

20.2.5 Shot noise and the cost of estimating a gradient

Quantum measurements are probabilistic. A device normally repeats the same circuit many times, using a process called sampling or taking shots, to estimate an expectation value.

For an observable with measurement outcomes between −1 and +1, the standard error of a simple sample-mean estimate decreases approximately as the inverse square root of the number of shots:

Standard error ∝ 1 / √N N is the number of independent measurement shots.

To reduce the standard error by a factor of ten, approximately one hundred times as many shots are needed, assuming comparable measurement variance and independent samples.

A tiny gradient may therefore be expensive to distinguish from measurement noise. The optimiser might need an impractically large number of circuit evaluations to determine the correct update.

20.2.6 Depth, hardware noise and the combined effect

Deeper circuits often require more gates and longer execution times. Depending on the hardware, this can increase the chance that unwanted noise affects the final state. A deep circuit may therefore face two difficulties at once: an unfavourable ideal cost landscape and a noisy physical implementation.

It is useful to distinguish the mechanisms:

Mechanism What goes wrong? Possible response
Excessive random expressivity Typical gradients concentrate near zero. Use structured circuits and suitable initialisation.
Deep, broadly entangling circuits Local parameters may have weak influence on the selected cost. Control depth and use problem-aware connectivity.
Global cost functions Useful global information can become exponentially weak. Consider local or layered objectives.
Hardware noise Noise can suppress sensitivity and corrupt estimates. Use noise-aware design and shorter circuits.
Finite measurement shots Gradient estimates have substantial statistical uncertainty. Allocate shots carefully and monitor uncertainty.

20.3 Mathematical Intuition and Key Theorems

20.3.1 The cost is an expectation value

Quantum mechanics gives us a natural way to define many cost functions: measure an observable on the state prepared by the circuit and take its expected value.

C(θ) = ⟨ψ(θ)| O |ψ(θ)⟩ Here, O is an observable, represented mathematically by a Hermitian operator.

The observable O describes what is measured. The parameterised circuit prepares the state |ψ(θ)⟩. Changing θ changes the state and can therefore change the cost.

For a circuit composed of gates, the state can be written as

|ψ(θ)⟩ = U(θ)|0⟩⊗n

The symbol U(θ) represents the complete unitary transformation implemented by the circuit, while n is the number of qubits. The initial state shown here is the all-zero computational basis state. Other initial states are possible.

A barren plateau concerns the behaviour of the derivatives of C(θ), not simply whether the circuit has many parameters. To understand it, we examine how gradients behave over a distribution of possible parameter settings.

20.3.2 Why gradient variance matters

Suppose we randomly initialise a circuit many times and compute the gradient for one parameter each time. We obtain a distribution of gradient values. Some may be positive, some negative, and some close to zero.

The variance measures how widely those gradient values are spread around their mean:

Var(g) = E[g²] − E[g]²

Here, E[g] is the expected gradient and E[g²] is the expected squared gradient. If the mean gradient is zero and the variance is also extremely small, the gradients are typically close to zero.

This is a useful way to formalise the barren plateau phenomenon. In many relevant results, the expected gradient is zero or vanishes by symmetry, while the variance becomes exponentially small in the number of qubits.

Var(∂C/∂θᵢ) ≤ A e−bn Schematic exponential-decay bound: A and b are positive constants independent of n in the regime under consideration. This is not a universal bound for every circuit or cost function.

If b is positive, increasing n reduces the bound exponentially. For example, a factor such as e−bn can become extremely small even when n increases by only a few dozen qubits, depending on b.

The significance is practical. When gradients are exponentially small, estimating their direction may require a rapidly growing amount of measurement data or computational effort.

20.3.3 The exponential cost of resolving a small gradient

Assume, for intuition, that a gradient signal has magnitude of order e−bn and that an estimator's standard error decreases as 1/√N. To make the standard error comparable to or smaller than the signal, we need roughly

1 / √N ≲ e−bn
N ≳ e2bn

This simplified argument shows why exponentially small gradient signals can lead to exponentially expensive sampling. It assumes comparable measurement variance and a suitable estimator; exact shot requirements depend on the observable, noise, circuit and desired confidence.

Notice that two separate problems are involved. The optimiser may need a great deal of data to estimate a small gradient, and even a perfectly estimated small gradient may produce very slow progress. More shots address the first problem, but they do not automatically fix the second.

20.3.4 The parameter-shift rule

Many quantum circuit gradients can be estimated using the parameter-shift rule. For a suitable gate generated by a two-eigenvalue generator, the derivative can be obtained from cost evaluations at shifted parameter values.

∂C/∂θᵢ = [C(θ + (π/2)eᵢ) − C(θ − (π/2)eᵢ)] / 2

The vector eᵢ changes only the i-th parameter. This expression applies to the standard rotation-gate setting with the usual generator normalisation; more general generators can require different shift rules.

The rule is valuable because it provides an exact derivative formula for the ideal expectation value, rather than relying on an arbitrary finite-difference approximation. In hardware, however, both shifted costs must still be estimated from measurements.

If the two costs are nearly equal, their difference may be tiny. The parameter-shift rule does not remove the barren plateau; it gives a valid method for measuring the gradient whose statistical cost may still be large.

20.3.5 The McClean et al. barren plateau result

One of the foundational papers on this subject is the 2018 work by McClean and collaborators, Barren plateaus in quantum neural network training landscapes.

The work established that, under specific assumptions involving random parameterised circuits and cost functions, gradient variance can decrease exponentially with the number of qubits. This was an important warning against assuming that adding parameters and circuit depth necessarily makes a quantum model easier to train.

The result is not a theorem saying that every quantum neural network has a barren plateau. The circuit ensemble, structure, cost function and placement of the differentiated parameter matter. The theorem identifies conditions under which trainability becomes difficult, rather than declaring all variational quantum algorithms unusable.

20.3.6 The role of 2-designs

A useful mathematical concept in this area is a unitary 2-design. Informally, an ensemble of quantum transformations forms a 2-design when its first and second statistical moments reproduce those of the uniform Haar-random unitary ensemble.

The Haar ensemble describes a mathematically uniform distribution over unitary transformations. It is useful for analysing what happens when a circuit behaves sufficiently like a random transformation, even though physically sampling exact Haar-random unitaries is generally impractical.

Certain gradient-variance calculations depend on second moments. Therefore, when a circuit ensemble forms an appropriate 2-design, mathematical tools from random-unitary theory can establish small-gradient results for particular cost functions.

A circuit need not contain every possible quantum gate to exhibit this behaviour. Certain sufficiently expressive random circuits can approximate the relevant statistical properties. But not every deep circuit is a 2-design, and circuit depth alone is not enough to conclude that a barren plateau must occur.

20.3.7 Global and local costs have different scaling behaviour

The distinction between global and local objectives has a strong mathematical basis. For particular random circuit ensembles, the variance of gradients for global cost functions can decrease exponentially with system size. Under suitable assumptions, local costs can avoid this particular exponential scaling and have more favourable variance behaviour.

A common intuition is that a local observable examines only a small part of the system. Its value may therefore depend on a smaller portion of the circuit's behaviour, whereas a global objective may require many parts of the system to align in a coordinated way.

This is a trend under specified mathematical conditions, not a guarantee. Local objectives can also develop barren plateaus, especially when circuit depth, noise, parameter placement or other structural features create unfavourable conditions.

20.3.8 Noise-induced barren plateaus

Work on noise-induced barren plateaus has shown that certain local-noise models can suppress gradient information exponentially with circuit depth. In such settings, the relevant scaling may depend strongly on depth and the noise process, rather than only on the number of qubits.

This creates an important distinction:

  • Expressivity-induced plateaus: the ideal circuit and cost landscape have concentrated gradients.
  • Noise-induced plateaus: the physical noisy implementation suppresses gradient signals.
  • Sampling difficulty: measurement uncertainty makes small gradients difficult to estimate.

These difficulties can overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A useful diagnosis should examine ideal gradients, noisy gradients and measurement uncertainty separately.

What the mathematics teaches us: the key question is not simply whether a circuit is large. It is how gradient information scales with the number of qubits, circuit depth, measurement cost and noise level.

20.4 Mitigation Strategies: Initialisation, Local Costs and Layerwise Training

Barren plateaus are not solved by one universal technique. The appropriate strategy depends on why the gradients are small. A useful design principle is to build the model so that its parameters retain a meaningful influence on measurable outputs.

20.4.1 Strategy one: choose a better initialisation

Initialisation means choosing the parameter values before training begins. A random initialisation may seem natural, but an unrestricted random choice can place the circuit in a region where gradients are already very small.

Instead, the initial parameters can be chosen to keep the circuit near a simple, interpretable transformation. For rotation gates, one possible starting point is to initialise angles near zero, so that many gates initially perform transformations close to the identity.

Ry(θ) = [ cos(θ/2) −sin(θ/2) ]
[ sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2) ] Matrix representation of a single-qubit rotation around the y-axis, using the computational basis.

At θ = 0, this gate is the identity transformation. Starting near zero can make the overall circuit initially simpler than a circuit whose gates are all sampled from broad random distributions.

However, setting every parameter exactly to zero is not always appropriate. Symmetries may force certain derivatives to vanish, and a completely symmetric initial state may prevent the model from learning distinctions it needs. Small, structured perturbations can sometimes help.

A more informed approach uses the task, circuit architecture and expected output to choose initial parameters. For example, a circuit intended to learn small corrections to a known transformation may be initialised close to that transformation.

20.4.2 Identity-block initialisation

An identity block is a group of gates arranged so that their combined operation is the identity, or close to it, at the initial parameter setting. The block can contain several adjustable gates, even though its initial overall action is simple.

During training, the parameters move away from their initial values and the block begins to perform a learned transformation. The idea is to obtain a circuit that starts with a controlled behaviour rather than a highly random one.

Identity-block initialisation is useful in some variational architectures, but it does not guarantee trainability. The surrounding circuit and objective can still suppress gradients.

20.4.3 Strategy two: use local cost functions

Suppose the task is to prepare a target state across many qubits. A global objective might compare the entire output state with the target in a single quantity. A local alternative could measure the agreement of individual qubits or small groups of qubits with their desired states.

If the target is a product state, for example, a local cost could be built from single-qubit observables:

Clocal(θ) = (1/n) Σi=1n Ci(θ)

Each Cᵢ evaluates a local property associated with qubit i. The total objective averages the local errors.

This can provide more direct information about the circuit's local behaviour. It can also be easier to interpret: if the contribution from one region is poor, the optimiser has a measurable indication of that problem.

The challenge is that the local objective must still be related to the desired final task. A circuit may optimise all selected local measurements without reproducing the required global correlations.

One practical solution is to use local objectives during early training and gradually introduce terms that capture more global properties. Such a schedule is problem-dependent and must be evaluated experimentally.

20.4.4 Strategy three: layerwise training

A deep parameterised circuit may contain many layers. Training all the layers simultaneously can create a complicated optimisation problem, especially if the circuit is poorly initialised.

Layerwise training changes the process. Instead of activating every layer at once, we train a smaller circuit first, then extend it gradually.

The first layer is trained using the original cost function. When the circuit reaches a useful state, a second layer is introduced. The parameters of the existing layers may be retained or partly frozen, while the new layer is trained. Further layers are added as needed.

There are several variants. One approach trains each newly added layer while keeping earlier layers fixed. Another allows earlier layers to be fine-tuned after the new layer has become useful. A third gradually increases the number of active parameters.

The advantage is that the optimiser initially deals with a simpler problem. The limitation is that an early layer may learn a representation that makes later optimisation difficult. Layerwise training can also consume more total training stages and does not eliminate every kind of barren plateau.

20.4.5 Strategy four: control circuit depth and connectivity

More layers are not automatically better. Every extra layer introduces additional gates, parameters and opportunities for noise. The goal is to use the depth needed by the task, rather than making the circuit as deep as possible.

Connectivity matters as well. A circuit in which every qubit interacts with every other qubit has a different structure from one in which each qubit interacts only with its neighbours. Local connectivity can preserve a clear relationship between parts of the circuit and may delay some randomisation effects.

Restricted connectivity can, however, make some tasks more difficult or require additional circuit depth. The right choice depends on the relationships the model needs to represent and on the hardware's available interactions.

20.4.6 Strategy five: use problem-informed ansätze

An ansatz is the chosen family of quantum circuits from which the model selects a particular transformation. A generic ansatz might repeat the same rotation and entangling gates regardless of the task. A problem-informed ansatz uses knowledge about the task to restrict the available transformations.

For example, a molecular simulation circuit may be designed to respect particle-number constraints. A circuit for a spin system may preserve a relevant symmetry. A circuit for data arranged on a grid may use a connectivity pattern reflecting that arrangement.

These restrictions can reduce unnecessary freedom and help keep the circuit within a physically meaningful part of the state space. They can improve trainability, but the effect is not universal: a poorly chosen restriction can exclude the desired solution altogether.

20.4.7 Strategy six: improve the measurement budget

Even a trainable ideal circuit can be difficult to optimise when gradient estimates are noisy. Measurements should therefore be allocated according to the information needed.

For example, an optimiser may use fewer shots during early exploration and more shots when comparing promising parameter settings. It can also estimate uncertainty and avoid treating two costs as meaningfully different when the observed difference is smaller than the statistical error.

This strategy helps manage sampling costs. It does not remove the underlying exponential concentration of gradients if that concentration is present.

20.4.8 Strategy seven: use suitable optimisers and gradient-free methods

Gradient-based methods are not the only way to optimise a quantum circuit. Derivative-free methods can search using cost evaluations alone, and methods such as Bayesian optimisation may be useful for low-dimensional parameter spaces.

Their success depends on the problem. If the cost landscape is almost flat and measurement estimates are noisy, a gradient-free method may also struggle. It may simply explore the landscape with a different strategy.

Similarly, adaptive learning rates and momentum can help with some optimisation difficulties, but they cannot guarantee recovery of a gradient signal that is exponentially small relative to the noise.

20.4.9 A practical mitigation workflow

  1. Start small. Test the circuit on a modest number of qubits.
  2. Inspect gradients. Measure their mean, variance and magnitude at several initial parameter settings.
  3. Compare ideal and noisy simulations. Determine whether the problem exists before hardware noise is introduced.
  4. Check the objective. Investigate whether a global cost can be replaced or supplemented by a useful local objective.
  5. Change the initialisation. Compare random, near-identity and task-informed starting points.
  6. Control depth. Add layers only when the existing circuit needs more capacity.
  7. Evaluate scaling. Repeat the experiment at different qubit counts and depths.

A mitigation strategy should be judged by measurable evidence: gradient statistics, optimisation success, number of circuit evaluations, final task performance and robustness to noise. A visually complicated circuit is not evidence of a good model.

20.5 Symmetry-Aware and Geometric Ansätze

20.5.1 Why symmetry matters

Symmetry appears whenever a system remains unchanged under certain transformations. A square, for example, retains its shape when rotated by 90 degrees. In physics, a symmetry can express a conservation law or a constraint on which states are physically allowed.

Quantum systems also have symmetries. An observable or Hamiltonian may remain unchanged under a particular transformation. A quantum circuit can be designed to respect that symmetry, restricting the states it can prepare to a physically relevant sector.

Why should this help trainability? If a task only requires states within a particular symmetry sector, allowing the circuit to explore every possible state may introduce unnecessary degrees of freedom. A symmetry-aware circuit removes some of those irrelevant directions.

20.5.2 Symmetry in mathematical form

Let H be a Hamiltonian and let S be a symmetry operator. A common symmetry condition is

[H, S] = HS − SH = 0

This means that H and S commute. They can therefore share eigenstates under the usual finite-dimensional Hermitian setting. If the system begins in a suitable symmetry eigenstate, its ideal evolution under H preserves the corresponding symmetry sector.

For a circuit ansatz U(θ), a strong symmetry-preserving condition is

[U(θ), S] = 0

If this holds for every allowed parameter setting, the circuit preserves the relevant symmetry. More generally, circuit design may preserve a sector without requiring this exact commutation relation for every possible operator S.

The important point is that the ansatz is restricted by mathematical structure, rather than by an arbitrary decision to reduce its size.

20.5.3 Example: preserving particle number

In some quantum chemistry problems, the number of particles is a physically meaningful quantity. A circuit that changes the particle number without a physical reason may waste computational effort exploring states that are irrelevant to the target problem.

A particle-number-preserving ansatz uses gates chosen to preserve the appropriate particle-number sector. This can reduce the search space and prevent the circuit from leaving the desired sector during ideal evolution.

Such a restriction can be helpful for both physical correctness and optimisation. However, a circuit must still have enough freedom to represent the desired state within that sector. If the ansatz is too restrictive, it can introduce an approximation error that training cannot eliminate.

20.5.4 Symmetry and trainability are related, but not identical

Symmetry-aware design is not a universal cure for barren plateaus. Restricting a circuit to a smaller sector can improve the relevance of its parameters, but a random circuit that becomes sufficiently expressive within that sector can still develop small gradients.

The effect also depends on the cost function. If the cost cannot distinguish states within a symmetry sector, or if the chosen initial state is incompatible with the desired sector, the circuit may still be difficult to train.

The best outcome occurs when the symmetry reflects genuine structure in the task, the initial state belongs to the appropriate sector, and the ansatz provides enough flexibility to reach the target.

20.5.5 Geometric ansätze

The word geometry here refers to the structure of the state space and the paths that a parameterised circuit can follow through it. It does not mean merely drawing gates in a visually attractive arrangement.

A parameterised circuit defines a family of quantum states. As its parameters change, the prepared state moves through this family. The optimiser tries to find a path through parameter space that leads towards a low-cost state.

Different parameterisations produce different paths. Some paths may be poorly conditioned, meaning that changes in certain parameter directions have almost no effect while changes in others have a very large effect. This can make optimisation slow or unstable.

Geometric circuit design aims to make the accessible family of states and the parameterisation suitable for the task. It can draw on physical symmetries, local interactions, conservation laws, circuit topology and the geometry of quantum states.

20.5.6 Quantum natural gradient

Ordinary gradient descent measures parameter changes using the coordinates θ chosen by the circuit designer. But different parameter changes can have very different effects on the quantum state. A small change in one direction may substantially alter the state, while a larger change in another direction may barely change it.

Quantum natural gradient methods try to account for this geometry. They use a metric related to the distinguishability of nearby quantum states, commonly connected to the quantum geometric tensor and, in appropriate settings, the Fubini–Study metric.

Δθ = −η F−1 ∇C Schematic natural-gradient update. F is a suitable metric or metric approximation, with regularisation or a pseudoinverse used when necessary.

Here, ∇C is the ordinary cost gradient. The metric F adjusts the update to reflect how parameter changes affect the represented quantum state.

This can improve conditioning and reduce the effect of an arbitrary choice of parameter coordinates. It is especially useful when the model's geometry differs greatly across parameter directions.

However, natural gradient does not guarantee a solution to barren plateaus. If the cost gradient itself is extremely small, multiplying it by an approximate inverse metric may still fail to provide a useful update. In addition, estimating the metric can be expensive, and a nearly singular metric can require careful regularisation.

20.5.7 Expressivity, geometry and redundancy

A circuit may contain many parameters without representing an equally large number of independent changes in quantum state. Some parameters may be redundant, or different parameter settings may prepare physically equivalent states.

From a geometric perspective, these redundancies mean that parameter space can contain directions that produce little or no meaningful change in the state. Such directions can complicate optimisation.

A carefully designed ansatz seeks a useful balance: enough independent directions to represent the required states, but not so much unnecessary freedom that training becomes needlessly difficult.

Design principle: use the physics and mathematical structure of the task to decide which states and transformations the circuit should explore. The aim is not maximum freedom; it is sufficient, relevant freedom.

20.6 Connections to Classical Simulability

20.6.1 What does classical simulability mean?

A quantum circuit is classically simulable when a classical computer can reproduce the quantities of interest from that circuit with manageable computational resources. Depending on the task, this might mean computing an output probability, estimating an expectation value or sampling from the circuit's output distribution.

Some quantum circuits are easy to simulate classically. Others appear to require substantially more resources as their size grows, although the difficulty depends on the circuit family, the observable and the computational task.

It is tempting to assume that a circuit which is difficult to simulate classically must be a good machine-learning model. That conclusion does not follow. Classical simulation difficulty and optimisation difficulty are different properties.

20.6.2 Entanglement and classical simulation

A general pure state of n qubits requires 2n complex amplitudes to represent directly. This exponential growth is one reason why general quantum-state simulation can become expensive.

|ψ⟩ = Σx=02ⁿ−1 αx|x⟩
Σx |αx|² = 1

The second equation expresses normalisation: the total probability across all computational basis outcomes is one. In a general state, the amplitudes may require an exponentially large description.

Yet not every quantum state needs an exponential description. A product state can be described by its individual qubit states. Certain structured states can also be represented efficiently using methods such as tensor networks.

Entanglement is relevant because it can make a state harder to represent as a simple product of independent states. However, the amount and pattern of entanglement matter. Some highly entangled states still have efficient representations under particular structures.

20.6.3 Tensor networks and circuit structure

Tensor-network methods represent a large quantum state using smaller tensors connected according to a chosen structure. Their efficiency often depends on quantities such as bond dimension and the entanglement structure across partitions of the system.

For one-dimensional systems, matrix product states can represent many physically relevant states efficiently when the required entanglement is sufficiently limited. But a general state may require a very large bond dimension, removing this advantage.

This creates an interesting relationship with variational circuits. A shallow, locally connected circuit may be easier to simulate classically than a deep, highly entangling circuit. At the same time, the shallow circuit may be easier to train because its parameters retain more direct influence over local measurements.

This is not a universal relationship. A shallow circuit can still be difficult to simulate for particular tasks, and a deeper circuit can remain classically simulable when its structure permits an efficient representation.

20.6.4 The surprising connection: random circuits can be difficult to simulate and difficult to train

Random, highly expressive circuits can be challenging to simulate classically because they may generate complicated quantum states and output distributions. Certain random circuit families also exhibit barren plateaus for suitable cost functions.

These two observations seem surprising at first. We might expect a circuit that produces complex quantum behaviour to be a powerful model. But complexity of representation does not guarantee that an optimiser can identify a useful direction in parameter space.

A random circuit may be hard for a classical computer to represent explicitly, while its average cost changes very little under small parameter adjustments. In that situation, the circuit is computationally complex in one sense but difficult to learn in another.

This distinction is central to quantum machine learning: computational complexity is not the same as learning efficiency.

20.6.5 A simple comparison

Property Question being asked
Expressivity Can the circuit represent the desired state or function?
Classical simulability Can a classical computer reproduce the relevant circuit behaviour efficiently?
Trainability Can an optimiser find useful parameters with reasonable effort?
Quantum advantage Can the quantum approach outperform relevant classical alternatives on a well-defined task and resource measure?

These properties influence one another, but none can be substituted for another. A circuit may be expressive and hard to simulate yet difficult to train. Another circuit may be easy to simulate but still be a useful model for a small problem or as a component in a larger algorithm.

20.6.6 Can avoiding barren plateaus make a model classically simulable?

Not necessarily. Reducing circuit depth, restricting entanglement or introducing local structure may improve trainability. Those same restrictions can sometimes make classical simulation easier, but there is no general theorem saying that every trainable circuit must be efficiently classically simulable.

Conversely, adding enough entanglement to make simulation expensive does not prove that the circuit provides a useful quantum-learning advantage. The model still needs to learn the task, and the data preparation, measurements, optimisation and total resource costs must be considered.

A sensible research objective is therefore to find architectures that balance three requirements:

  1. Representation: the circuit can express useful solutions.
  2. Trainability: its parameters can be optimised efficiently.
  3. Computational value: the complete method offers a meaningful benefit over relevant classical approaches.

20.6.7 The role of data loading and measurement

Quantum machine-learning proposals sometimes focus on the internal circuit while overlooking the cost of loading classical data into quantum states. If encoding a large dataset requires substantial resources, that cost can offset a speed-up within the circuit itself.

Measurement creates another limitation. A quantum state may contain many amplitudes, but a measurement does not reveal all those amplitudes in a single run. Estimating a desired output can require repeated experiments.

Trainability adds a third cost: the number of circuit evaluations needed to optimise the parameters. Therefore, a fair comparison with a classical model must include encoding, circuit execution, optimisation and measurement, rather than considering only the number of quantum gates.

Research perspective: a quantum model should not be judged by how mysterious or complicated its circuit looks. It should be judged by whether the model can be trained, whether the result is useful and whether the complete computational cost is competitive.

Putting Everything Together

Trainability is the bridge between a circuit's theoretical capabilities and its practical usefulness. Expressivity determines what a model can represent, but optimisation determines whether its parameters can be found in a reasonable amount of time.

Barren plateaus arise when gradient information becomes too small to guide training effectively. Random and highly expressive circuits, certain global cost functions, deep noisy circuits and finite measurement budgets can all contribute, although their mathematical mechanisms differ.

The most useful response is to identify the mechanism before changing the architecture. Better initialisation, local objectives, layerwise training, symmetry-aware ansätze and geometry-aware optimisation can help under suitable conditions. None of these methods guarantees success for every problem.

The relationship with classical simulability adds one final lesson. Making a circuit hard to simulate is not the same as making it easy to train, and neither property by itself establishes quantum advantage.

Six ideas to remember

  1. A barren plateau is a region where gradients are typically extremely small, often with exponentially decreasing variance.
  2. Expressivity is useful, but randomisation and excessive depth can make optimisation difficult.
  3. Entanglement supports quantum correlations; its effect on trainability depends on the circuit and objective.
  4. Local costs and layerwise training can improve gradient behaviour under suitable conditions.
  5. Symmetry-aware design and quantum natural gradient exploit problem structure and quantum-state geometry, but neither is a universal cure.
  6. Classical simulation difficulty, trainability and quantum advantage are separate questions that must be evaluated independently.

Exercise 2: Sampling requirements

Suppose the gradient signal decreases like e−bn and the standard error of its estimate decreases like 1/√N. Derive the approximate scaling of N needed to keep the standard error comparable to the gradient signal.

Hint: set 1/√N approximately equal to e−bn and square both sides after rearranging.

Exercise 3: Design a mitigation experiment

Consider gradient variance, optimisation success, number of circuit evaluations and final task performance.

Exercise 4: Global versus local objectives
