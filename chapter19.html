19.0 Introduction: What is a Quantum Neural Network?

Artificial neural networks are inspired by the way biological nervous systems process information. They contain connected computational units called neurons. Each neuron receives inputs, combines them using adjustable weights, applies a mathematical function and passes the result to the next layer.

A quantum neural network, usually shortened to QNN, uses a parameterised quantum circuit as part or all of a machine learning model. Instead of relying only on ordinary numerical operations, the model encodes information into quantum states and processes those states using quantum gates. Measurements then produce classical numbers that can be used to make predictions.

The word parameterised is important. A quantum circuit can contain gates whose rotation angles are controlled by trainable parameters. During learning, an optimisation algorithm adjusts these parameters so that the circuit produces outputs closer to the desired answers.

The basic idea behind a QNN

Classical data
→
Encode into qubits
→
Trainable quantum circuit
→
Measurement
→
Prediction

The circuit parameters are updated repeatedly using a classical optimiser until the model learns a useful relationship between inputs and outputs.

Does a QNN have to look like a human brain?

No. The name comes from the mathematical role that the circuit plays in a learning system, not from a claim that quantum circuits behave like biological neurons. Most QNNs are better understood as trainable mathematical models built from quantum operations.

What you should understand by the end

  • How quantum convolution, recurrence, attention and graph models are constructed.
  • Why many useful QNNs are hybrid classical-quantum systems.
  • How to implement and train a small QNN in Python.
  • Why a QNN is not automatically faster or more accurate than a classical neural network.

19.1 From Classical Neurons to Quantum Circuits

19.1.1 How does a classical neuron work?

Imagine building a system that predicts whether a student will pass an examination. The inputs might include the number of hours studied, attendance and marks in earlier tests. A classical neuron combines these inputs into a score.

Each input has a weight. A larger weight means that the corresponding input has a stronger influence on the score. The neuron also has a bias, which allows the decision boundary to shift.

z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + ... + wₙxₙ + b

y = φ(z)

Here, x₁, x₂ and the other x values are inputs; w₁, w₂ and the other w values are trainable weights; b is the bias; and φ is an activation function such as sigmoid, tanh or ReLU.

The first equation combines the information. The second converts the combined score into an output. A neural network connects many such computations into layers, allowing it to learn complicated relationships.

19.1.2 What changes when we use a quantum circuit?

A quantum circuit uses qubits instead of ordinary bits as its basic information-processing units. A classical bit has a value of either 0 or 1. A qubit can exist in a quantum superposition of the computational basis states |0⟩ and |1⟩.

A pure single-qubit state can be written as:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

|α|² + |β|² = 1

The complex numbers α and β are probability amplitudes. If we measure the qubit in the computational basis, the probability of obtaining 0 is |α|² and the probability of obtaining 1 is |β|².

Superposition does not mean that a measurement reveals both 0 and 1 at the same time. A measurement returns a particular outcome. The amplitudes determine the probabilities of the possible outcomes.

Quantum circuits also support entanglement. Entangled qubits can possess joint quantum properties that cannot be described by treating each qubit as an independent quantum state. This gives quantum models another way to represent relationships between parts of the input.

19.1.3 Gates take the place of trainable computations

Classical neural networks apply weighted sums and activation functions. A QNN applies quantum gates to transform a quantum state. Some gates are fixed, while others depend on trainable parameters.

For example, a rotation gate changes a qubit's state according to an angle θ. A controlled-NOT gate, or CNOT, performs an operation on a target qubit depending on the state of a control qubit. Such gates can create entanglement.

|ψ(θ)⟩ = U(θ)|ψ₀⟩

f(θ) = ⟨ψ(θ)| O |ψ(θ)⟩

In this notation, |ψ₀⟩ is the initial quantum state, U(θ) is the parameterised circuit, and O is a measurable observable. The function f(θ) is the expected measurement value of O.

For a Pauli-Z measurement on a single qubit, the expectation value lies between -1 and +1. If the probabilities of obtaining 0 and 1 are P(0) and P(1), respectively, then:

⟨Z⟩ = P(0) - P(1)

This expected value is a classical number. It can be passed to another layer or used as a prediction. In practice, the value may be estimated by repeating measurements, also called shots.

19.1.4 Where does learning happen?

Suppose a circuit contains trainable rotation angles θ₁, θ₂ and θ₃. Initially, these angles may be selected randomly. The model makes a prediction, compares it with the correct answer and calculates a loss.

The optimiser then changes the angles to reduce the loss. This process repeats over many training examples. The circuit does not automatically know which gates are useful; learning comes from selecting parameters that improve the objective.

Initial parameters
→
Quantum circuit
→
Prediction
→
Loss
Optimiser updates parameters
←
Gradient information

This is a feedback loop. The model improves by repeatedly evaluating its current parameters and adjusting them.

19.1.5 A useful comparison

Classical neural network Quantum neural network
Uses classical numbers and tensors. Uses quantum states represented by qubits.
Computations use operations such as matrix multiplication and activation functions. Computations use quantum gates and measurements.
Trainable parameters are commonly weights and biases. Trainable parameters are commonly gate angles or other circuit controls.
Activations can often be evaluated directly. Outputs are obtained through measurements or estimated expectation values.
Many operations can be efficiently performed on ordinary hardware. Quantum circuit execution requires a simulator or quantum processor.
Deep architectures can be trained using established techniques. Training can be affected by measurement noise, hardware limitations and difficult gradients.

A point worth remembering

A QNN is not simply a classical neural network running on a quantum computer. Its internal representation and operations are different. However, the complete learning system can still depend heavily on classical computers for data preparation, optimisation and evaluation.

19.2 Quantum Perceptrons

19.2.1 What is a perceptron?

A perceptron is one of the simplest models used in neural networks. It receives input values, combines them using weights and produces an output. A traditional binary perceptron often uses a threshold to decide between two classes.

A quantum perceptron attempts to perform an analogous learning role using quantum states, quantum operations and measurements. There is no single universal circuit design that defines every quantum perceptron. Different proposals use different encodings, gates, measurements and learning rules.

19.2.2 A simple quantum-perceptron design

Consider a model with two numerical inputs, x₁ and x₂. Each input is encoded into a qubit using a rotation gate. A trainable circuit then mixes the encoded information before a measurement produces an output.

x₁
→
Encode qubit 1
x₂
→
Encode qubit 2
Trainable rotations
→
Entangling gate
→
Measurement

For example, an angle encoding might prepare the state:

|ψ(x)⟩ = RY(x₁)|0⟩ ⊗ RY(x₂)|0⟩

Here, RY(x) is a rotation around the Y axis of the Bloch sphere, and ⊗ denotes the tensor product. The resulting two-qubit state is a joint representation of the two input features.

A trainable circuit V(θ) can then transform this state. If we measure the first qubit using the Pauli-Z observable, the model output may be defined as:

f(x, θ) = ⟨ψ(x)| V†(θ) Z₁ V(θ) |ψ(x)⟩

The dagger symbol indicates the conjugate transpose, and Z₁ means that the observable acts on the first qubit. The result is an expected value between -1 and +1.

19.2.3 Turning the output into a prediction

Suppose the task is binary classification. One possible rule is to predict class 1 when f(x, θ) is above zero and class 0 otherwise. Another approach maps the expectation value to a probability:

p(class 1 | x) = (1 + f(x, θ)) / 2

This mapping produces a value between zero and one. It is a possible modelling choice, not a requirement for every QNN. The model can be trained using a suitable loss function.

19.2.4 Why use trainable quantum operations?

A trainable circuit can represent a family of functions rather than a single fixed function. Different parameter values produce different transformations, and training searches for a useful member of that family.

Entangling gates can help represent relationships between input features. This does not guarantee that every quantum perceptron is more expressive than a classical perceptron, or that it learns better. Its performance depends on the circuit structure, encoding, task and training procedure.

Example: deciding whether to recommend a book

Imagine that x₁ represents a reader's interest score and x₂ represents the similarity between the reader and the book. The quantum circuit encodes both values, processes them and returns a score.

During training, examples of books that readers liked or disliked guide the adjustment of circuit parameters. After training, the model can estimate how suitable another book might be.

This example describes the modelling idea. It does not imply that a quantum perceptron would outperform an ordinary recommendation model for this task.

19.2.5 The limitations of a quantum perceptron

  • Classical inputs must be encoded into quantum states.
  • Measurement introduces statistical uncertainty when finite shots are used.
  • The number of useful trainable parameters is constrained by the circuit design.
  • Some proposed quantum neuron models use specialised operations that are difficult to realise on current hardware.
  • A small quantum circuit may not have enough capacity for a complicated task.

19.3 Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks (QCNN)

19.3.1 Understanding classical convolution

A classical convolutional neural network, or CNN, is commonly used for images. An image contains local patterns such as edges, corners and textures. Instead of examining every pixel in an unrelated way, a CNN applies small filters across the image.

The same filter is reused at different locations. This allows the network to detect a similar pattern even when it appears in a different part of an image. Later layers combine simpler patterns into more complex ones.

Pooling or downsampling can reduce the size of the representation. For example, a network might gradually transform pixels into edges, edges into shapes and shapes into object-level features.

19.3.2 How does a QCNN differ?

A quantum convolutional neural network uses structured quantum operations to process information in a manner inspired by convolutional architectures. Some QCNN designs use local parameterised gates followed by pooling-like operations that reduce the number of active qubits.

In one common conceptual design, neighbouring qubits interact first. A smaller collection of qubits then carries information into the next stage. This process repeats, allowing the circuit to build a hierarchy of quantum features.

A hierarchical quantum convolutional circuit

Input qubits
→
Local trainable gates
→
Pooling-like stage
↓
Fewer active qubits
→
More local gates
→
Final measurement

The phrase pooling-like is deliberate. Classical max pooling selects values from a region. A quantum pooling operation can instead use gates and measurements, discard or reset qubits, or use other circuit structures to reduce the representation. It need not be identical to classical pooling.

19.3.3 What does a quantum convolutional layer learn?

The trainable gates can learn transformations of quantum states that help separate different classes. Local gates initially process small groups of qubits, while subsequent stages combine information across increasingly large portions of the system.

This hierarchical structure is useful for thinking about structured data. In certain quantum physics problems, the inputs themselves may be quantum states rather than ordinary images. QCNN architectures have been studied for tasks involving quantum phases of matter and related state-classification problems.

19.3.4 Why are QCNNs interesting?

  • Hierarchical processing: Local transformations build towards larger-scale representations.
  • Parameter sharing: Some architectures reuse related gate structures, which can reduce the number of independent parameters.
  • Structured interactions: The circuit can reflect local relationships in the problem.
  • Quantum-native applications: Some tasks involve quantum states that are naturally processed by quantum hardware.

19.3.5 Why is a QCNN not automatically an image-classification breakthrough?

Ordinary images contain many pixel values. Encoding a large image into a useful quantum state can be expensive. Loading the data, executing the circuit, repeating measurements and transferring results back to classical software all contribute to the total cost.

A QCNN might use fewer trainable parameters than a particular classical network, but parameter count alone does not establish computational advantage. A fair comparison must account for data loading, circuit execution, training cost, prediction quality and the hardware required.

Important distinction

A QCNN is a family of architectures, not one standard circuit. Its usefulness depends on the task and on how the quantum representation is prepared. For ordinary image datasets, classical CNNs remain the established practical choice in most applications.

19.4 Quantum Recurrent and Attention-Style Models

19.4.1 Why do sequences need special models?

Consider the sentence, "The boy picked up the book because it was interesting." Understanding the word "it" requires looking back at earlier words. The order of information matters.

The same problem appears in speech, time-series forecasting, sensor data and financial records. A model that treats every input as an unrelated item may lose the relationships between earlier and later observations.

Classical recurrent neural networks process a sequence one step at a time. They maintain a hidden state that summarises information from previous steps. Long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) and gated recurrent units (GRUs) were developed to help recurrent models learn useful dependencies over longer sequences.

19.4.2 Quantum recurrent neural networks

A quantum recurrent neural network, or QRNN, incorporates a quantum circuit into a sequence-processing model. The circuit may receive the current input together with a representation of the previous state and produce an output or updated state.

Input at time t
→
Encode input
→
Quantum circuit
→
Measured output
↓
Classical or quantum state update
→
Next time step

There are several ways to implement this idea. A model may keep a classical hidden state between circuit executions, use a quantum state that is carried forward, or combine both. These choices lead to different computational costs and different meanings of recurrence.

19.4.3 A simple recurrent formulation

A classical recurrent model often uses the following equation:

hₜ = g(xₜ, hₜ₋₁; θ)

yₜ = r(hₜ)

Here, xₜ is the current input, hₜ₋₁ is the previous hidden state, hₜ is the updated state, and yₜ is the output. The parameter set θ controls the update rule.

In a quantum-inspired recurrent design, the update function g can include a parameterised quantum circuit and a measurement step. For example, the classical hidden state may be encoded into qubits alongside the current input. The circuit then produces measured values that help construct the next hidden state.

This approach is hybrid because the circuit performs quantum operations while the recurrent state update can remain classical.

19.4.4 What about quantum attention?

Attention is a mechanism that lets a model assign different levels of importance to different parts of an input. In a transformer, an input representation is transformed into query, key and value vectors.

A query represents what a position is looking for. Keys represent what each position offers for matching. Values carry the information that is combined using the attention weights.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dₖ)V

The expression QKᵀ calculates similarity scores between queries and keys. Dividing by the square root of the key dimension helps control the scale of the scores. Softmax converts them into weights, and these weights are applied to the value vectors.

A quantum attention-style model may use quantum circuits to generate feature representations, estimate similarities or implement a trainable transformation inside an attention architecture. Other proposals define quantum analogues of attention using quantum states and measurements.

However, the term quantum attention does not refer to one universally accepted algorithm. Some models retain the ordinary classical attention equation and replace part of its feature-processing pipeline with a quantum circuit. Others investigate more directly quantum formulations.

19.4.5 Quantum circuits inside a transformer

One practical research direction is to replace a small transformation within a transformer with a variational quantum circuit. For example, a circuit might process a compressed representation of each token and return a few measured features. Classical layers can then perform the usual attention calculation.

Input tokens
→
Classical embeddings
→
Quantum feature circuit
↓
Classical attention
→
Feed-forward layers
→
Prediction

Such a design does not make the entire transformer quantum. It is a hybrid architecture with a quantum component.

19.4.6 The main challenges

  • Sequences may contain many tokens or time steps, making repeated circuit execution expensive.
  • Quantum measurements can introduce sampling noise into hidden-state updates and attention scores.
  • Long sequences may require many circuit calls during training.
  • Compressing a high-dimensional token representation into a few qubits may discard useful information.
  • Quantum recurrence and quantum attention must be compared against strong classical baselines, not merely simple neural networks.

19.5 Quantum Graph Neural Networks

19.5.1 What is a graph?

Many real-world problems are not naturally represented as ordinary rows and columns. A social network contains people connected by friendships. A molecule contains atoms connected by chemical bonds. A computer network contains machines linked by communication channels.

A graph represents such a system using nodes and edges. Nodes represent entities, while edges represent relationships between them. Nodes and edges can also have their own features.

A simple graph

The nodes are entities, and the lines show which entities are connected. A graph learning model uses both node features and relationships.

19.5.2 How does a classical graph neural network work?

A classical graph neural network, or GNN, commonly updates a node's representation using information from its neighbours. This process is known as message passing.

mᵥ = AGGREGATE({φ(hᵥ, hᵤ, eᵤᵥ) : u ∈ N(v)})

hᵥ' = UPDATE(hᵥ, mᵥ)

Here, hᵥ is the current representation of node v, N(v) is its set of neighbours, and eᵤᵥ represents an optional edge feature. The model builds messages from neighbouring nodes, aggregates them and updates the representation of the central node.

Repeating this process allows a node to learn from increasingly distant parts of the graph. After several layers, the model can use these representations to classify nodes, predict edges or make predictions about an entire graph.

19.5.3 How can quantum circuits help?

A quantum graph neural network uses quantum circuits within graph representation learning. One design may encode the features of a node and its neighbours into qubits, apply parameterised gates and measure the circuit to generate a learned representation.

Another design may use quantum circuits to calculate similarities between node representations. Quantum kernels can then support graph classification or other prediction tasks.

The circuit architecture must reflect the graph problem. If every node is treated differently without respecting the graph's structure, the model may fail to capture the very relationships that make graph learning useful.

19.5.4 An illustrative quantum message-passing layer

Suppose node A represents a person, while nodes B and C represent two of that person's contacts. Each node has a feature vector. A quantum graph model could encode these features into a circuit and apply trainable operations that combine information from the connected nodes.

After measurement, the resulting numerical features can be combined with the previous node representation using a classical update function. The process can then be repeated across the graph.

Node features
→
Encode node information
→
Quantum circuit
↓
Measurement
→
Aggregate neighbour information
→
Updated node embedding

This is one possible architecture, not a definition that every quantum GNN must follow. The exact implementation depends on how nodes, edges and their features are encoded.

19.5.5 Where could quantum GNNs be useful?

  • Molecular modelling: Molecules naturally form graphs, and some molecular problems also involve quantum chemistry.
  • Material science: Researchers may study relationships between the structure of a material and its properties.
  • Network analysis: Graphs can represent communication networks, transport systems or social relationships.
  • Quantum systems: Graphs can describe relationships between interacting physical components.

These are areas where graph learning or quantum computation may be relevant. They are not proof that a quantum GNN currently outperforms classical graph neural networks in those applications.

19.6 Dissipative and Measurement-Based QNNs

So far, we have mainly considered models in which trainable gates transform a quantum state and the final state is measured. Other approaches change how the state is processed, including dissipative quantum models and measurement-based quantum computation.

19.6.1 What does dissipative mean?

In everyday language, dissipation means that energy or another physical quantity is dispersed into the surroundings. In quantum computing, an open quantum system can interact with its environment. Its evolution may no longer be described by a unitary operation on the system alone.

A unitary gate transforms a pure quantum state reversibly. Dissipative evolution can instead involve effects such as relaxation, decoherence or engineered interactions with an environment. These effects may normally be unwanted noise, but carefully controlled dissipation can also be used as part of a computational model.

19.6.2 Quantum states and density matrices

A pure quantum state is often represented by a state vector. When a system is mixed, or when we do not have complete information about its state, a density matrix is useful.

ρ = Σᵢ pᵢ |ψᵢ⟩⟨ψᵢ|

pᵢ ≥ 0, Σᵢ pᵢ = 1

This expression represents a mixture of possible pure states. Each state |ψᵢ⟩ has a probability pᵢ. A density matrix contains the information needed to calculate measurement statistics for the mixed state.

A general open-system evolution can be represented by a quantum channel:

ρ' = E(ρ)

Here, E is a map that transforms the input density matrix into an output density matrix. A physically valid quantum channel is completely positive and trace-preserving. In a dissipative QNN, the trainable model may be built around such channels rather than only around unitary gates.

19.6.3 How can dissipation support learning?

Some dissipative models are designed to drive the system towards a desired steady state. A steady state is one that remains unchanged under the relevant evolution.

Trainable interactions can be chosen so that the final state depends on the input and model parameters. Measurements of this state provide features or predictions. Learning then adjusts the parameters so that the measured outputs better match the target.

The important idea is that the system's evolution itself can perform part of the information-processing task. The environment is not always merely a nuisance; in engineered models, its interaction with the system can be part of the design.

19.6.4 What is measurement-based quantum computing?

Measurement-based quantum computing is a model of quantum computation in which a highly entangled resource state is prepared first. Computation is then carried out by measuring individual qubits in chosen bases. Later measurement choices may depend on earlier measurement outcomes.

This differs from the familiar circuit model, where computation is usually described as a sequence of gates applied to qubits. Both models can implement quantum computations, but their operations are organised differently.

In measurement-based QNN proposals, trainable measurement bases, adaptive measurement patterns or the preparation of the resource state can provide the model's adjustable components.

Prepare entangled resource
→
Choose measurement settings
→
Measure qubits
→
Classical output

19.6.5 Why are these models important?

They broaden the range of possible quantum learning architectures. A model does not have to be restricted to the most familiar sequence of unitary gates. Open-system dynamics and measurement patterns offer different ways to build trainable transformations.

However, these approaches can be challenging to implement. Environmental effects must be controlled or modelled correctly, and measurement-based systems may require carefully prepared entangled resource states. Their practical value depends on the application, available hardware and training method.

19.7 Hybrid Classical-Quantum Neural Networks

19.7.1 Why combine classical and quantum computation?

Modern neural networks require many operations, including data processing, matrix multiplication, activation functions, loss calculation and parameter optimisation. Classical computers are excellent at these tasks and have highly developed software libraries.

Current quantum processors have a different set of capabilities and limitations. They can execute quantum circuits, but they are not a general replacement for GPUs or CPUs. A practical approach is to assign a limited part of the model to a quantum circuit while leaving the remaining operations classical.

Such a model is called a hybrid classical-quantum neural network. It combines classical trainable layers with one or more parameterised quantum circuits.

19.7.2 The architecture of a hybrid network

Raw input
→
Classical preprocessing
→
Classical feature layer
↓
Encode into qubits
→
Quantum circuit
→
Measurements
↓
Classical output layer
→
Prediction and loss

The classical preprocessing layer reduces or transforms the input into a representation that can be encoded by the quantum circuit. The circuit transforms that representation and returns measured features. A classical output layer converts those features into the final prediction.

19.7.3 Why not send the entire dataset directly to the quantum circuit?

The number of qubits available on a processor is limited, and loading a large classical dataset into a quantum state is not automatically free. A hybrid model may therefore use a classical network to compress the data before encoding it into a smaller quantum system.

This creates a design question: which part of the computation should be quantum? The answer should come from the problem's structure and evidence about the model, not from the assumption that any quantum layer must improve performance.

19.7.4 The hybrid training loop

Training a hybrid network follows a feedback process. First, the classical layers transform the input. The quantum circuit executes using its current parameters, and measurements provide numerical outputs. The remaining classical layers produce a prediction and calculate the loss.

Gradients are then calculated for the trainable parameters. The classical optimiser updates them, and the next training step begins.

θ* = arg minθ L(y, f(x; θ))

θ ← θ - η ∇θ L

Here, L is the loss function, y is the correct target, f is the hybrid model, θ represents its trainable parameters, and η is the learning rate. The gradient indicates how the loss changes with the parameters.

The quantum circuit may contribute gradients through a differentiable simulator, a parameter-shift calculation or another supported differentiation method. The exact method depends on the circuit operations and software interface.

19.7.5 Advantages of a hybrid design

  • It can reuse established classical machine learning libraries.
  • It allows a small quantum circuit to be tested without requiring an entirely quantum software stack.
  • Classical layers can perform preprocessing and complex output tasks.
  • It is suitable for research into how quantum features interact with classical representations.
  • It can be developed using simulators before attempting execution on quantum hardware.

19.7.6 The costs of a hybrid design

Every quantum circuit execution has a cost. If the training process needs thousands of circuit evaluations for one optimiser step, training may become slow. On real hardware, finite-shot measurements can make the estimated output and gradient noisy.

Communication between classical software and a quantum processor can also add latency. A model that works quickly on a simulator may be much slower on hardware. Consequently, total end-to-end training time matters more than the number of quantum gates alone.

Example: a hybrid model for classifying measurements

Suppose each record contains four numerical sensor readings. A classical layer first normalises the readings. A quantum circuit then encodes the four values into four qubits and applies trainable rotations and entangling gates.

Measurements return four expectation values. A classical output layer uses these values to predict one of two classes. The loss is calculated against the correct labels, and the optimiser updates both the classical weights and the quantum circuit parameters.

This architecture is small enough to study on a simulator. Whether it is useful for a real sensor problem would require experiments against suitable classical models.

19.8 Hands-on: Hybrid Networks with PyTorch and PennyLane

We will now build a small hybrid neural network. The goal is to understand how classical inputs travel through a quantum circuit, how measurements become outputs, and how training updates the circuit parameters.

We will use Python, PyTorch and PennyLane. PennyLane provides tools for defining quantum circuits as differentiable functions and connecting them to classical machine learning frameworks.

19.8.1 Install the required libraries

pip install pennylane torch scikit-learn

These packages provide the quantum circuit interface, the classical neural network framework and a simple dataset generator. The example below uses a simulator, so a physical quantum processor is not required.

19.8.2 What will we build?

The model will classify two-dimensional points into two categories. A classical linear layer transforms each input into two values. These values are encoded into two qubits. A trainable quantum circuit processes the state and returns two measurement expectation values. A final classical layer converts those measurements into class scores.

19.8.3 Complete example

import pennylane as qml
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.optim as optim

from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader, TensorDataset

# Reproducible classical initialisation.
torch.manual_seed(7)

# -------------------------------------------------
# 1. Create a small binary classification dataset
# -------------------------------------------------

X, y = make_moons(
    n_samples=300,
    noise=0.15,
    random_state=7
)

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X,
    y,
    test_size=0.25,
    random_state=7,
    stratify=y
)

scaler = StandardScaler()
X_train = scaler.fit_transform(X_train)
X_test = scaler.transform(X_test)

X_train = torch.tensor(X_train, dtype=torch.float32)
X_test = torch.tensor(X_test, dtype=torch.float32)

y_train = torch.tensor(y_train, dtype=torch.long)
y_test = torch.tensor(y_test, dtype=torch.long)

train_loader = DataLoader(
    TensorDataset(X_train, y_train),
    batch_size=16,
    shuffle=True
)

# -------------------------------------------------
# 2. Define a two-qubit quantum device
# -------------------------------------------------

n_qubits = 2
dev = qml.device("default.qubit", wires=n_qubits)

# -------------------------------------------------
# 3. Define the parameterised quantum circuit
# -------------------------------------------------

@qml.qnode(dev, interface="torch", diff_method="backprop")
def quantum_circuit(inputs, weights):
    # Encode two classical features.
    qml.RY(inputs[0], wires=0)
    qml.RY(inputs[1], wires=1)

    # Trainable single-qubit transformations.
    qml.RY(weights[0], wires=0)
    qml.RY(weights[1], wires=1)

    # Couple the qubits.
    qml.CNOT(wires=[0, 1])

    # Additional trainable transformations.
    qml.RY(weights[2], wires=0)
    qml.RY(weights[3], wires=1)

    # Return classical expectation values.
    return (
        qml.expval(qml.PauliZ(0)),
        qml.expval(qml.PauliZ(1))
    )

# -------------------------------------------------
# 4. Wrap the circuit in a PyTorch layer
# -------------------------------------------------

weight_shapes = {"weights": (4,)}

quantum_layer = qml.qnn.TorchLayer(
    quantum_circuit,
    weight_shapes
)

# -------------------------------------------------
# 5. Build the hybrid neural network
# -------------------------------------------------

class HybridQNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()

        self.preprocess = nn.Linear(2, 2)
        self.quantum = quantum_layer
        self.classifier = nn.Linear(2, 2)

    def forward(self, x):
        # Map classical inputs into circuit angles.
        x = torch.tanh(self.preprocess(x)) * torch.pi

        # PennyLane evaluates the circuit for each input.
        x = self.quantum(x)

        # Convert measured features into class logits.
        return self.classifier(x)

model = HybridQNN()

# -------------------------------------------------
# 6. Choose the loss and optimiser
# -------------------------------------------------

criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()

optimizer = optim.Adam(
    model.parameters(),
    lr=0.02
)

# -------------------------------------------------
# 7. Train the hybrid model
# -------------------------------------------------

epochs = 15

for epoch in range(epochs):
    model.train()
    total_loss = 0.0

    for features, labels in train_loader:
        optimizer.zero_grad()

        logits = model(features)
        loss = criterion(logits, labels)

        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()

        total_loss += loss.item() * len(labels)

    average_loss = total_loss / len(X_train)

    print(
        f"Epoch {epoch + 1:02d} | "
        f"Loss: {average_loss:.4f}"
    )

# -------------------------------------------------
# 8. Evaluate on unseen examples
# -------------------------------------------------

model.eval()

with torch.no_grad():
    logits = model(X_test)
    predictions = logits.argmax(dim=1)
    accuracy = (predictions == y_test).float().mean()

print(f"Test accuracy: {accuracy.item():.3f}")

19.8.4 Understanding the code, step by step

Step 1: Create a dataset

The make_moons function generates two classes of points shaped roughly like two interlocking moons. It is a useful small classification problem because the classes are not separated by a single straight line in the original two-dimensional input space.

We split the dataset into training and testing portions. The scaler learns its mean and standard deviation from the training inputs only, then applies the same transformation to the test inputs. This avoids leaking information from the test set into preprocessing.

Step 2: Create a quantum device

The line qml.device("default.qubit", wires=2) creates a state-vector simulator with two qubits. A simulator represents the circuit mathematically and allows us to study its behaviour without physical hardware.

A two-qubit state vector has four complex amplitudes. In general, a pure state of n qubits is described by 2ⁿ complex amplitudes, which is why simulating large quantum systems becomes demanding.

Step 3: Encode the data

The RY gates rotate the qubits by angles supplied by the input. The input features are first transformed by the classical layer and scaled into a bounded interval.

Angle encoding is straightforward, but the numerical values must be mapped sensibly to rotation angles. Different encodings can change the functions the circuit can represent.

Step 4: Introduce trainable parameters

The four values in weights control the trainable rotations. During training, the optimiser adjusts them. The CNOT gate couples the two qubits, allowing the circuit to create entanglement for suitable input states.

Notice that the circuit structure itself stays fixed. Learning changes the parameters inside that structure.

Step 5: Measure the qubits

The circuit returns the expectation values of Pauli-Z for both qubits. Each value is between -1 and +1. The simulator calculates these values from the state vector.

On real hardware, the same expectation values are usually estimated from repeated measurements. Finite-shot estimates fluctuate, so the training process may be noisier.

Step 6: Combine quantum and classical layers

The TorchLayer object exposes the quantum circuit as a PyTorch-compatible layer. Its output is a tensor containing the measured features. The final linear layer converts those features into two class logits.

A logit is an unnormalised class score. PyTorch's CrossEntropyLoss combines the logits and target labels to calculate the classification loss.

Step 7: Calculate gradients and update parameters

The call to loss.backward() asks PyTorch's automatic differentiation system to calculate gradients. PennyLane connects the quantum layer to the differentiation mechanism specified by the QNode. The simulator used here supports backpropagation through its state-vector computation.

The call to optimizer.step() updates the trainable parameters using Adam. This includes the classical layers and the quantum layer.

The precise differentiation method supported depends on the device, circuit operations and software version. On quantum hardware, a common alternative is the parameter-shift rule, which estimates derivatives using additional circuit evaluations.

19.8.5 What results should you expect?

The code prints the average training loss for each epoch and the test accuracy at the end. The exact values depend on the software environment, initial parameters and optimisation behaviour.

Do not assume that one successful run proves that the quantum circuit is useful. The appropriate next step is to repeat the experiment with different random seeds and compare the model against a classical neural network trained on the same splits.

19.8.6 Common implementation issues

  • Tensor shape errors: Check the batch dimension and ensure that each example has the expected number of features.
  • Version differences: PennyLane and PyTorch interfaces can change. If an argument or differentiation method is unsupported, consult the installed version's documentation.
  • Slow training: A quantum circuit can be evaluated repeatedly during gradient computation. Start with a small dataset and a small circuit.
  • Unexpected gradients: Confirm that the circuit parameters are trainable and that the selected device supports the differentiation method.
  • Poor accuracy: Inspect preprocessing, learning rate, circuit depth and model capacity before assuming that the quantum approach is inherently unsuitable.

19.9 Training Quantum Neural Networks: The Deeper Picture

Understanding the architecture is only half the job. A neural network must also be trainable. QNNs face several issues that arise from the mathematics of quantum circuits and the practical limitations of measurement.

19.9.1 The loss function

A loss function measures how far a model's predictions are from the desired answers. For regression, a common choice is mean squared error. For classification, cross-entropy is widely used when the model provides class scores or probabilities.

MSE = (1/N) Σᵢ (yᵢ - ŷᵢ)²

Here, yᵢ is the correct target, ŷᵢ is the prediction, and N is the number of examples. A training algorithm attempts to reduce the loss over the training data while maintaining useful performance on unseen examples.

19.9.2 Parameter-shift gradients

Classical neural networks often use backpropagation to calculate gradients. For suitable parameterised quantum gates, derivatives can be calculated using a method called the parameter-shift rule.

For a common gate parameter whose generator has eigenvalues of equal magnitude, the derivative of an expectation value takes the form:

∂f(θ)/∂θ = [f(θ + π/2) - f(θ - π/2)] / 2

The formula applies to the relevant class of parameterised gates and observables; it is not a universal derivative rule for every possible quantum operation. More general cases may require different shifts or additional terms.

The practical idea is simple: evaluate the circuit at shifted parameter values and combine the outputs to estimate the derivative. On hardware, these evaluations require circuit executions and measurements, which can make gradient calculation expensive.

19.9.3 Why can gradients become difficult?

In some parameterised circuit families, especially sufficiently random circuits with certain properties, gradients can become extremely small as the system grows. This behaviour is associated with a phenomenon called a barren plateau.

When gradients are tiny, changing the parameters produces only a very small estimated improvement. Optimisation can then become difficult, and measurement noise may hide the useful gradient signal.

Barren plateaus do not affect every QNN equally. Their occurrence depends on the circuit structure, initialisation, cost function, problem and other conditions.

19.9.4 Techniques that may help

  • Begin with shallow circuits and increase depth only when needed.
  • Use circuit structures that reflect the problem rather than choosing gates entirely at random.
  • Choose parameter initialisation methods that avoid immediately uninformative regions where possible.
  • Measure training behaviour and gradient sizes instead of relying only on the final loss.
  • Compare different data encodings and circuit designs.
  • Use suitable classical preprocessing to avoid loading unnecessarily large inputs into the quantum circuit.

19.9.5 Generalisation and overfitting

A QNN can overfit just like a classical neural network. It may learn patterns that work well on the training examples but fail on new data. A quantum circuit is not protected from overfitting merely because it uses quantum states.

Training and test splits, validation data, regularisation, early stopping and repeated experiments are still important. For noisy hardware, it is also necessary to distinguish genuine learning behaviour from statistical variation caused by measurements.

19.10 Challenges and Realistic Expectations

Quantum neural networks are an active research area. They offer interesting mathematical structures and possible ways to use quantum computation for learning. However, their potential should be assessed carefully.

19.10.1 The data-loading problem

A classical dataset must be represented in a form that the quantum circuit can process. If the dataset contains many features, encoding them can require substantial resources. A compact quantum state does not automatically make the process of loading arbitrary classical data inexpensive.

19.10.2 Measurement costs

Quantum measurements return individual outcomes. Expectation values are usually estimated from multiple repetitions. More repetitions generally improve statistical precision, but they also require more circuit executions.

If a model requires many expectation values and many gradient evaluations, the total number of circuit runs can grow quickly.

19.10.3 Noise and limited hardware

Real quantum processors have imperfect gates, measurement errors and finite coherence times. Long circuits can accumulate errors. Hardware constraints may also limit the number of qubits and the interactions that can be performed efficiently.

Simulators avoid many hardware errors but can become expensive as the number of qubits increases. A successful simulation therefore does not guarantee that the same model will train efficiently on a physical processor.

19.10.4 Expressivity is not the same as usefulness

A circuit may represent a complicated family of functions. That does not automatically mean it can learn the correct function from a limited dataset. Training difficulty, generalisation, measurement cost and the structure of the target problem all matter.

A model that is highly expressive but difficult to optimise may be less useful than a smaller model that trains reliably.

19.10.5 What would count as a meaningful quantum advantage?

A meaningful advantage would require a well-defined task on which the quantum approach offers a convincing benefit over suitable classical alternatives. Depending on the task, that benefit might concern runtime, sample complexity, memory, prediction quality or another clearly defined measure.

A convincing evaluation must include the cost of preparing inputs, running the quantum circuit, estimating measurements, training parameters and processing outputs. It must also use competitive classical baselines and comparable experimental conditions.

What should you conclude?

QNNs are not guaranteed replacements for classical neural networks. At present, their strongest role is often in research: exploring trainable quantum models, studying quantum data, developing new circuit architectures and testing whether particular problems benefit from quantum computation.

Learning to build small QNNs is valuable even without immediate practical advantage. It teaches you how quantum states, measurement, optimisation and machine learning interact.

19.11 Chapter Summary

A quantum neural network is a trainable model that uses quantum circuits to transform information and generate measurable outputs. Its parameters are adjusted through a learning algorithm, much as weights are adjusted in a classical neural network.

A quantum perceptron provides a simple way to study the idea of a trainable quantum model. QCNNs introduce hierarchical processing and pooling-like operations. Quantum recurrent architectures address sequential information, while attention-style approaches explore quantum components in similarity calculations and feature processing. Quantum GNNs investigate how circuits can support learning on structured relationships.

Dissipative and measurement-based models broaden the possible ways to perform quantum information processing. Hybrid classical-quantum networks combine these quantum components with classical layers and optimisers, making them practical starting points for experimentation.

PennyLane allows quantum circuits to be connected to frameworks such as PyTorch and, in supported configurations, TensorFlow. A small hybrid model can be trained on a simulator, giving you an opportunity to understand data encoding, circuit parameters, measurements, gradients and optimisation.

The central idea

Data → Quantum representation → Trainable circuit → Measurement → Prediction → Loss → Parameter update

The important question is not whether a model contains a quantum circuit. The important question is whether that circuit helps solve a well-defined learning problem under realistic computational and hardware constraints.
