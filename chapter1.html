Chapter 1: Why Quantum? The Big Picture

People keep saying "quantum computers will change everything". Some of that is true and a lot of it is hype. This chapter helps you tell the two apart.

Numbers in 1.5 (qubit counts, records, dates) change every few months. Treat them as a snapshot and check the latest before using them in a project or exam.

1.1 Limits of classical computing

A "classical computer" is any everyday computer: your phone, your laptop, the server behind a website. It stores information as bits, each either 0 or 1.

The good years are slowing down

For about 60 years, computers got faster and cheaper because engineers kept shrinking transistors. Moore's Law says the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. That ride is slowing:

  • Transistors are now only a few nanometres wide, a few dozen atoms. Electrons leak through walls meant to stop them (quantum tunnelling).
  • Chips get too hot to pack tighter.
  • Clock speeds have been stuck near 3 to 5 GHz for almost twenty years, so makers add more cores and GPUs instead. These help only when a job splits into independent pieces.

But this is not the main reason for quantum computing. A cleverer chip design might fix a size problem. The real reason goes deeper.

The real problem: doubling again and again

Remember the chessboard and rice grains story? One grain on square one, two on square two, four on square three, doubling each time. By square 64 you need more rice than has ever been grown. That is exponential growth.

Kind of growthWhat happens
Polynomial (n × n)Double the input, work becomes 4 times. Manageable.
Exponential (2ⁿ)Add just one more item and the work doubles.

Even a computer a million times faster handles only about 20 more items before it is stuck again.

Where it bites: simulating nature

To describe n quantum particles on a normal computer, you must store about 2ⁿ numbers.

ParticlesNumbers neededVerdict
101,024Tiny file
30about 1 billion (≈16 GB)Fits on a good laptop
50about 10¹⁵Beyond almost every machine
300about 2 × 10⁹⁰The universe has only ~10⁸⁰ atoms

Yet a real molecule with a few hundred electrons exists happily in nature. Nature is not "calculating"; it just does what it does. That observation is where quantum computing begins.

Problems we would love to solve

  • Molecules and materials: new medicines, batteries and catalysts. The enzyme that turns nitrogen from air into fertiliser is too complex to simulate classically.
  • Codes: online banking relies on multiplying two huge primes being easy but factoring the result being brutally hard.
  • Search through huge unsorted collections, and some optimisation such as best delivery routes.

A little complexity theory

  • P: problems a normal computer solves quickly.
  • NP: problems whose answer you can check quickly, even if finding it is hard (a sudoku).
  • BQP: problems a quantum computer solves quickly, with a tiny chance of error that repetition shrinks.

Whether BQP is truly bigger than what normal computers do quickly is not proved, but Shor's algorithm is strong evidence. The Extended Church-Turing Thesis says any real computer can be imitated efficiently by an ordinary one. Quantum computing is its biggest challenge.

1.2 A brief history: Feynman, Deutsch, Shor, Grover

Around 1980, Paul Benioff and Yuri Manin started wondering about quantum computing. Rolf Landauer said "information is physical": a bit lives in a real object, so physics decides what you can do with it. Charles Bennett showed computing can in principle be reversible, which suits quantum physics, where nothing is lost.

  • Feynman, 1981. If simulating quantum physics on a normal computer explodes in memory, build the computer from quantum parts. In one line: to imitate nature, use nature. This is the quantum simulator, still the most believable use.
  • Deutsch, 1985. Described a universal quantum computer, the way a laptop can run any program. His puzzle: a black box is either constant or balanced. A normal computer may need over half of all inputs to be sure; a quantum one asks once. Useless in real life, but it showed interference can reveal a "big picture" property (Deutsch-Jozsa, with Jozsa in 1992).
  • Bernstein-Vazirani 1993, Simon 1994. More proven speed-ups. Simon's was exponential and inspired Shor.
  • Shor, 1994. A quantum method for factoring and discrete logarithms, the problems guarding internet security. It turns factoring into finding a repeating pattern, which the Quantum Fourier Transform does well: wrong answers cancel and the right pattern stands out. The best classical method is sub-exponential; Shor is polynomial.
  • Grover, 1996. Searches an unsorted list with a square-root speed-up. For 1,000,000 entries: about 500,000 classical checks versus around 800 for Grover. Proven optimal, and not exponential. It halves effective key strength, so AES-256 behaves like 128-bit and stays safe.

Other pieces that made it believable

  • Quantum error correction (Shor 1995, Steane 1996): possible even though quantum states cannot be copied.
  • Threshold theorem (late 1990s): below a certain error rate, arbitrarily long computations are possible at manageable cost.
  • Seth Lloyd, 1996: proved Feynman right about simulation.
  • Later: HHL for linear equations (2008), and VQE and QAOA (2014) for today's imperfect machines.

1.3 What quantum computers can (and cannot) do

Where the power comes from

A qubit can be a mixture of 0 and 1, like a coin spinning in the air. Three ideas work together:

  • Superposition: n qubits hold a blend of all 2ⁿ bit patterns.
  • Entanglement: qubits linked so that only the whole group has a complete description.
  • Interference: each possibility has an amplitude, and amplitudes add or cancel like waves. Noise-cancelling headphones use a similar idea.

The biggest myth: "A quantum computer tries every answer at once and picks the right one." Wrong. Measuring gives just one random answer. The real skill is arranging steps so wrong answers cancel and the right one becomes likely. Without that, superposition gives nothing.

Expected to helpNot expected or not known to help
Simulating molecules, materials and particles (huge speed-up)Solving the hardest NP problems quickly; Grover's square root still leaves exponential time
Factoring and discrete logs (huge, needs a big error-corrected machine)Everyday tasks like YouTube, WhatsApp or Word. Quantum machines will be add-on specialists, like a GPU
Search, amplitude estimation, some Monte Carlo (square-root speed-up)"Big data": loading data is slow, and n qubits return at most n bits
Hidden-pattern problems, a few questions about knotsGuaranteed better AI: Ewin Tang (2018) matched a celebrated quantum ML speed-up with a classical algorithm
Made-up sampling tasks that prove a point but are not usefulCopying unknown states (no-cloning), faster-than-light messages, or ignoring noise (decoherence)

Think of a Formula 1 car: wonderful on a race track, a poor choice for buying vegetables.

Why it still matters

  • Security: Shor could break RSA and elliptic curves, so the world is moving to post-quantum cryptography. NIST published its first standards in August 2024. "Harvest now, decrypt later" means someone can record data today and unlock it years later, so long-lived secrets need protection early.
  • Chemistry and materials: fertilisers, batteries, solar materials, drugs.
  • Finance and logistics: possible gains, but the least certain area.

1.4 Quantum advantage vs. quantum supremacy

  • Supremacy (John Preskill, 2012): a quantum device does some task no normal computer can finish in reasonable time. It need not be useful.
  • Advantage: a quantum device beats the best normal method (faster, cheaper, more accurate, less energy), ideally on something people care about.

Many scientists now avoid "supremacy" because of its overtones and because it suggests a final victory. "Advantage" or "beyond-classical" is preferred.

The famous claims

  • Google Sycamore (2019): 53 qubits, about 200 seconds, versus an estimated 10,000 years classically. IBM replied about 2.5 days with clever disk use, and later tensor-network methods cut it further.
  • China's Jiuzhang (from 2020): photon-based boson sampling.
  • Google (2024-25): newer Willow experiments, including 2025's "Quantum Echoes", described as a verifiable advantage on a physics task.

Each claim is followed by classical scientists trying to beat it. That is how science stays honest. An advantage claim compares against the best known classical method at that moment, so it is a snapshot, not a trophy.

What makes a claim convincing?

  • A clearly defined task with a clear success measure.
  • Solid theory, not just a benchmark, saying classical computers will struggle.
  • Checkability. Factoring is easy to check (multiply back); random circuit sampling is very hard to check.
  • Usefulness: someone should care about the answer.
  • A fair fight against the best classical algorithm and hardware, including cost and energy.

1.5 The current state of the field (NISQ era and beyond)

In 2018, Preskill named today's period NISQ: Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum. "Intermediate" means tens to a few thousand qubits. "Noisy" means each operation errs about 0.1% to 1%, so after a few hundred operations errors wash out the answer.

Qubit typeGoodBad
Superconducting (Google, IBM, Rigetti)Very fast, chip-making methodsCooled to ~10-15 millikelvin; limited connections
Trapped ions (Quantinuum, IonQ)Very accurate; any ion talks to any otherSlower; hard to scale
Neutral atoms (QuEra, Pasqal, Atom Computing, Infleqtion)Hundreds to thousands in a rearrangeable gridSlower cycles; atoms get lost
Photonic (PsiQuantum, Xanadu, USTC)Room-temperature parts; good for networkingPhoton loss; probabilistic operations
Silicon spin (Intel)Uses existing chip factoriesEarly stage; hard to make identical
Topological (Microsoft)Naturally error-protected, if it works"Majorana 1" (2025) evidence still debated

There is no clear winner. Headlines shout qubit counts, but that is like judging a cricket team by the number of players. What matters is two-qubit accuracy, coherence time, connectivity, usable program length, readout quality and, above all, the quality of logical (error-corrected) qubits. 100 excellent qubits can beat 1,000 poor ones.

What runs on NISQ machines

  • VQE for molecular energies and QAOA for optimisation, both hybrid loops between a quantum chip and a normal computer.
  • Error mitigation: run circuits many times and process results cleverly to reduce noise, without extra qubits.

Honestly, no NISQ algorithm has yet shown a clear, widely accepted practical win. Training can also hit "barren plateaus", flat regions where the learning signal vanishes.

Beyond NISQ: fault tolerance

The goal is error correction: spread one logical qubit across many physical qubits, like writing a message several times so mistakes can be fixed. The surface code needs error rates below about 1% and hundreds to thousands of physical qubits per logical one.

  • December 2024: Google's 105-qubit Willow chip showed logical error falling as the code grew (distance 3, 5, 7). This "below threshold" result is what theory promised.
  • Quantinuum, IBM, and Harvard with QuEra also report progress in logical qubits, fast decoding and quantum LDPC codes.
  • Roadmaps promise fault-tolerant machines around 2029-2030. Treat these as goals; dates tend to slip.

How big a machine do we need?

  • Breaking RSA-2048: about a billion physical qubits in 2012 estimates; a 2025 estimate by Craig Gidney says under a million noisy qubits for under a week. Today's chips have hundreds to a few thousand.
  • The nitrogen-fixing enzyme: thousands of logical qubits and enormous numbers of operations.

India's place

The National Quantum Mission (approved 2023, about Rs 6,000 crore through 2030-31) covers computing, communication, sensing and materials, with hubs at top institutions. You need no lab to start: IBM, Amazon Braket and Azure Quantum offer cloud access, and Qiskit, Cirq and PennyLane are free.

Reading quantum news sensibly: What was the task? What was the error rate? Physical or logical qubits? Was the classical method fairly tuned? Has anyone repeated it? Press releases often skip these.

Quick recap

  1. Classical computers are limited not just by chip size. Some problems blow up exponentially and faster hardware cannot fix that.
  2. Feynman: use quantum to simulate quantum. Deutsch gave the model. Shor and Grover showed real power.
  3. Quantum speed-ups are real but specific. No trying all answers at once, and no cracking the hardest NP problems.
  4. "Supremacy" often means a made-up task. A useful, checkable advantage is the harder goal.
  5. We are in the NISQ era, moving slowly to error-corrected machines.
  6. Because of "harvest now, decrypt later", prepare for post-quantum security today.
Figures reflect a 2025-26 snapshot. Verify before quoting.
