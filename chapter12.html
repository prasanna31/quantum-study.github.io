Quantum Computing · Chapter 12

Noise, Error Mitigation, and Error Correction

A quantum computer does not work with perfectly isolated qubits. Its surroundings disturb quantum states, its control operations are imperfect, and its measurements can be wrong. This chapter explains how we identify these errors, reduce their effects, and build quantum computers that can eventually compute reliably despite them.

The central problem

Imagine writing an important answer on a whiteboard while somebody continuously shakes the room. Even if your calculation is correct, the information may become difficult to read. Quantum computing faces a similar problem, except the disturbance comes from interactions with the physical environment and imperfections in the hardware. In classical computing, a bit is designed to represent either 0 or 1. A small electrical disturbance often does not change its interpreted value because digital circuits use voltage ranges to represent the two states. Quantum information is more delicate. A qubit can exist in a superposition, and the relative phase between its components matters. Small disturbances can damage this information even when the final measurement still produces an apparently valid 0 or 1.

A quantum computation can be mathematically correct and still produce an unreliable answer because of physical errors.

The important distinction in this chapter is that error mitigation reduces the effect of errors on the answers we estimate, whereas error correction protects quantum information by detecting errors and correcting them without directly measuring the encoded information itself. Both are useful, but they solve different problems.

12.1 Sources of noise

12.1.1 What does noise mean in a quantum computer?

Noise is any unwanted physical effect that changes the state of a qubit, makes a gate behave incorrectly, or causes the measurement to report the wrong result. An ideal quantum gate performs exactly the mathematical operation specified by the algorithm. A real gate is implemented using physical controls such as microwave pulses, laser beams, or interactions between particles. These controls are never perfectly precise. There are several important sources of noise.

1. Relaxation

A qubit in an excited state can lose energy to its surroundings and move towards its lower-energy state. This can destroy stored quantum information.

Energy loss

2. Dephasing

The relative phase between the components of a superposition changes unpredictably. The qubit may lose its useful quantum interference even without an energy transition.

Phase disturbance

3. Gate errors

A gate rotates a qubit by a slightly incorrect angle, acts at the wrong time, or introduces an unwanted interaction.

Control imperfections

4. Readout errors

The hardware measures the qubit but reports 1 when the state was 0, or reports 0 when the state was 1.

Measurement mistakes

5. Crosstalk

Operating one qubit unintentionally affects another qubit. This becomes especially important when many operations occur in parallel.

Unwanted coupling

6. Leakage

A physical device may have energy levels beyond the two levels chosen to represent a qubit. An operation can accidentally move the system outside those two computational states.

Outside the qubit space

12.1.2 Relaxation and dephasing are different

Consider a qubit prepared in the state:
|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

Here, α and β are complex probability amplitudes, with |α|² + |β|² = 1.

The qubit contains information in both the populations of the two basis states and their relative phase. During relaxation, energy may be lost from the |1⟩ state to the environment. The system can transition towards |0⟩. This is similar to a swinging object gradually losing energy and coming to rest. During dephasing, the qubit can retain its energy populations while losing the predictable relationship between the phases of |0⟩ and |1⟩. A useful analogy is two waves that initially rise and fall together but gradually drift out of step. Their interference pattern becomes less predictable. These errors matter because quantum algorithms rely on controlled interference. If the phase relationships are damaged, amplitudes that should reinforce each other may no longer do so reliably.

Important distinction

Noise is not simply the same as a wrong answer. A qubit may experience noise during a computation even when a particular measurement happens to return the expected value. Conversely, a single unexpected measurement does not always prove that a gate was faulty; quantum measurements are probabilistic by design.

12.1.3 Quantum noise as a mathematical process

An ideal quantum operation is represented by a unitary operator U:
|ψ⟩ → U|ψ⟩
In the real world, the state may instead undergo a noisy quantum channel. For a general mixed state represented by a density matrix ρ, a common mathematical description is:
ρ → E(ρ) = Σᵢ Kᵢ ρ Kᵢ†

The operators Kᵢ describe possible effects of the noise and satisfy Σᵢ Kᵢ†Kᵢ = I for a trace-preserving channel.

Do not worry if density matrices and these operators are unfamiliar. The main idea is simple: instead of assuming that the computer performs one perfectly known operation, we describe the range of possible outcomes produced by the physical process. For example, a bit-flip channel changes |0⟩ into |1⟩ and |1⟩ into |0⟩ with probability p. Its effect can be written as:
E(ρ) = (1 − p)ρ + pXρX
Here, X is the Pauli-X gate, which exchanges |0⟩ and |1⟩. With probability 1 − p, the state remains unchanged; with probability p, the bit-flip operation occurs. This is a simplified model. Real devices can have several kinds of errors, and their noise may depend on the qubits, gates, timing, temperature, calibration, and other operating conditions.

12.1.4 Why quantum noise is particularly difficult

A classical computer can often copy a bit, compare copies, and replace a corrupted value with the majority value. Quantum information cannot be protected by simply making identical copies of an arbitrary unknown qubit. The reason is the no-cloning theorem: an unknown quantum state cannot be copied perfectly by a universal physical operation. Suppose a qubit is in an unknown state:
|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩
We cannot build a general-purpose machine that takes this state and produces two independent, perfect copies of |ψ⟩ for every possible α and β. Measuring the qubit directly is not a solution either. Measurement can disturb a superposition and remove information needed by the algorithm. Quantum error correction gets around this difficulty by encoding one logical qubit into an entangled state of several physical qubits. It detects information about the error, not the full unknown logical state.
Connection to the rest of quantum computing: Superposition and interference make quantum algorithms powerful. Noise damages the phase relationships and correlations that these algorithms use. Error protection must therefore preserve quantum information without directly reading out the unknown state.

12.2 Characterising noise: T1, T2, and randomized benchmarking

Before reducing errors, we must measure them. This is called characterisation. It helps engineers determine which qubits are unreliable, how quickly coherence disappears, and how accurately gates operate. Three widely used concepts are T1, T2, and randomized benchmarking.

12.2.1 T1: the energy-relaxation time

T1 describes how quickly a qubit loses energy from its excited state. Imagine preparing a qubit in |1⟩ and allowing it to wait. Because of relaxation, the probability of finding it in |1⟩ gradually decreases. Under a simple exponential relaxation model:
P₁(t) = P₁(0)e−t/T₁
Here:
  • P₁(0) is the initial excited-state population.
  • P₁(t) is the excited-state population after time t.
  • T₁ is the energy-relaxation time.
After one T1, the initial excess excited-state population has fallen to approximately 36.8% of its initial value under this model.

Example

Suppose a qubit has T1 = 100 microseconds and begins in |1⟩. Ignoring other effects, the excited-state population remaining after 100 microseconds is approximately:
e−1 ≈ 0.368
If the initial excited-state population was 100%, approximately 36.8% remains after that interval.
A longer T1 is generally desirable because it means the qubit retains its excitation for a longer time. However, T1 alone does not tell us how well the qubit preserves the phase of a superposition.

12.2.2 T2: the coherence or dephasing time

T2 describes how quickly the phase coherence between the computational basis states disappears. A qubit can be prepared in an equal superposition:
|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2
This state has equal probabilities of measuring 0 and 1, but it also has a definite relative phase. That phase allows later gates to create interference. As the qubit interacts with its environment, its phase can drift. The coherence then decays, often approximately exponentially:
C(t) = C(0)e−t/T₂
Here C(t) represents the magnitude of the relevant coherence term in a simplified model. A short T2 means the qubit loses useful phase information quickly. This limits how long a quantum algorithm can safely wait between operations.

12.2.3 How T1 and T2 are related

For a common model involving independent energy relaxation and pure dephasing:
1/T₂ = 1/(2T₁) + 1/Tφ
Tφ is the pure-dephasing time: it describes dephasing beyond that already associated with energy relaxation. This relationship implies:
T₂ ≤ 2T₁
This bound applies to the standard model described above. Real devices may require more detailed models, and measured coherence times can depend on the experimental procedure.
Remember: T1 measures energy relaxation. T2 measures loss of coherence. They are related, but they are not interchangeable.

12.2.4 How T1 and T2 are measured

A simplified T1 experiment follows these steps:
  1. Prepare the qubit in the excited state |1⟩.
  2. Allow it to wait for a selected duration.
  3. Measure the qubit.
  4. Repeat the experiment many times for the same duration.
  5. Repeat for several waiting times and fit the observed populations to a decay curve.
To estimate T2, an experiment can prepare a superposition, allow the qubit to evolve, and measure how its phase coherence changes. A commonly used method is the Ramsey experiment. A spin-echo experiment adds a refocusing pulse that can reverse some slowly varying phase errors. T2 measured with a Ramsey experiment is often called T2*, because it includes certain slowly varying frequency differences and inhomogeneous dephasing effects. A spin-echo measurement can produce a longer coherence time by refocusing some of those effects.

Wait
→
Measure
→
Fit decay

Repeated experiments turn noisy individual measurements into statistical estimates of physical parameters.

12.2.5 Gate fidelity and randomized benchmarking

T1 and T2 describe aspects of a qubit's physical behaviour. They do not directly tell us how accurately a particular gate performs. Gate fidelity is a measure of how closely an implemented operation matches its intended ideal operation. Several definitions exist, so a fidelity value must always be interpreted in the context of its definition and measurement method. Randomized benchmarking is an experimental technique used to estimate average error behaviour for sequences of quantum gates. A simplified experiment works as follows:
  1. Choose a sequence of random gates from a suitable gate set.
  2. Apply the gates to the qubit or qubits.
  3. Append a recovery operation intended to return the system to its starting state in the ideal case.
  4. Measure whether the expected result is obtained.
  5. Repeat for sequences of different lengths and average the results.
In the ideal case, the complete sequence returns the system to its intended starting state. In a real device, errors accumulate as the sequence becomes longer, and the average probability of success usually decreases. A commonly used simplified fit is:
P(m) = A pm + B
Here:
  • m is the number of randomised operations or sequence units.
  • p describes the decay associated with average error under the assumptions of the benchmarking protocol.
  • A and B account for state preparation, measurement, and other offsets in the fitted signal.
For a single qubit and a suitable single-qubit Clifford benchmarking protocol, the average error per Clifford can be estimated as:
r ≈ (1 − p)/2
For a d-dimensional system under the corresponding assumptions, the general relationship is:
r ≈ (d − 1)(1 − p)/d
These expressions are protocol-dependent approximations, not universal formulas for every gate or device. Additional methods are needed to estimate individual gate errors, leakage, coherent errors, or errors specific to a particular operation.

12.3 Error mitigation

Error mitigation aims to obtain better estimates of ideal quantum computation results without necessarily encoding every logical qubit in a full quantum error-correcting code. This is especially useful for noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices, where the number of available qubits and the quality of operations may be insufficient for large fault-tolerant computations. The central idea is that we may not be able to remove every error from every circuit execution, but we can sometimes use additional experiments, calibration data, or statistical methods to estimate what the ideal answer would have been. Three important approaches are readout mitigation, zero-noise extrapolation, and probabilistic error cancellation.

12.3.1 Readout error mitigation

Suppose we prepare a qubit in |0⟩ many times. Because measurement is imperfect, the hardware may sometimes report 1. We can estimate these mistakes by preparing known states and measuring them repeatedly. For a single qubit, define a readout confusion matrix:
M = [ P(0 reported | 0 prepared)   P(0 reported | 1 prepared) ]
    [ P(1 reported | 0 prepared)   P(1 reported | 1 prepared) ]
More compactly:
q = M p
Here p is the vector of true outcome probabilities and q is the vector of observed probabilities. For example, assume the calibrated readout probabilities are:
M = [0.95   0.10]
    [0.05   0.90]
This means:
  • If the true state is 0, the device reports 0 with probability 0.95 and 1 with probability 0.05.
  • If the true state is 1, the device reports 0 with probability 0.10 and 1 with probability 0.90.
Suppose the true probabilities are 70% for 0 and 30% for 1. The expected observed probabilities are:
q₀ = 0.95 × 0.70 + 0.10 × 0.30 = 0.695
q₁ = 0.05 × 0.70 + 0.90 × 0.30 = 0.305
The hardware would therefore report approximately 69.5% zeros and 30.5% ones, rather than the ideal 70% and 30%. If we know the confusion matrix, we can estimate the original probabilities by solving:
p ≈ M⁻¹q
This inverse is only a conceptual shorthand. In practice, the estimate must respect the available data, numerical stability, and probability constraints. For many qubits, the calibration problem becomes considerably more difficult.
Readout mitigation does not undo all errors in the computation. It corrects statistical bias associated with measurement mistakes described by the calibration model. It cannot generally recover quantum information already destroyed by earlier noise.

12.3.2 Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)

Zero-noise extrapolation estimates the ideal result by deliberately evaluating a computation at several effective noise levels. The method begins with an important observation: if we can estimate how an observable changes as noise increases, we may be able to extrapolate that trend towards the zero-noise limit. The basic procedure is:
  1. Choose a quantum circuit and an observable whose expectation value we want to estimate.
  2. Execute the circuit under its ordinary noise conditions.
  3. Create additional circuit executions with higher effective noise.
  4. Measure the observable at each noise level.
  5. Fit an appropriate model and extrapolate towards the zero-noise limit.
There are different ways to increase the effective noise. One technique, called gate folding, replaces a gate U with a longer equivalent sequence:
U → U U† U
Ideally, U†U equals the identity, so the overall operation is still U. Physically, however, the longer sequence introduces more opportunities for noise. This can increase the effective error while preserving the intended ideal operation. Another approach is to use hardware controls to vary noise strength, where such control is available and sufficiently well characterised.

A simple illustrative example

Suppose the measured expectation values at three effective noise strengths are:
Relative noise scale Measured expectation
10.80
20.65
30.50
These illustrative points lie on a straight line. Extending that line back to a noise scale of zero gives an estimate of 0.95.
Estimated zero-noise value = 0.95
This is a deliberately simplified example, not a guarantee that real quantum data follows a straight line. Actual extrapolation depends on the noise model, the available measurements, and statistical uncertainty.

Advantages of ZNE

  • It can improve estimates without requiring a complete quantum error-correcting code.
  • It can be applied to expectation values from many kinds of quantum circuits.
  • It can work with several techniques for increasing effective noise.

Limitations of ZNE

  • Higher-noise executions may produce very poor data.
  • Extrapolation can magnify statistical uncertainty.
  • The assumed relationship between noise and the observable may be inaccurate.
  • It does not recover arbitrary quantum information that has been lost.
  • The extra executions consume measurement time and computational resources.

12.3.3 Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)

Probabilistic error cancellation attempts to correct the average effect of known noise by combining results from specially chosen circuits with positive and negative statistical weights. The key idea is that an ideal operation can sometimes be represented as a signed combination of noisy operations. A signed combination allows some contributions to cancel the unwanted effects of others. Suppose the ideal operation U is not directly available. We may instead implement noisy operations that approximate related transformations. If the noise model is sufficiently well known, mathematical techniques can express the ideal operation as a combination of these available operations. We then sample circuits according to an appropriate probability distribution and weight their measured outcomes. Some contributions receive negative weights. The weighted average can estimate the ideal expectation value even though individual circuit executions remain noisy.
Calibrate the noise model
→
Choose sampled circuits
→
Measure outcomes
→
Combine weighted results
PEC is powerful in principle, but it has a serious cost. The weights needed for cancellation can have large magnitudes. Consequently, the number of circuit executions needed to achieve a desired statistical accuracy may grow rapidly with circuit size and noise strength. If the noise model is inaccurate, the cancellation may also be incomplete or misleading.
ZNE asks, "What would the result approach if the noise were reduced towards zero?" PEC asks, "Can we combine noisy operations statistically to reproduce the effect of ideal operations?" Both rely on additional data and assumptions about noise.

12.3.4 Comparing error-mitigation methods

Method Main idea Major limitation
Readout mitigation Calibrate and compensate for measurement mistakes. Does not repair arbitrary gate errors or lost coherence.
ZNE Measure at several noise levels and extrapolate. Extrapolation can be unstable and noisy.
PEC Use a noise model and weighted circuit sampling to cancel errors statistically. Sampling overhead can become very large.

12.3.5 Mitigation is not the same as correction

Error mitigation generally improves estimates of selected outputs or observables. It does not guarantee that the physical qubits remain in the correct quantum state throughout a computation. Error correction takes a different approach: it encodes quantum information redundantly and repeatedly obtains information about error patterns. Mitigation is useful when full error correction is not available, but its overhead can become prohibitive as circuits grow larger and noise accumulates. It is not a general substitute for fault-tolerant quantum computing.

12.4 Quantum error correction basics

Quantum error correction (QEC) is one of the central ideas behind building reliable quantum computers. Its purpose is to protect a logical quantum state against physical errors by distributing the information across multiple physical qubits and measuring carefully chosen properties of those qubits.

12.4.1 Physical qubits and logical qubits

A physical qubit is a real quantum device: for example, a superconducting circuit, a trapped ion, or a photon-based qubit. A logical qubit is quantum information encoded across several physical qubits. The logical qubit is the unit that the error-corrected computer aims to preserve and manipulate reliably. Think of a physical qubit as a single fragile storage component and a logical qubit as an error-protected representation built from several such components. The goal is not simply to store multiple independent copies of the same unknown quantum state. Instead, the physical qubits are prepared in a carefully chosen joint state that represents one logical quantum state.

12.4.2 Why measuring the qubit directly does not work

Suppose the logical qubit is:
|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩
If we measure it in the computational basis, we obtain either 0 or 1. That measurement generally destroys the original superposition, so it cannot be used to determine and restore arbitrary α and β. Error correction therefore measures a different kind of information: the error syndrome. A syndrome tells us which error pattern is consistent with the measured properties. It does not reveal the unknown logical amplitudes in the way a direct measurement would. This is the clever part of quantum error correction: learn enough about the damage to repair it without directly learning the quantum information we are trying to protect.

12.4.3 The three-qubit bit-flip code

Begin with a simple example that protects against one type of error: a bit flip. A bit flip is the quantum operation X:
X|0⟩ = |1⟩     X|1⟩ = |0⟩
The three-qubit bit-flip code encodes:
|0⟩ → |000⟩
|1⟩ → |111⟩
Thus, the logical state becomes:
|ψL⟩ = α|000⟩ + β|111⟩
Notice that the amplitudes α and β have not been copied into three independent qubits. Instead, the logical information is stored in the joint three-qubit state.

Step 1: Introduce an error

Suppose the first physical qubit flips. The state becomes:
α|100⟩ + β|011⟩
The physical state is no longer in the intended encoded form. However, the error may still be identified without measuring α and β directly.

Step 2: Measure parity

We measure two parity checks:
S₁ = Z₁Z₂
S₂ = Z₂Z₃
Here, Z is the Pauli-Z operator. Measuring the product of two Z operators reveals whether the corresponding computational-basis bits agree or disagree, without individually measuring their values. The possible syndrome outcomes are:
S₁ S₂ Interpretation
+1+1No bit flip detected.
−1+1The first qubit is inconsistent with the second.
−1−1The second qubit is inconsistent with both neighbours.
+1−1The third qubit is inconsistent with the second.
The syndrome for a single bit flip identifies which physical qubit is inconsistent with the others.

Step 3: Correct the error

If the syndrome indicates that the first qubit flipped, apply X to the first qubit. This restores the encoded state:
α|100⟩ + β|011⟩ → α|000⟩ + β|111⟩
The unknown amplitudes α and β remain intact.
The three-qubit bit-flip code can correct any single bit-flip error under its idealised assumptions. It cannot, by itself, correct every possible quantum error. In particular, it does not independently protect against arbitrary phase-flip errors.

12.4.4 The three-qubit phase-flip code

A phase flip is represented by the Pauli-Z operator:
Z|0⟩ = |0⟩
Z|1⟩ = −|1⟩
Unlike X, Z does not exchange the basis states. It changes the relative phase of a superposition. The three-qubit bit-flip code does not directly detect a single phase flip because it focuses on the wrong set of properties. However, we can construct a related code in the X basis. Define:
|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2
|−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2
The phase-flip code encodes:
|0⟩ → |+++⟩
|1⟩ → |−−−⟩
Since Z exchanges |+⟩ and |−⟩, the phase-flip problem becomes analogous to the bit-flip problem in this basis. This shows that quantum errors can affect different aspects of information: bit values, phases, or combinations of both.

12.4.5 Correcting arbitrary single-qubit errors

A general single-qubit error can be described using combinations of the Pauli operators I, X, Y, and Z, where I is the identity operation and:
Y = iXZ
The overall sign convention for Y is not important for the basic explanation here. A code that corrects an arbitrary single-qubit error must be able to protect against both bit-flip and phase-flip components. The nine-qubit Shor code is a famous early example of such a code. Modern quantum error correction uses more sophisticated constructions, including surface codes, which arrange physical qubits on a lattice and repeatedly measure local parity checks.

12.4.6 Why correction must be repeated

Correcting a qubit once is not enough. New errors can occur during subsequent gates, waiting periods, measurements, and even the correction procedure itself. A practical error-correcting computer repeatedly performs syndrome measurements in rounds. The sequence of syndromes over time provides evidence about how errors develop.

A key challenge is that syndrome measurements, control operations, and classical processing can also be faulty. A useful code must tolerate these imperfections rather than assume that every correction step is perfect.

12.5 Stabilizer codes and the surface code

Stabilizer codes provide a systematic mathematical framework for quantum error correction. They allow us to describe an encoded quantum state by specifying a set of commuting measurements that the valid code states satisfy. The surface code is one of the best-studied stabilizer codes because it combines a useful error-correction structure with local interactions between neighbouring qubits.

12.5.1 The Pauli operators

Stabilizer codes are commonly described using the Pauli operators:
I = [1 0; 0 1]

X = [0 1; 1 0]

Z = [1 0; 0 −1]
The fourth Pauli operator is Y, which combines bit-flip and phase-flip behaviour up to a phase factor. These operators form the basic language used to describe many quantum errors. For several qubits, we can form products such as X₁X₂ or Z₂Z₃.

12.5.2 What is a stabilizer?

A stabilizer is an operator that leaves every valid code state unchanged. If a state |ψ⟩ belongs to a code space and S is one of its stabilizers, then:
S|ψ⟩ = |ψ⟩
In a typical stabilizer code, the stabilizer operators are built from products of Pauli operators. They commute with one another, allowing their properties to be measured together without creating a conflict between the measurements. The set of states satisfying all stabilizer conditions defines the code space.

12.5.3 How stabilizers detect errors

Suppose the original encoded state satisfies:
S|ψ⟩ = |ψ⟩
An error E changes the state to E|ψ⟩. If E anticommutes with S, then:
SE = −ES
It follows that:
S(E|ψ⟩) = −E|ψ⟩
The stabilizer measurement now returns −1 instead of +1. This change is evidence of an error. The collection of measurement outcomes is called the syndrome. If an error commutes with a particular stabilizer, that stabilizer's outcome does not change. Therefore, error detection relies on measuring a suitable set of stabilizers rather than just one.
A stabilizer measurement does not ask, "What is the logical qubit's value?" It asks, "Does this physical state satisfy the expected parity relationship?" That distinction allows the code to detect damage without directly measuring the encoded logical state.

12.5.4 Code distance

The distance of a quantum error-correcting code is the minimum number of physical-qubit errors required to produce an undetectable logical operation, under the usual stabilizer-code definition. If a code has distance d, it can correct any arbitrary pattern of up to:
t = floor((d − 1)/2)
physical-qubit errors, provided the code's assumptions hold and the relevant error syndrome is measured reliably enough. Examples:
Code distance Guaranteed correction capability
d = 31 arbitrary physical-qubit error.
d = 52 arbitrary physical-qubit errors.
d = 73 arbitrary physical-qubit errors.
This guarantee concerns the code's ideal error-correction capability. Real devices also experience faulty syndrome measurements, correlated errors, leakage, and other effects. These must be included when designing a practical fault-tolerant system.

12.5.5 What is the surface code?

The surface code is a family of topological stabilizer codes in which qubits and parity checks are arranged on a two-dimensional structure. Each check involves only a small local group of nearby physical qubits. A common layout distinguishes between:
  • Data qubits: physical qubits that collectively encode the logical information.
  • X-type checks: parity measurements sensitive to certain phase-related error patterns.
  • Z-type checks: parity measurements sensitive to certain bit-related error patterns.
  • Ancilla or measurement qubits: additional physical qubits used to extract stabilizer information.
Exact layouts depend on the particular surface-code variant. In a common arrangement, measurement qubits interact with nearby data qubits to extract the relevant parity information.

12.5.6 Repeated syndrome measurements and decoding

A single syndrome measurement may not be sufficient to determine exactly which errors occurred. Measurement operations can also be noisy, and different physical error patterns can produce the same syndrome. Surface-code systems therefore repeat their stabilizer measurements over many rounds. A change in a stabilizer outcome compared with the previous round is called a detection event. The pattern of detection events across space and time helps identify likely physical errors. A classical algorithm called a decoder processes these events and chooses a correction or a corresponding logical-frame update. Popular decoder families include minimum-weight perfect matching and union-find methods, with different trade-offs in accuracy and speed. The decoder does not need to reconstruct every microscopic detail of the physical noise. Its job is to infer a correction that restores the intended logical information as reliably as possible.

12.5.7 Why the surface code is important

The surface code is attractive because it uses local interactions and has a relatively practical structure for several hardware platforms. It can also tolerate a non-zero rate of physical errors. Below a suitable error threshold, increasing the code distance can reduce the logical error rate. The threshold is not a universal number. It depends on the code variant, the physical noise model, the available gates, measurement quality, decoder, circuit design, and assumptions about error correlations. Increasing code distance improves protection but also requires more physical qubits, more syndrome measurements, and greater control and processing resources.
The central surface-code strategy is to turn local physical errors into detectable patterns of stabilizer changes. The decoder uses those patterns to protect the logical information encoded across the lattice.

12.6 Logical qubits and fault tolerance

Error correction gives us a way to protect quantum information, but a useful quantum computer must also perform gates, measurements, and algorithmic operations on that protected information. This leads to two connected ideas: logical qubits and fault-tolerant quantum computing.

12.6.1 What makes a logical qubit different?

A logical qubit is encoded in a subspace of a larger physical system. Its logical basis states, often written |0L⟩ and |1L⟩, are represented by carefully chosen multi-qubit states. A logical superposition is:
|ψL⟩ = α|0L⟩ + β|1L⟩
The logical amplitudes α and β carry the quantum information. Physical errors may disturb individual qubits, but the encoding and syndrome measurements can allow the system to recover the logical state. The number of physical qubits required for a logical qubit depends on the code, code distance, target error rate, hardware, and operations that must be performed. One logical qubit is therefore not necessarily one physical qubit. In practical error-corrected systems, a logical qubit can require many physical qubits and repeated measurement cycles.

12.6.2 What is a logical error?

A physical error affects one or more hardware qubits. A logical error occurs when the accumulated physical errors cause the encoded information to change in a way that the code fails to detect or correct. For example, an individual physical-qubit error may produce a syndrome that identifies the damage. A sufficiently long chain of errors can sometimes imitate a logical operation while remaining undetected by the stabilizer checks. In a surface code, the distance determines the minimum weight of a non-trivial logical error pattern. A higher distance generally means that a larger pattern of physical errors is needed to cause a logical failure. This is why the logical error rate, rather than the physical error rate alone, is a central measure of progress.

12.6.3 What does fault tolerance mean?

Fault tolerance means designing a quantum computation so that individual faults do not automatically destroy the entire computation. It is not enough to correct storage errors while assuming that every gate and measurement is perfect. Real error-correction circuits contain imperfect operations, so the correction procedure itself must be designed to tolerate faults. A fault-tolerant design aims to prevent a single physical fault from spreading uncontrollably into many errors within the same encoded block. Important ideas include:
  • Using carefully designed logical operations.
  • Preventing unwanted error propagation during gates.
  • Performing repeated syndrome extraction.
  • Using reliable decoders to infer error patterns.
  • Handling faulty measurements and state preparation.
  • Keeping the overall logical error rate sufficiently low.

12.6.4 Physical error rate versus logical error rate

Imagine that a physical qubit has a small probability of error during a particular operation. A quantum error-correcting code combines many such qubits and uses redundancy to detect and correct a range of error patterns. If the hardware operates within the suitable noise regime and the code is implemented correctly, increasing the code distance can reduce the probability of an uncorrectable logical error.
Lower physical error rate + suitable code + reliable syndrome processing → potentially lower logical error rate
This relationship is not automatic. Correlated noise, leakage, faulty measurements, poor decoding, or excessive operation overhead can undermine the expected improvement. A code may also have an operating threshold: below that threshold, increasing its size can improve logical protection; above it, simply adding more physical qubits may not help.

12.6.5 The overhead problem

Fault-tolerant quantum computing requires more resources than an ideal mathematical circuit suggests. Consider an algorithm that needs a certain number of logical qubits and logical operations. The hardware must also provide the physical qubits used to encode them, the ancillas used for syndrome measurements, the control operations, classical decoding, and any additional resources needed to implement logical gates. The complete resource requirement may be much larger than the logical circuit alone. For example, non-Clifford gates are important for universal quantum computation. In surface-code architectures, many such operations require additional techniques, such as magic-state preparation and distillation, which can consume substantial time and physical resources. The exact overhead depends on the algorithm, code, hardware error rates, target success probability, and architecture. There is no single physical-qubit count that applies to every logical qubit or algorithm.

12.6.6 Logical gates and universal computation

Quantum algorithms use operations such as the Hadamard gate, Pauli gates, controlled operations, and non-Clifford gates. An error-corrected system must implement the required operations on encoded information while preserving error protection. Some logical operations are relatively straightforward for particular codes. Others require more elaborate procedures. For the surface code, logical operations can involve lattice surgery, code deformation, or teleportation-based techniques. Non-Clifford operations commonly rely on special resource states, often called magic states, which are prepared and purified using distillation procedures. The point is that a fault-tolerant computer is not simply a collection of protected memory cells. It must also support a practical set of reliable logical operations.

12.7 The path to fault-tolerant quantum computing

Building a fault-tolerant quantum computer is not one engineering breakthrough. It requires progress across hardware, control, quantum error correction, classical processing, software, and system architecture. A useful way to understand the journey is to follow the progression from a single imperfect qubit to a machine that can run a long quantum algorithm with a predictable probability of success.

12.7.1 Stage one: improve physical qubits

The first task is to build reliable physical devices. Engineers work to improve relaxation and coherence times, reduce gate errors, improve readout accuracy, and minimise unwanted interactions. They also develop calibration procedures and better control electronics. Better physical qubits help because the error-correction system has less damage to manage. However, a device with excellent individual qubits does not automatically provide a scalable fault-tolerant computer. The operations and measurements between those qubits must also be reliable.

12.7.2 Stage two: characterise the hardware

The next task is to measure what the hardware actually does. Engineers use T1 and T2 experiments, gate benchmarking, readout calibration, leakage measurements, and other diagnostics to understand the device's error behaviour. Characterisation is an ongoing process. Noise can change with temperature, device drift, operating frequency, control settings, and the execution of other circuits. Reliable error correction requires a sufficiently accurate understanding of the errors that matter to the architecture.

12.7.3 Stage three: demonstrate error suppression

Researchers implement small error-correcting codes and measure whether encoded information performs better than unprotected physical qubits under comparable conditions. The important test is not simply whether the code can detect an error. The stronger test is whether the complete encoded system reduces the logical error rate after accounting for the overhead of encoding, syndrome extraction, and decoding. Demonstrating error suppression as code distance increases is an important step towards scalable fault tolerance.

12.7.4 Stage four: scale the code distance

Once a code performs well, the next task is to increase its distance and show that stronger protection is achievable. Larger code distances require more data qubits, more check qubits, additional control operations, and more syndrome data to process. The engineering challenge is to scale the system without allowing new problems, such as crosstalk, wiring complexity, correlated errors, or decoding delays, to dominate.

12.7.5 Stage five: build reliable logical operations

A useful machine must do more than store a logical state. It must execute logical gates, initialise logical qubits, measure them, and connect operations together into circuits. Each operation introduces additional error opportunities and resource requirements. Researchers therefore work on fault-tolerant gate implementations, logical-state preparation, logical measurements, magic-state distillation, and efficient scheduling of error-correction cycles.

12.7.6 Stage six: run complete fault-tolerant algorithms

The final goal is to run quantum algorithms whose depth, number of logical operations, and required accuracy would be impractical without sustained error protection. At this stage, the system must coordinate:
  • Physical qubits and control electronics.
  • Repeated syndrome measurements.
  • Fast and reliable classical decoding.
  • Logical gates and state preparation.
  • Resource management and error-budget estimation.
  • Algorithm-level verification and final measurement.
The goal is to ensure that the logical error probability remains low enough across the entire computation, not merely during one gate or one short experiment.

12.7.7 The complete progression

  1. Reliable physical hardware
    Improve coherence, gates, readout, connectivity, and control.
  2. Accurate noise characterisation
    Measure T1, T2, gate errors, readout errors, leakage, and relevant correlations.
  3. Useful near-term error mitigation
    Apply calibration, readout mitigation, ZNE, or PEC where their assumptions and resource costs make sense.
  4. Encoded logical qubits
    Protect quantum information through an appropriate error-correcting code.
  5. Repeated correction and decoding
    Detect changes in error syndromes and infer likely physical errors.
  6. Scalable logical operations
    Implement gates, measurements, and state preparation while controlling error propagation.
  7. Fault-tolerant quantum computing
    Execute long computations with a sufficiently low logical failure probability and manageable resource overhead.

12.7.8 What remains difficult?

Several problems must be solved together.

Hardware scale

Large systems need many well-controlled physical qubits, suitable connectivity, and practical wiring and control arrangements.

Error correlations

Error-correction estimates can become unreliable if physical errors are strongly correlated or differ significantly from the assumed noise model.

Classical processing

Syndrome data must be processed quickly enough to support the required correction and control strategy.

Resource overhead

The total physical-qubit count, operation count, and execution time must remain manageable for useful algorithms.

Logical gate reliability

Every gate, measurement, and state-preparation procedure must fit within the overall logical error budget.

End-to-end verification

A successful small error-correction experiment does not by itself demonstrate that a large algorithm can run reliably.

12.7.9 Why error correction matters for quantum advantage

A quantum computer is not useful merely because it contains many qubits. It must perform a task accurately enough to produce a meaningful result. Quantum algorithms such as Shor's factoring algorithm and many quantum simulation algorithms can require deep circuits, repeated operations, and precise interference. Without adequate error protection, noise may accumulate until the final output becomes unreliable. Error mitigation can improve selected estimates on noisy hardware, but its statistical cost can grow significantly with circuit size and noise. Fault-tolerant computing aims to control logical errors throughout a computation so that useful algorithmic depth becomes achievable. This does not mean every quantum algorithm will outperform every classical algorithm. Quantum advantage depends on the task, the algorithm, the available hardware, the resource requirements, and the best classical methods. Fault tolerance is an enabling technology, not a guarantee of quantum advantage for every problem.
The long-term objective: Build quantum systems in which physical errors still occur, but their effects can be detected, corrected, and controlled well enough that logical quantum information remains reliable throughout useful computations.

Key ideas to remember

  • Noise is an unwanted physical effect that changes quantum states, gates, or measurements.
  • T1 describes energy relaxation, while T2 describes the loss of phase coherence.
  • Randomized benchmarking estimates average gate-error behaviour using sequences of random operations.
  • Error mitigation improves estimates of selected outputs but does not generally protect an entire quantum state throughout a computation.
  • Quantum error correction encodes information across multiple physical qubits and uses syndrome measurements to detect errors.
  • Stabilizers describe properties that valid code states satisfy.
  • The surface code uses local parity checks and repeated syndrome measurements to protect logical qubits.
  • Code distance describes how many physical errors are required to produce an undetectable logical operation.
  • Fault tolerance means designing the complete computation to remain reliable despite imperfect components.
  • The ultimate goal is not to eliminate every physical error, but to control their effects well enough to perform useful quantum computations reliably.
