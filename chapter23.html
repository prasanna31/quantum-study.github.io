Quantum Computing and Machine Learning

Chapter 23: Quantum Algorithms for Classical ML Subroutines

How quantum computers might accelerate the mathematical operations behind machine learning, why the proposed speedups are interesting, and what must be true before those speedups become useful in practice.

Chapter Overview

Machine learning is often described as a process in which a computer learns patterns from data. Behind this simple description are many mathematical operations: multiplying matrices, solving equations, finding similarities, reducing dimensions, sampling probability distributions and improving model parameters.

A classical computer performs these operations using ordinary bits and numerical calculations. A quantum computer uses qubits, quantum gates and measurements. Under certain conditions, quantum algorithms may perform particular parts of an ML workflow more efficiently.

The aim is not to replace every classical ML algorithm with a quantum circuit. It is to identify specific computational tasks where quantum mechanics provides a genuine advantage.

Before We Begin: What Does a Quantum Speedup Mean?

Imagine that an ML application needs to analyse a million records. Some of the work may involve finding correlations between variables, solving a system of equations or repeatedly evaluating a loss function. If one of these operations becomes expensive, improving it can improve the overall workflow.

This is where quantum algorithms enter the picture. Instead of directly storing every numerical value in a conventional array, some quantum algorithms encode information into the amplitudes of a quantum state. Quantum gates transform that state, and a measurement extracts a classical result.

Classical data
Features and labels
Encoding
Prepare a quantum state
Quantum processing
Apply a circuit
Measurement
Extract useful information

The difficult part is that preparing the state and reading the answer also cost time. A quantum state containing many amplitudes does not mean that we can simply read all those amplitudes at once. Measurement gives limited information from each circuit execution.

Big-O notation and speedup

Big-O notation describes how an algorithm's resource requirements grow with the size of its input. Let N represent the number of records in a dataset.

Classical cost: O(N)
Possible quantum cost: O(log N)

This comparison is only illustrative. It does not mean that every operation requiring O(N) classical time has a quantum equivalent requiring O(log N) time. A quantum algorithm may achieve such a dependence for a carefully defined task, but only under specific assumptions about data access, conditioning, precision and the type of output required.

Important distinction: a fast quantum subroutine does not automatically make the complete ML application faster. Loading data, repeating measurements, processing outputs and communicating with classical systems can dominate the total cost.

The two questions to ask about every quantum ML algorithm

  1. Computational question: Which operation does the quantum algorithm perform more efficiently, and under which assumptions?
  2. Practical question: Can we prepare the input and obtain the answer cheaply enough to preserve the advantage?

23.1 Quantum Principal Component Analysis (Quantum PCA)

23.1.1 Why do we need PCA?

Suppose a company collects information about customers: age, purchase frequency, average spending, preferred teams, location, membership duration and many more features. A model may receive hundreds or thousands of features for each customer.

However, many features may carry similar information. Customers who spend frequently may also have high annual spending. Some measurements may be strongly correlated. PCA helps identify a smaller collection of directions that capture much of the variation in the data.

These directions are called principal components. They are new combinations of the original features, not simply a selection of the most important original columns.

23.1.2 The classical PCA procedure

Consider a dataset represented by a matrix X with N rows and d columns. Each row represents one observation, and each column represents one feature.

The usual procedure is:

  1. Centre each feature by subtracting its mean.
  2. Calculate the covariance matrix.
  3. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of that matrix.
  4. Rank the eigenvectors by their eigenvalues.
  5. Project the original observations onto the leading components.

The covariance matrix describes how pairs of features vary together. An eigenvector identifies a direction in feature space, while its eigenvalue measures the variance along that direction.

C = XᵀX / (N - 1)

C vᵢ = λᵢ vᵢ

Here, X is assumed to be centred, C is the covariance matrix, vᵢ is an eigenvector and λᵢ is its corresponding eigenvalue. In PCA, eigenvectors with large eigenvalues represent directions along which the observations vary substantially.

23.1.3 The intuition behind quantum PCA

The quantum approach aims to extract information about the dominant eigenvectors of a suitable matrix using quantum states and quantum operations. One influential approach uses a quantum version of phase estimation to reveal eigenvalue information, together with a density matrix that represents the data-related operator.

A density matrix is a mathematical object used to describe a quantum state, including a statistical mixture of possible states. In a simplified quantum PCA setting, the state may encode a normalised covariance-like matrix or a related positive semidefinite matrix.

If the relevant matrix is represented by a density operator ρ, its spectral decomposition is:

ρ = Σᵢ λᵢ |vᵢ⟩⟨vᵢ|

The vectors |vᵢ⟩ are eigenvectors, and the λᵢ values are non-negative eigenvalues that sum to one. The quantum algorithm attempts to obtain information about the important eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors without necessarily constructing the entire matrix as a classical array.

23.1.4 How phase estimation helps

Suppose a quantum operation can be constructed from the matrix of interest. Quantum phase estimation can estimate the phase associated with an eigenstate of that operation. With an appropriate construction, this phase can reveal information about an eigenvalue.

The broad process is as follows:

  1. Prepare a quantum state containing components along relevant eigenvectors.
  2. Apply controlled operations that encode eigenvalue information into phases.
  3. Use phase estimation to obtain estimates of those eigenvalues.
  4. Measure and process the output to infer the dominant components.

The algorithm does not magically print every eigenvector. The useful information obtained depends on the state preparation, the eigenvalue distribution, the measurement strategy and the requested output.

23.1.5 A simple example

Imagine that two customer features are average monthly spending and annual spending. If they are strongly correlated, much of their variation may lie along one dominant direction.

PCA identifies that direction. A downstream model may then use one transformed feature instead of both original features. Quantum PCA explores whether dominant spectral information can be extracted efficiently when the data and matrix operations satisfy suitable quantum-access assumptions.

23.1.6 Where is the possible speedup?

Some theoretical quantum PCA algorithms can have favourable dependence on the matrix dimension when the input is available through suitable quantum states or efficient quantum access procedures. Their complexity may instead depend on quantities such as the inverse eigenvalue gap, precision, state preparation cost and the number of copies of the state.

Practical limitation: if the original dataset is stored in an ordinary database and every element must first be loaded into a quantum state, that cost can remove the theoretical advantage. Likewise, returning a complete list of classical principal-component vectors can require substantial output work.
Remember: classical PCA is a general-purpose dimensionality-reduction method. Quantum PCA is a family of quantum approaches for extracting spectral information under particular input and output assumptions. It is not a universal replacement for standard PCA libraries.

23.2 Quantum Linear Regression and Least Squares

23.2.1 Why does machine learning solve equations?

Many ML models estimate parameters from observed data. In linear regression, the model predicts a numerical output using a weighted sum of input features.

For a model with d features, the prediction is:

ŷ = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + ... + wᵈxᵈ + b

The weights determine how the features affect the prediction, while b is the bias term. Training estimates these parameters from examples for which the actual outputs are known.

23.2.2 Least squares

Let X contain the training features, y contain the target values, and w contain the unknown weights. The predicted values are Xw. Least squares chooses weights that minimise the sum of squared prediction errors.

Minimise: ||Xw - y||₂²

If the relevant inverse exists, the solution can be expressed through the normal equations:

XᵀXw = Xᵀy

w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy

The second expression requires XᵀX to be invertible. In practice, a matrix may be singular or poorly conditioned, so algorithms such as QR decomposition, singular value decomposition and regularised least squares are often preferable to explicitly computing the inverse.

23.2.3 What is a quantum linear-system algorithm?

A quantum linear-system algorithm attempts to solve a system represented by:

A x = b

Here A is a matrix, b is the input vector and x is the unknown solution. Algorithms in the Harrow–Hassidim–Lloyd (HHL) family aim to prepare a quantum state proportional to the solution:

|x⟩ = A⁻¹|b⟩ / ||A⁻¹b||

This notation assumes that the system has an appropriate solution and that the inverse operation is well-defined on the relevant subspace. The state |b⟩ encodes the input vector, while |x⟩ represents the normalised solution.

23.2.4 How can the algorithm find the solution?

The main idea is to use quantum phase estimation to learn information about the eigenvalues of A, then apply a controlled transformation that behaves like taking the reciprocal of those eigenvalues.

  1. Prepare the quantum state representing b.
  2. Use suitable quantum operations to encode information about A's eigenvalues.
  3. Apply a reciprocal-like transformation to the eigenvalue information.
  4. Uncompute temporary registers and obtain a state related to the solution x.
  5. Measure the output to estimate a property of x or use the state in a later quantum computation.

The fourth step is not guaranteed to succeed on every execution. Some versions use an ancilla measurement and may require amplitude amplification or repeated trials to improve the success probability.

23.2.5 Why conditioning matters

A matrix is poorly conditioned when small changes in its input can cause large changes in the solution. The condition number measures the ratio between its largest and smallest relevant singular values.

κ(A) = σmax(A) / σmin(A)

A large condition number can make both classical and quantum linear-system algorithms more difficult. In important HHL-style settings, the complexity depends on the condition number, precision and sparsity or other structural properties of the matrix. Later algorithms can improve particular dependencies, but no single complexity expression applies to every version.

23.2.6 Connecting linear systems to regression

Ordinary least squares can be written as a linear system using A = XᵀX and b = Xᵀy. However, forming the normal equations can square the condition number in the full-rank case, which may worsen numerical stability.

Quantum least-squares methods therefore need careful choices about the matrix representation, regularisation and desired output. Some approaches target a quantum state representing the regression weights; others estimate predictions or particular observables directly.

A crucial distinction: preparing a quantum state proportional to the regression weights is not the same as returning every weight as a readable classical number. If an application needs all d weights explicitly, the readout cost may be substantial.

23.2.7 Example: predicting house prices

Suppose a model uses floor area, number of bedrooms and distance from the city centre to predict a house price. The training process estimates three weights and a bias.

A quantum linear-system method might be relevant if the training problem has a suitable matrix representation, the inputs can be prepared efficiently and the application can use the quantum-form solution without demanding an expensive full classical readout.

Remember: quantum linear-system algorithms are most compelling when the required answer is a compact property of the solution or a quantum state that feeds into further quantum processing, rather than a huge list of classical numbers.

23.3 Quantum Clustering: k-means and k-medians

23.3.1 What is clustering?

Clustering groups observations that are similar to one another. Unlike supervised learning, it does not require a known label for every observation.

For example, an online retailer may want to discover groups of customers based on their spending patterns. One group may purchase regularly, another may buy only during major events, and another may prefer discounted products.

23.3.2 Classical k-means

k-means divides N observations into k clusters. Each cluster is represented by its centroid, which is the mean of the points assigned to it.

The usual iterative procedure is:

  1. Choose k initial centroids.
  2. Assign each point to its nearest centroid.
  3. Recalculate each centroid as the mean of its assigned points.
  4. Repeat the assignment and update steps until the algorithm stops.
Minimise: Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ Σₓ∈Cᵢ ||x - μᵢ||₂²

Here Cᵢ is the set of points in cluster i, and μᵢ is its centroid. The objective is the total squared distance from each point to its assigned centroid.

The objective can have several local minima. Consequently, the final clusters can depend on the initial centroids. Classical implementations often use careful initialisation, multiple restarts or approximation techniques.

23.3.3 What changes in quantum clustering?

A quantum clustering algorithm may use quantum routines to estimate distances, compare similarity scores or search for candidate points more efficiently under particular assumptions. The aim is to reduce the cost of a computationally expensive subroutine inside the larger clustering algorithm.

There is no single algorithm that should be called the quantum version of k-means. Different proposals use different data encodings, similarity measures, state-preparation methods and measurement procedures.

23.3.4 Quantum distance estimation

Consider two vectors x and y. Their squared Euclidean distance is:

||x - y||₂² = ||x||₂² + ||y||₂² - 2xᵀy

If normalised vectors can be prepared as quantum states, an overlap-related measurement may provide information about their inner product. In some settings, this can help estimate a similarity or distance measure.

However, an inner-product measurement generally requires repeated circuit executions to estimate a result to a specified accuracy. Preparing the states can also be costly. A quantum distance routine is therefore not automatically faster than a classical distance calculation.

23.3.5 Quantum k-means workflow

Prepare encoded data and centroid states
Estimate relevant similarities or distances
Assign points to clusters
Update centroids and repeat

The centroid-update step is especially important. Computing the mean of a cluster requires information about its assigned points. If the algorithm must reconstruct every centroid as a classical vector after every iteration, the output and data-access costs may remove any benefit from faster quantum distance estimation.

23.3.6 What about k-medians?

k-medians follows a similar assignment-and-update pattern, but it uses a coordinate-wise median rather than a mean to represent each cluster. A common objective uses the Manhattan distance.

Manhattan distance: ||x - y||₁ = Σⱼ |xⱼ - yⱼ|

Because medians are determined by the ordering of coordinate values, updating a median can require a different computational procedure from updating a mean. Quantum distance estimation alone does not automatically accelerate this update.

A proposed quantum k-medians algorithm must therefore explain how it performs both assignment and representative updates, and account for the cost of obtaining the information required for those operations.

23.3.7 Where could quantum clustering help?

  • Similarity-based analysis of high-dimensional data.
  • Clustering workflows where a suitable quantum data representation already exists.
  • Hybrid algorithms where a quantum subroutine estimates a small number of useful quantities.
Practical caution: high dimensionality alone does not establish a quantum advantage. A meaningful comparison must include the number of data points, state preparation, number of measurements, number of clustering iterations and the cost of obtaining the final labels or centroids.

23.4 Quantum Recommendation Systems and Dequantization

23.4.1 How do recommendation systems work?

When a streaming platform recommends a film or a retailer recommends a product, it is trying to estimate what a person may prefer. One classical approach is collaborative filtering. It uses patterns in the interactions between users and items.

These interactions can be represented by a matrix R:

R[u, i] = rating or interaction of user u with item i

Most real-world interaction matrices are sparse. A person has interacted with only a tiny fraction of all available items. Recommendation algorithms attempt to infer useful structure from these incomplete observations.

23.4.2 Matrix factorisation

A common approach approximates R using two lower-dimensional matrices:

R ≈ U Vᵀ

The row of U associated with a user describes that user's latent preferences. The row of V associated with an item describes its latent characteristics. A predicted score is obtained by taking the inner product of the corresponding user and item vectors.

The goal is not to memorise every missing rating. It is to learn a compact representation that captures patterns such as preferred genres, price ranges or product categories.

23.4.3 The quantum idea

Some quantum recommendation proposals use quantum linear-system algorithms or related quantum matrix-processing methods to manipulate low-rank structures and prepare states related to useful latent representations.

A vector v can, under suitable normalisation, be encoded as:

|v⟩ = Σᵢ vᵢ |i⟩ / ||v||₂

The amplitudes of the quantum state contain information about the vector's components. Some operations on such states may depend on the number of qubits rather than on the full dimension of the vector.

Nevertheless, the amplitudes are not directly available as a complete classical array. Quantum recommendation algorithms often target particular predictions, probability distributions or compact summaries rather than returning every matrix entry.

23.4.4 What is dequantization?

Dequantization is the process of finding a classical algorithm that matches or approaches the performance of a proposed quantum algorithm, often under comparable input-access assumptions.

It does not mean converting a quantum computer into a classical computer. Instead, it asks whether the claimed advantage remains when the classical algorithm is allowed a similarly powerful way of accessing the input.

This question became important for quantum-inspired machine learning. Certain quantum linear-algebra algorithms rely on efficient access to entries or samples from data according to carefully defined distributions. Researchers showed that classical algorithms with analogous sampling access could solve some related low-rank problems efficiently as well.

23.4.5 Why the comparison matters

Suppose a quantum algorithm assumes that a state proportional to a data vector can be prepared efficiently. If a classical comparison algorithm is forced to read every element one at a time, the comparison may be unfair.

A fair analysis asks what input access each algorithm receives. If both are given powerful access to the data, does the quantum algorithm still have an advantage? If the quantum system needs an expensive state-preparation procedure, does the benefit survive when that cost is included?

Question Why it matters
How is the input accessed? Data-loading assumptions can dominate total runtime.
What answer is required? One recommendation is different from an entire score matrix.
How much structure exists? Low rank, sparsity and sampling access can help both classical and quantum methods.
Is the classical baseline strong? A speedup over a weak baseline may disappear against modern algorithms.

23.4.6 A recommendation example

Imagine a platform with ten million users and one million products. The full interaction matrix would be enormous, and most entries would be missing.

A quantum algorithm might aim to estimate the score of a particular user-item pair or sample a promising recommendation. Such a result is useful only if the user representation, candidate selection, data preparation and final ranking can all be completed efficiently.

Remember: dequantization is a necessary part of careful quantum ML research. A useful quantum advantage must survive comparison with the best appropriate classical algorithms under clearly stated access assumptions.

23.5 Quantum Boltzmann Sampling

23.5.1 Why does ML need sampling?

Many ML methods use probability distributions to represent uncertainty or learn the structure of data. Generative models, for instance, aim to learn a distribution from which realistic samples can be drawn.

A Boltzmann distribution assigns probabilities to configurations according to their energy. Lower-energy configurations receive greater probability, while higher-energy configurations receive less.

p(s) = exp(-βE(s)) / Z

Z = Σₛ exp(-βE(s))

Here s represents a configuration, E(s) is its energy, β is an inverse-temperature parameter and Z is the partition function. The partition function normalises the probabilities so that they sum to one.

23.5.2 What is a Boltzmann machine?

A Boltzmann machine is a probabilistic model with variables connected through interactions. In a common binary formulation, each variable takes a value of either -1 or +1. The energy depends on individual biases and interactions between pairs of variables.

E(s) = -Σᵢ hᵢsᵢ - Σᵢ<ⱼ Jᵢⱼsᵢsⱼ

The values hᵢ represent biases, while Jᵢⱼ represent pairwise interactions. The model learns these parameters so that its probability distribution captures useful patterns in the training data.

23.5.3 Why is sampling difficult?

With n binary variables, there are 2ⁿ possible configurations. Even a moderately large model can therefore have an enormous state space.

Calculating the partition function exactly may require summing over all configurations. Training also requires estimating expectations under model distributions, which can be difficult when the variables interact strongly.

23.5.4 The quantum approach

Quantum systems naturally have many possible measurement outcomes, and their energy levels are governed by a Hamiltonian. A Hamiltonian is the quantum-mechanical operator that describes the energy of a system.

In quantum Boltzmann approaches, a model may use a Hamiltonian H and a quantum thermal state:

ρ = exp(-βH) / Z

Z = Tr(exp(-βH))

The trace, written Tr, is the sum of the diagonal elements of an operator. Measuring the thermal state in a suitable basis produces outcomes according to probabilities determined by the state.

If H is diagonal in the measurement basis, the probabilities can correspond directly to a classical energy-based model. If H contains non-commuting terms, the model can exhibit quantum effects that do not have the same simple classical interpretation.

23.5.5 How might the samples be generated?

There are several approaches, and they should not be treated as one identical algorithm:

  • Quantum thermal-state preparation: prepare a state approximating the desired Gibbs distribution and measure it.
  • Quantum circuits as samplers: train a parameterised circuit so that its measurement distribution matches a target distribution.
  • Quantum-assisted sampling: use a quantum device for a difficult sampling subroutine inside a hybrid training pipeline.

Preparing an accurate thermal state is itself a challenging problem. It may require deep circuits, controlled interactions, special preparation methods or repeated operations. The existence of a quantum state with a desired distribution does not guarantee an efficient method for preparing it.

23.5.6 Connection to generative modelling

A trained energy-based model assigns scores to configurations and uses those scores to define probabilities. Sampling from the learned distribution can generate new configurations that resemble the training data.

For example, a model might learn relationships among binary features describing simple patterns. Sampling from it produces new feature combinations according to the learned distribution. More complicated models can represent richer patterns.

A quantum model may use quantum interactions or circuit measurements to represent its distribution. Whether that distribution is more expressive or harder for a classical sampler to reproduce depends on the chosen model and its measurable properties.

23.5.7 What would establish an advantage?

A convincing result must compare sampling quality and cost. Relevant measurements include preparation time, circuit depth, number of shots, mixing or convergence behaviour where applicable, and the computational resources required by the best classical sampler.

Quantum sampling is not automatically useful merely because the classical state space is large. A practical advantage requires a distribution that can be sampled efficiently by the quantum method and is meaningfully difficult for the relevant classical alternatives.
Remember: quantum Boltzmann sampling connects quantum statistical mechanics with probabilistic machine learning. The central problem is not only defining a useful quantum distribution, but also preparing it, sampling it accurately and using the samples to train a model.

23.6 Quantum Speedups in Optimisation for ML

23.6.1 What does optimisation mean in ML?

Training a machine learning model usually involves choosing parameters that minimise a loss function. The loss measures how far the model's predictions are from the desired results or how well the model satisfies a chosen objective.

θ* = arg minθ L(θ)

Here θ represents the trainable parameters, L is the loss function, and θ* denotes a parameter setting that minimises that loss. In real applications, finding a global minimum may be difficult, so training algorithms often seek a good solution within an acceptable amount of time.

23.6.2 Why can optimisation be expensive?

The difficulty depends on the structure of the objective. Some problems have smooth, well-behaved loss functions. Others contain many local minima, flat regions, constraints or discrete choices.

Examples of optimisation tasks in ML include:

  • Learning weights in a neural network.
  • Selecting features under a computational or storage budget.
  • Choosing a subset of training examples.
  • Optimising model architecture or hyperparameters.
  • Solving combinatorial scheduling or resource-allocation problems.

23.6.3 Quantum optimisation approaches

Quantum optimisation includes several distinct families of algorithms. Two commonly discussed examples are quantum approximate optimisation and quantum annealing.

The Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA) uses a parameterised quantum circuit designed around an objective expressed as a cost Hamiltonian. It alternates between operations related to the cost and operations that mix candidate configurations.

|ψ(γ, β)⟩ = Uᴮ(βₚ)Uᶜ(γₚ) ... Uᴮ(β₁)Uᶜ(γ₁)|+⟩

In this expression, Uᶜ represents a cost-related operation, Uᴮ represents a mixing operation, and p is the number of alternating layers. The parameters γ and β are adjusted classically to improve the expected objective value.

Quantum annealing instead uses an evolving physical system intended to reach low-energy configurations of a problem Hamiltonian. Its performance depends on the problem encoding, device properties, noise, embedding overhead and the time required to obtain reliable solutions.

23.6.4 How a hybrid optimisation loop works

Classical optimiser proposes parameters
Quantum circuit evaluates the candidate
Measurements estimate the objective
Classical optimiser updates parameters

This loop is central to many variational quantum algorithms. The quantum device evaluates an objective or a related measurement, while a classical optimiser decides how the parameters should change.

The process is repeated until the stopping condition is met. However, every objective estimate can require multiple circuit executions, and noisy estimates can make the classical optimisation loop harder to control.

23.6.5 Quantum search and optimisation

Grover's search algorithm provides a quadratic query advantage for unstructured search under its standard assumptions. If there are N possible candidates and one can efficiently test whether a candidate is acceptable, the number of oracle queries can scale as O(√N), rather than O(N).

This is a genuine theoretical result, but it is not a universal quadratic speedup for ML optimisation. Applying it to an optimisation problem requires a suitable search procedure, an efficient test and a careful accounting of how the objective is evaluated.

23.6.6 Why there is no universal quantum optimiser

Optimisation problems have different mathematical structures. A method designed for discrete binary decisions may not be suitable for training a large neural network with millions of continuous parameters.

Similarly, a quantum circuit that improves a small benchmark does not establish that it will improve a production model. The result must be compared with strong classical optimisation methods, including well-tuned heuristics and specialised solvers.

23.6.7 How should speedup be measured?

A useful comparison includes more than the time spent inside a quantum circuit. The total cost may include circuit compilation, state preparation, device communication, measurement repetitions, classical optimisation and post-processing.

Total runtime = input preparation + computation + measurement + post-processing

The equation is a conceptual accounting identity rather than a complexity bound. In a complete experiment, each term should be measured or estimated consistently for the classical and quantum implementations.

Remember: quantum optimisation is a collection of approaches, not one algorithm that makes every training problem faster. Its value depends on the objective, encoding, available hardware and the strength of the classical baseline.

23.7 Quantum-Enhanced Gradient Descent

23.7.1 What is gradient descent?

Gradient descent is one of the most widely used optimisation methods in machine learning. It improves model parameters by moving them in a direction that reduces the loss.

The gradient contains the partial derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters. Each derivative tells us how the loss changes when one parameter changes slightly, while the other parameters are held fixed.

θₜ₊₁ = θₜ - η ∇L(θₜ)

Here θₜ is the current parameter vector, η is the learning rate, and ∇L(θₜ) is the gradient. The minus sign means that the update moves against the gradient when the aim is to minimise the loss.

23.7.2 A simple example

Suppose the loss for one parameter is:

L(θ) = (θ - 3)²

dL/dθ = 2(θ - 3)

The loss is smallest when θ equals 3. If the current value is θ = 1, the derivative is -4. With a learning rate of 0.1, the update becomes:

θnew = 1 - 0.1(-4) = 1.4

The parameter moves from 1 towards 3. Repeating the process can move it closer to the minimum, provided the learning rate and optimisation conditions are suitable.

23.7.3 Where does quantum computing enter?

In quantum-enhanced gradient methods, a quantum circuit evaluates a parameter-dependent quantity. Measurements estimate an expectation value, and the classical optimiser uses those estimates to update parameters.

This is especially natural in variational quantum circuits, where trainable gate parameters define a quantum model. A quantum circuit can also be part of a hybrid model that accepts classical features and returns a prediction.

For example, let a circuit produce an expectation value:

f(θ) = ⟨ψ(θ)| O |ψ(θ)⟩

The state |ψ(θ)⟩ depends on trainable parameters, and O is an observable whose measurement produces an expectation value. If this output contributes to the loss, training requires estimating how the expectation changes with θ.

23.7.4 The parameter-shift rule

For certain parameterised quantum gates, the derivative can be calculated using two shifted circuit evaluations. For a common rotation-gate convention, the rule is:

df/dθ = [f(θ + π/2) - f(θ - π/2)] / 2

This exact expression applies to suitable gates and parameter conventions; it is not a universal derivative formula for every quantum circuit. More general gates or parameterisations may require a different shift rule or a sum of several shifted evaluations.

The key idea is that quantum derivatives can sometimes be obtained by evaluating the same circuit at carefully chosen parameter values. The quantum hardware does not need to expose a classical description of every intermediate amplitude.

23.7.5 What does a gradient estimate cost?

Although the parameter-shift rule is exact at the expectation-value level, a real quantum device estimates each expectation using a finite number of measurements, often called shots.

For a circuit with many parameters, evaluating the gradient may require many shifted circuit settings. The measurement cost can therefore grow with the number of trainable parameters, the precision required and the noise level.

More shots generally reduce statistical uncertainty, but they also increase runtime. A noisy gradient can point in a less useful direction, causing slower convergence or unstable updates.

23.7.6 Can a quantum computer speed up classical gradient descent?

This question needs a careful distinction. There is a difference between training a model that contains a quantum circuit and using a quantum algorithm to speed up optimisation of an ordinary classical model.

In a hybrid quantum neural network, the quantum circuit is part of the model itself. Quantum gradient estimation helps train that circuit, but it does not automatically make the training of an ordinary classical neural network faster.

For a classical objective, a quantum method would need to show how it obtains useful gradient information more efficiently than classical alternatives. It must also account for input access, gradient accuracy and the cost of converting quantum results into classical parameter updates.

23.7.7 Connection to stochastic gradient descent

Classical stochastic gradient descent estimates gradients using a subset, or mini-batch, of the training examples. This reduces the work required for each update and introduces statistical noise into the optimisation.

Quantum gradient estimation introduces another possible source of uncertainty: finite-shot measurement noise. In a hybrid training system, the total gradient estimate may therefore contain both data-sampling noise and quantum measurement noise.

An effective training method must balance batch size, number of measurement shots, learning rate and total circuit evaluations. More accurate gradients are not always worth their additional cost, especially early in training when approximate updates may be sufficient.

Remember: quantum gradient methods provide techniques for differentiating parameterised quantum computations. Whether they improve an ML training pipeline depends on gradient cost, statistical noise, hardware limitations and the quality of the final model.

Connecting All Seven Topics

The seven topics may appear unrelated at first. In reality, they are connected by a common idea: each quantum algorithm targets a mathematical operation that appears inside a larger ML workflow.

Topic Underlying task What the quantum method seeks to improve Main challenge
Quantum PCA Eigenvalue and eigenvector analysis Extracting dominant spectral information State preparation and spectral precision
Quantum linear regression Solving linear systems Preparing a state related to the solution Conditioning and classical readout
Quantum clustering Distance and similarity calculations Estimating selected similarities Data access and centroid updates
Quantum recommendations Low-rank matrix processing Selected predictions or compact summaries Fair classical comparison and output cost
Quantum Boltzmann sampling Sampling from energy-based distributions Generating samples from useful quantum states State preparation and classical simulability
Quantum optimisation Finding low-cost solutions Searching or exploring particular solution spaces Noise and strong classical baselines
Quantum gradient descent Estimating derivatives Training parameterised quantum computations Shot cost and gradient variance

A useful way to classify quantum ML algorithms

One useful distinction is whether the quantum computer returns a classical answer or prepares a quantum state that another operation can use.

Classical-output algorithms aim to return ordinary values, such as a predicted score or a set of labels. These values are easy to use in conventional software, but obtaining many of them may require substantial measurement and post-processing.

Quantum-state-output algorithms prepare states that encode a solution or distribution. These can be valuable when the next step is also quantum. If the next step requires the entire answer as a classical array, readout can become the bottleneck.

This distinction connects quantum PCA, linear-system algorithms and quantum recommendation systems particularly closely. It also explains why a theoretical speedup for an internal operation may not translate directly into a faster end-to-end ML application.

How to Evaluate a Quantum ML Speedup Properly

A strong research claim should answer the following questions before concluding that a quantum method is superior.

  1. What exactly is being computed? Define the input, the output and the accuracy required.
  2. How is the data accessed? Include the cost of preparing quantum states or querying a data structure.
  3. What assumptions are needed? State the conditions involving sparsity, rank, conditioning, spectral gaps or sampling access.
  4. What is the full resource cost? Include circuit depth, measurements, repetitions, communication and classical processing.
  5. What is the best classical baseline? Compare against suitable optimised classical algorithms, not just a deliberately slow implementation.
  6. Does the result matter to the ML task? A faster mathematical subroutine is useful only if it improves a meaningful end-to-end objective.
Research principle: a quantum speedup is a mathematical claim under stated assumptions. A practical quantum advantage is an experimental or application-level claim that also accounts for real hardware, input preparation, accuracy, total cost and competitive classical methods. These are related, but they are not the same claim.

Chapter Summary

Quantum algorithms for classical ML subroutines explore whether quantum computing can improve the mathematical building blocks used by machine learning. Quantum PCA targets spectral information. Quantum linear-system methods target solution states for equations. Quantum clustering explores faster similarity estimation. Recommendation algorithms investigate low-rank structures, while dequantization checks whether comparable classical methods can achieve the same result.

Quantum Boltzmann sampling connects quantum statistical mechanics with probabilistic models. Quantum optimisation investigates the search for good solutions, and quantum-enhanced gradient methods study the derivatives required to train parameterised computations.

Across all these areas, the same practical issues return: how data enters the quantum system, how much information measurement reveals, how errors affect the answer, and whether the complete algorithm beats a strong classical alternative.

The central lesson: quantum computing is not automatically faster because a problem has many variables or because a quantum state has many amplitudes. Its promise lies in finding a carefully defined task whose structure can be exploited by quantum mechanics, while keeping input, output and implementation costs under control.

Suggested learning order

Study classical PCA, linear regression, clustering, matrix factorisation, probability distributions and gradient descent first. Then study quantum state preparation, quantum phase estimation, linear-system algorithms, quantum measurement and variational circuits. This order makes the purpose and limitations of each quantum algorithm much easier to understand.
