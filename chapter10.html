Quantum Computing · Chapter 10

Variational and Near-Term Quantum Algorithms

How quantum computers and classical computers can work together to solve scientific and mathematical problems, even when quantum hardware is still noisy and limited.

Topics: NISQ computing, VQE, QAOA, ansatz design, classical optimisation, chemistry and combinatorial optimisation.

Introduction: What Can We Do With Today's Quantum Computers?

A quantum computer has a remarkable ability to represent and manipulate quantum states. However, today's quantum machines cannot perform every quantum algorithm reliably. Variational algorithms were developed to make useful progress within these limitations.

Imagine that you have a calculator which can perform certain complicated operations that an ordinary calculator cannot perform efficiently. Unfortunately, this special calculator sometimes produces errors, has limited memory, and cannot execute a very long sequence of operations reliably.

Would you abandon it completely? Not necessarily. You could use it only for the operations where it has an advantage and let an ordinary computer perform the remaining work. You could also repeat measurements to reduce uncertainty and keep the quantum calculation as short as possible.

This is the basic idea behind many variational quantum algorithms. A quantum processor prepares a state and measures a quantity of interest. A classical computer analyses the result and changes the quantum circuit's parameters. The process continues until a useful solution is found or a stopping condition is reached.

The loop repeats. The classical computer decides what to try next using information obtained from the quantum processor.

Two especially important examples are the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), which is often studied in connection with chemistry and ground-state energies, and the Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA), which is designed for discrete optimisation problems.

Before studying these algorithms, we need to understand why near-term quantum computing requires a different approach from the large, fault-tolerant quantum computers imagined for the future.

10.1 The NISQ Paradigm

What does NISQ mean?

NISQ stands for Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum. The term describes a generation of quantum computers that have a useful number of physical qubits but still suffer from noise and limited circuit capabilities.

The word intermediate-scale indicates that these processors are beyond the smallest experimental demonstrations. They may contain tens, hundreds, or more physical qubits, depending on the device and the period being discussed. The number of qubits alone, however, does not tell us how powerful the computer is.

The word noisy is equally important. Quantum information is delicate. Interactions with the surrounding environment, imperfect control pulses, inaccurate gates and measurement errors can change the state we intended to prepare.

Why are quantum states so delicate?

A classical bit is normally represented as either 0 or 1. A qubit can be prepared in a superposition of its computational basis states:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩
|α|² + |β|² = 1

Here, α and β are complex probability amplitudes. When we measure the qubit in the computational basis, we obtain 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|².

A quantum algorithm relies not only on these probabilities but also on the relationships between amplitudes. Quantum gates create interference, which can increase the likelihood of useful outcomes and decrease the likelihood of unwanted outcomes. Noise can disturb those relationships.

The main limitations of NISQ devices

1. Gate errors

A gate is a controlled operation on one or more qubits. Real gates are not perfect. A small error in an individual operation can accumulate when a circuit contains many operations.

2. Decoherence

A qubit can lose the quantum properties needed by a computation through unwanted interactions with its environment. Coherence times therefore limit how long delicate quantum information can be preserved.

3. Measurement errors

The hardware may report 1 when the qubit was actually in 0, or the reverse. Repeated measurements help us estimate probabilities, but they do not automatically eliminate systematic measurement errors.

4. Limited connectivity

Some hardware allows direct interactions only between particular pairs of qubits. If an algorithm needs an interaction between distant qubits, the compiler may need additional operations, such as SWAP gates. These increase circuit depth and can introduce more errors.

5. Limited circuit depth and execution time

A deep circuit contains many sequential operations. Even if every individual gate is reasonably accurate, the complete circuit may become unreliable when too many operations are performed before measurement.

Why not use quantum error correction for everything?

Quantum error correction encodes logical quantum information across multiple physical qubits and uses measurements to detect error syndromes. A sufficiently capable fault-tolerant quantum computer can protect logical information and execute much longer computations.

The difficulty is that reliable logical qubits require additional physical resources, carefully controlled operations and repeated error-correction procedures. Large-scale fault-tolerant machines therefore demand substantial hardware and engineering effort.

NISQ algorithms take a different route: they try to perform useful computations with the available hardware without assuming that a large fault-tolerant machine already exists. This does not mean they eliminate noise or replace error correction in the long term.

Why variational algorithms suit the NISQ era

Variational algorithms use parameterised circuits, often called ansätze. The quantum processor executes a circuit with selected parameters, and a classical computer chooses new parameters based on the measured result.

The quantum circuit is usually kept relatively shallow compared with many algorithms designed for fault-tolerant hardware. However, shallow does not automatically mean successful. Some problems require deep circuits, and some variational methods encounter optimisation difficulties even when their circuits are short.

Central idea: NISQ computing is about working within current hardware limitations. Variational algorithms divide a problem between quantum state preparation and measurement on one side, and classical optimisation on the other.

Are NISQ algorithms guaranteed to outperform classical computers?

No. This is one of the most important points in this chapter. The fact that an algorithm uses a quantum computer does not establish a quantum advantage. A useful comparison must account for classical algorithms, data preparation, circuit execution, measurement repetitions, hardware errors and the quality of the final answer.

Variational algorithms are promising research tools, but demonstrating a practical, repeatable advantage for a real problem remains a separate challenge.

The General Variational Principle

VQE and QAOA are different algorithms, but both use a common mathematical idea: choose a parameterised quantum state and adjust its parameters to improve a measurable objective.

What is a parameterised quantum circuit?

Consider a circuit containing rotation gates. A rotation gate changes a qubit's state according to an angle. Instead of fixing every angle in advance, we leave some angles as adjustable parameters:

|ψ(θ)⟩ = U(θ)|0...0⟩

In this expression, |0...0⟩ is the initial state, U(θ) is the quantum circuit, and θ represents the collection of parameters. The notation |ψ(θ)⟩ means that the resulting state depends on those parameters.

If the circuit has five adjustable rotation angles, for example, the parameter vector can be written as:

θ = (θ₁, θ₂, θ₃, θ₄, θ₅)

Changing θ changes the state prepared by the circuit. We then measure the state to estimate an objective function, such as energy or the cost of an optimisation problem.

The objective function

An objective function tells us how good a particular parameter choice is. In a minimisation problem, smaller values are better. In a maximisation problem, larger values are better.

θ* = arg minθ C(θ)

Here, C(θ) is the objective function and θ* represents a parameter choice that minimises it. The notation arg min asks for the parameter values, rather than merely the minimum numerical value.

The classical computer proposes parameters. The quantum computer prepares the corresponding state and returns measurement data. The classical computer estimates the objective and selects another set of parameters. This loop continues until a stopping condition is met.

Why is the quantum measurement repeated?

A single measurement does not reveal the complete quantum state. It returns one outcome. If the same circuit is prepared and measured repeatedly, the outcomes can be used to estimate probabilities and expectation values.

Suppose a measurement returns 1 in 70 out of 100 shots. The estimated probability of obtaining 1 is 0.70. This is an estimate, not necessarily the exact underlying probability. Increasing the number of independent shots generally improves the statistical estimate, although it also increases execution cost.

A variational algorithm is not simply a quantum circuit that runs once. It is a repeated computational process involving circuit execution, measurement, classical optimisation and decisions about what to run next.

10.2 Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)

First, what is an eigenvalue problem?

Eigenvalues appear throughout mathematics, physics, engineering and computer science. For a matrix A, an eigenvector |v⟩ and its corresponding eigenvalue E satisfy:

A|v⟩ = E|v⟩

The matrix transforms the eigenvector only by multiplying it by the number E. The direction represented by the eigenvector is preserved, although its scale can change.

In quantum mechanics, the Hamiltonian H represents the energy of a system. Its eigenvalues correspond to allowed energy values, while its eigenstates describe the associated quantum states.

H|ψ⟩ = E|ψ⟩

The lowest energy eigenvalue is called the ground-state energy. Finding it is important because the ground state often determines the most stable configuration of a physical system under the conditions being modelled.

Why is finding ground-state energy difficult?

A system of n qubits has a state space of dimension 2ⁿ. For example, 10 qubits have 1,024 computational basis states, while 50 qubits have more than one quadrillion basis states. Describing a general pure state exactly requires a number of amplitudes that grows exponentially with n.

Real molecules introduce further complexity because electrons interact with one another, and their quantum states must obey the rules of quantum mechanics. Classical methods can solve many chemical problems effectively, but accurately treating certain strongly correlated systems can be difficult.

VQE attempts to estimate the ground-state energy by preparing candidate states on a quantum processor and using a classical computer to improve them.

The variational principle

Let H be a Hamiltonian and |ψ⟩ any valid normalised trial state. Its expected energy is:

E(θ) = ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩

The variational principle states that this expectation value cannot be lower than the true ground-state energy E₀:

E(θ) ≥ E₀

This inequality assumes that H is the correct Hermitian Hamiltonian for the system and that |ψ(θ)⟩ is a valid normalised state. The energy expectation can equal E₀ if the trial state lies entirely in the ground-state subspace.

This gives VQE a useful target. We do not have to construct the exact ground state immediately. We begin with a manageable family of candidate states and search for the one with the lowest measured energy.

How VQE works, step by step

Step 1: Define the physical system

Decide what system is being studied, such as a molecule with a particular geometry. Construct its Hamiltonian, which describes the energy contributions and interactions relevant to the chosen model.

Step 2: Encode the Hamiltonian into qubit operations

Electronic structure problems are often expressed using fermionic creation and annihilation operators. These can be mapped to qubit operators, producing a Hamiltonian represented as a weighted sum of Pauli strings.

H = Σᵢ cᵢ Pᵢ

Each cᵢ is a real coefficient for a Hermitian Hamiltonian, and each Pᵢ is a tensor product of Pauli operators such as I, X, Y and Z. For example, Z ⊗ Z represents the product of Z measurements on two qubits.

Step 3: Choose a trial-state circuit

Select a parameterised circuit U(θ). Starting from an initial state, the circuit prepares a candidate state |ψ(θ)⟩. The circuit structure is called the ansatz.

Step 4: Estimate the energy

Since the Hamiltonian is a sum of Pauli strings, we can estimate the expectation value of each term and combine them using its coefficient.

E(θ) = Σᵢ cᵢ ⟨ψ(θ)|Pᵢ|ψ(θ)⟩

The terms are measured using suitable circuits and repeated shots. Some compatible Pauli terms can be measured together, reducing the total measurement workload.

Step 5: Update the parameters

A classical optimiser receives the energy estimate and proposes a new parameter vector. The goal is to lower the estimated energy.

Step 6: Repeat and assess convergence

The quantum circuit is executed again with the new parameters. The process continues until the energy stops improving sufficiently, a budget is exhausted, or another stopping condition is reached.

The optimiser returns new parameters to the ansatz circuit. The loop continues until the chosen stopping condition is met.

A simple numerical example

Suppose the true ground-state energy of a small model is -1.00 in the selected energy units. We do not know the exact ground state, so we try several parameter settings.

Trial Estimated energy Interpretation
A -0.60 The trial state is relatively far from the ground-state energy.
B -0.84 The new parameters have improved the energy estimate.
C -0.96 The trial energy is closer to the ground-state energy.
D -0.98 A further improvement has been achieved.

These are illustrative values, not results from a real experiment. They demonstrate the direction of the search. The variational principle means the exact expectation value of a valid trial state cannot fall below the true ground-state energy. In an experiment, statistical uncertainty can make an estimated value appear to violate this inequality slightly.

How does VQE estimate the energy from measurements?

Consider a one-qubit Hamiltonian:

H = 2I + 0.5Z

Suppose the prepared state produces the outcome 0 with probability 0.8 and the outcome 1 with probability 0.2. The Z measurement assigns +1 to outcome 0 and -1 to outcome 1. Therefore:

⟨Z⟩ = (0.8 × 1) + (0.2 × -1) = 0.6
E = 2 + 0.5(0.6) = 2.3

This is the energy expectation for the given state. VQE would try other parameter settings to find a lower expectation value. In this example, the minimum eigenvalue of H is 1.5, obtained for the state |1⟩.

What does a successful VQE calculation give us?

The primary result is an estimate of the energy of the optimised trial state. The result can be compared with known calculations or experimental values. In molecular applications, researchers may also calculate energy differences, such as those between molecular geometries.

However, a low energy estimate does not automatically provide every property of a molecule. Additional measurements or calculations may be required to determine other observables, chemical properties or the quality of the prepared state.

Strengths and limitations of VQE

Strengths Limitations
Can use relatively shallow parameterised circuits. Shallow circuits may not represent the required ground state.
Combines quantum measurements with classical optimisation. Many repeated circuit executions may be necessary.
The variational principle provides an energy upper bound for exact trial-state expectations. A poor ansatz can give a poor upper bound even after optimisation.
Can be applied to molecular and other Hamiltonian problems. Noise, measurement cost and optimiser behaviour can limit accuracy.
Remember: VQE does not directly calculate every energy eigenvalue. It searches over a chosen family of trial states to minimise the expectation value of a Hamiltonian.

10.3 Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA)

From physical energy to a mathematical cost

VQE is often introduced through the problem of finding a low-energy quantum state. QAOA uses a related variational approach for discrete optimisation problems, where we want to find a good assignment of values to a collection of variables.

Examples include selecting a group of connected vertices, scheduling jobs, assigning resources and choosing routes. Many of these problems have a huge number of possible solutions, making an exhaustive search impractical for large instances.

The aim of QAOA is to prepare a quantum state whose measurement is likely to produce a good solution to the chosen optimisation problem.

What is combinatorial optimisation?

Suppose a delivery company must choose which roads to use, subject to a budget or distance constraint. Every possible route is a candidate solution. We can assign each candidate a cost, such as its total travel distance, and seek the route with the smallest cost.

Other problems may ask us to maximise a score instead. A common way to handle this is to negate the score and minimise the resulting cost. The exact formulation depends on the problem.

Encoding a solution with bits

Many discrete problems can be expressed using binary variables:

xᵢ ∈ {0, 1}

A value of 1 might mean that an item is selected, while 0 means it is not. If a problem has n binary variables, there are 2ⁿ possible assignments before considering any constraints.

Quantum algorithms represent these assignments using computational basis states. For example, the three-bit string 101 corresponds to the basis state |101⟩.

Constructing a cost Hamiltonian

QAOA expresses the problem's cost as an operator called the cost Hamiltonian, usually written as H_C. It is constructed so that its eigenvalue on a computational basis state represents the cost of the corresponding assignment.

H_C|x⟩ = C(x)|x⟩

Here, x is a binary assignment and C(x) is its cost. If we minimise the cost, an optimal assignment corresponds to a basis state with the smallest eigenvalue of H_C.

For many problems, the cost can be expressed using products of binary variables. These terms can be converted into combinations of Pauli-Z operators using the relationship between a binary variable and a Z measurement:

xᵢ = (1 - Zᵢ)/2

This is an operator relationship when Zᵢ represents the Pauli-Z operator on qubit i. Its eigenvalues are +1 and -1, so the expression maps the computational basis value 0 to 0 and the value 1 to 1.

The two operations at the heart of QAOA

QAOA repeatedly combines two kinds of unitary operations: a cost-dependent operation and a mixing operation.

1. Cost unitary

U_C(γ) = exp(-iγH_C)

The cost unitary applies phases that depend on the cost of each basis state. It does not change the measurement probabilities of a state if applied alone, but it changes relative phases between basis states. Those phase changes can affect later interference.

2. Mixer unitary

A common mixer is built from Pauli-X operators:

H_M = Σᵢ Xᵢ
U_M(β) = exp(-iβH_M)

The mixer helps move amplitude between computational basis states. When combined with the cost unitary, it allows the circuit to redistribute measurement probabilities so that good solutions may become more likely.

The standard X mixer is not the only possibility. Some constrained problems use specialised mixers designed to preserve feasibility, such as keeping exactly a specified number of variables equal to 1.

How QAOA works, step by step

Step 1: Formulate the optimisation problem

Express the objective and any constraints mathematically. Choose a representation that maps candidate solutions to binary variables or quantum basis states.

Step 2: Build the cost Hamiltonian

Construct H_C so that the cost of a basis state matches the problem's objective value, subject to the chosen encoding and any penalty terms used for constraints.

Step 3: Prepare an initial state

The standard unconstrained QAOA approach often starts with a uniform superposition over all computational basis states:

|+⟩⊗ⁿ = (1/√(2ⁿ)) Σₓ |x⟩

This state gives equal initial measurement probability to each binary assignment.

Step 4: Apply cost and mixer operations

Apply a cost unitary followed by a mixer unitary. Repeat this pair p times, with adjustable angles γ and β at each layer.

|ψ(γ, β)⟩ = U_M(βₚ)U_C(γₚ) ... U_M(β₁)U_C(γ₁)|+⟩⊗ⁿ

The integer p is the QAOA depth parameter. Each layer has a cost angle γ and a mixer angle β. Increasing p gives the circuit more flexibility, but also generally increases the number of operations and the risk of noise.

Step 5: Measure candidate solutions

Measure the qubits in the computational basis. Each shot produces a bit string, which can be translated into a candidate solution and evaluated using the original classical cost function.

Step 6: Optimise the angles

Estimate the expected cost from repeated measurements. A classical optimiser proposes new values for the angles in order to lower the expected cost.

Step 7: Select and verify a solution

After optimisation, collect candidate bit strings, evaluate their actual costs, check feasibility and retain a good solution. The most frequently measured bit string is not guaranteed to be the best one.

A simple example: the Max-Cut problem

Imagine a network containing several vertices connected by edges. We want to divide the vertices into two groups so that as many edges as possible connect vertices belonging to different groups.

This is called Max-Cut. It has applications in graph optimisation and serves as a standard example for QAOA.

Vertices are assigned to two groups. Every edge joining different groups contributes to the cut.

Assign each vertex a binary variable xᵢ. If two vertices have different values, their connecting edge belongs to the cut. If their values are equal, it does not.

For an edge between vertices i and j, its contribution can be written as:

Cutᵢⱼ = xᵢ(1 - xⱼ) + xⱼ(1 - xᵢ)
Cutᵢⱼ = xᵢ + xⱼ - 2xᵢxⱼ

Adding these contributions across all edges gives the total cut size. Since standard QAOA is commonly written as a minimisation of cost, we can define the cost as the negative cut size. Minimising that cost maximises the number of cut edges.

The quantum circuit prepares candidate assignments. The classical optimiser adjusts the angles, and the measurements provide candidate cuts. QAOA is useful here because it searches through a probability distribution over possible assignments rather than simply evaluating one assignment per circuit execution.

Does QAOA guarantee the optimal answer?

Not in general. At a finite depth p, the algorithm may produce a distribution that favours good solutions without placing all its probability on the optimum. The result depends on the graph, circuit depth, initial state, optimiser, noise and measurement budget.

For a small problem, we can often calculate the optimum classically and compare the QAOA output against it. For larger problems, finding a strong classical baseline and evaluating solution quality becomes more challenging.

Remember: VQE typically minimises a physical energy expectation. QAOA typically prepares candidate solutions for a discrete optimisation problem by alternating cost-dependent and mixing operations.

10.4 Ansatz Design: Hardware-Efficient vs. Problem-Inspired

What exactly is an ansatz?

An ansatz is a chosen mathematical form for a trial state. In a variational quantum algorithm, it is usually implemented as a parameterised quantum circuit.

Think of an ansatz as a collection of possible states that the algorithm is allowed to explore. The optimiser chooses parameter values, but it cannot generally leave the family of states that the circuit structure can produce.

This creates a fundamental trade-off. An ansatz should be expressive enough to represent a useful solution, but it should also be executable on the available hardware and manageable for the classical optimiser.

1. Hardware-efficient ansätze

A hardware-efficient ansatz is designed around the native gates, connectivity and practical constraints of a quantum processor. It often consists of repeated layers of single-qubit rotations and entangling gates.

A simplified layer might contain rotations around the Y and Z axes on each qubit, followed by entangling operations between selected pairs of qubits. The rotation angles are adjustable parameters.

U(θ) = Uₗ(θₗ) ... U₂(θ₂)U₁(θ₁)

Each layer Uₖ contains a pattern of gates. Repeating the pattern creates a larger family of possible states.

Why use this approach?

  • It can be adapted to the hardware's available gates and connectivity.
  • It may avoid some of the extra operations required to compile an incompatible circuit.
  • It is flexible and can be used for a range of problems.
  • Its structure can be relatively straightforward to generate automatically.

What is the drawback?

The circuit may not reflect the physics or mathematical structure of the problem. It may need many layers before it can represent the desired state. More layers mean more parameters, more gates and more opportunities for noise.

A particularly important problem is a barren plateau. In certain ansätze and circumstances, the gradients of the objective can become extremely small as the number of qubits grows. The optimiser then receives little useful information about which direction to move. Barren plateaus are not inevitable in every variational circuit, but they are a serious design concern.

2. Problem-inspired ansätze

A problem-inspired ansatz is designed using knowledge of the problem being solved. Instead of choosing a generic sequence of gates, we use the problem's structure to guide circuit construction.

QAOA is a standard example. Its cost unitary comes from the objective function, while its mixer is selected to move between candidate assignments. The circuit is therefore connected directly to the optimisation problem.

In chemistry, a problem-inspired ansatz may reflect electronic excitations, symmetries or relationships in the molecular Hamiltonian. The unitary coupled-cluster family is one well-known example used in quantum chemistry.

Why use this approach?

  • The circuit structure can reflect meaningful properties of the problem.
  • It may represent relevant states more efficiently than a generic circuit.
  • It can preserve useful constraints or symmetries when designed carefully.
  • It can reduce the search space by excluding irrelevant state transformations.

What is the drawback?

The design may require substantial domain knowledge. A problem-inspired circuit may also contain complicated operations that are expensive to implement on real hardware. If the ansatz is too restrictive, it may be unable to represent the required solution.

Comparing the two approaches

Property Hardware-efficient Problem-inspired
Main design goal Fit the circuit to the hardware. Fit the circuit to the problem's structure.
Gate selection Often based on native gates and connectivity. Often based on the Hamiltonian, constraints or known physics.
Flexibility Can be broadly reusable. May be specialised to a particular problem family.
Domain knowledge May require less knowledge of the problem's internal structure. Often requires more mathematical or physical knowledge.
Main risk May need many layers or become difficult to optimise. May be difficult to implement or too restrictive.

How should we choose an ansatz?

There is no universally best ansatz. The choice depends on the objective, the available qubits, the hardware's native gates, the connectivity, the acceptable circuit depth and the optimiser's behaviour.

A sensible design process begins with the smallest circuit that can represent meaningful candidate states. We then test whether increasing its expressive power improves the solution enough to justify the extra operations and measurement cost.

Design principle: A more expressive circuit is not automatically a better circuit. The goal is to find a useful balance between expressiveness, hardware cost and trainability.

10.5 Classical Optimisers for Variational Circuits

Why does a quantum algorithm need a classical optimiser?

A parameterised quantum circuit may contain several adjustable angles. Even a circuit with ten parameters has an enormous number of possible real-valued parameter combinations. We need a systematic method to decide which values to try.

The classical optimiser performs this search. It receives estimates of the objective function, and sometimes estimates of its gradient, then proposes the next parameter vector.

The difficulty is that evaluating the objective can require many quantum circuit executions, and every estimate can contain statistical noise. An optimiser that works well on a clean mathematical function may behave differently when each evaluation is expensive and uncertain.

1. Gradient-based methods

A gradient describes how the objective changes as the parameters change. For an objective C(θ), the gradient is the vector of partial derivatives:

∇C(θ) = (∂C/∂θ₁, ∂C/∂θ₂, ..., ∂C/∂θₙ)

For minimisation, a simple gradient-descent update is:

θₜ₊₁ = θₜ - η∇C(θₜ)

Here, η is the learning rate, which controls the step size. If the step is too large, the search may overshoot useful regions. If it is too small, convergence may be slow.

Gradient-based methods are attractive when reliable gradients can be obtained. However, gradients estimated from noisy measurements may be inaccurate. Small gradients can also make optimisation difficult.

2. The parameter-shift rule

For many parameterised quantum gates, derivatives can be calculated using additional circuit evaluations rather than ordinary numerical differentiation alone. A common example is the parameter-shift rule.

∂C/∂θ = [C(θ + π/2) - C(θ - π/2)] / 2

This form applies to common rotation parameters under the appropriate gate and objective assumptions. It is not a universal derivative formula for every possible circuit parameterisation.

The rule tells us to run circuits with shifted parameter values, measure their objectives and combine the estimates. It is useful because the derivative is obtained from valid quantum circuit evaluations. The cost is that estimating a gradient can require additional executions.

3. Gradient-free methods

Gradient-free methods choose new parameters using function evaluations without explicitly requiring a gradient. They can be useful when gradient estimates are unreliable or expensive, although they also face difficulties in high dimensions and under noisy evaluations.

Nelder–Mead

Nelder–Mead maintains a collection of candidate points, called a simplex. It changes this collection through operations such as reflection, expansion and contraction. The method uses objective values to guide the search.

It is relatively simple to use for problems with a small number of parameters. However, it does not generally guarantee finding a global optimum and can become expensive as the number of parameters increases.

COBYLA

COBYLA is a derivative-free optimisation method that builds local linear approximations to the objective and constraints. It can be useful when derivatives are unavailable and constraints need to be handled.

Like other local optimisation methods, it may converge to a solution that is not globally optimal. Noisy objective estimates can also affect its behaviour.

SPSA: Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation

SPSA estimates a gradient using simultaneous perturbations of the parameter vector. Rather than estimating every component through separate parameter shifts, it perturbs many parameters together in a carefully chosen direction.

This can reduce the number of objective evaluations needed for an approximate gradient, especially when the circuit has many parameters. However, the estimates can be noisy, and its performance depends on the perturbation and learning-rate schedules.

4. Adam and adaptive gradient methods

Adam is a widely used optimiser in machine learning. It tracks moving averages of gradients and squared gradients to adapt the effective step size for each parameter. It can also be used in variational quantum algorithms when sufficiently useful gradient estimates are available.

Adam is not automatically superior to gradient-free methods for quantum circuits. Its success depends on gradient quality, measurement noise, circuit structure and the shape of the objective function.

Comparison of common optimisers

Optimiser Uses gradients? Potential benefit Important concern
Gradient descent Yes Simple and interpretable updates. Step-size sensitivity and costly gradients.
Adam Yes Adaptive parameter-wise updates. Noisy gradients may still be problematic.
Nelder–Mead No Simple derivative-free search. Can scale poorly with many parameters.
COBYLA No Handles local approximations and constraints. May be sensitive to noisy evaluations.
SPSA Approximate gradient Uses simultaneous perturbations. Gradient estimates can have high variance.

Why optimisation is unusually difficult for quantum circuits

In ordinary machine learning, an objective evaluation may be computed directly by software using the available data. In a variational quantum algorithm, objective evaluation can require preparing a quantum state, executing gates, repeating measurements and estimating expectation values.

This creates several practical challenges:

  • Shot noise: a finite number of measurements produces uncertain objective estimates.
  • Hardware noise: gate and measurement errors can distort the objective.
  • Expensive evaluations: each proposed parameter vector may require many circuit executions.
  • Local minima and flat regions: the optimiser may struggle to discover better parameters.
  • Changing evaluation accuracy: using too few shots early may mislead the search, while using too many shots throughout can waste resources.

How do researchers make the optimisation more reliable?

They may initialise parameters carefully, compare multiple optimiser families, exploit known problem structure and adjust the measurement budget during the search. For example, a promising candidate may receive more shots to estimate its objective more accurately.

Researchers also monitor the objective over time, test multiple initial points and compare against simpler baselines. An apparently converged optimiser does not prove that the global optimum has been found.

Important distinction: The quantum circuit defines the states that can be explored. The classical optimiser determines how the parameters are searched. Improving one does not automatically solve the limitations of the other.

10.6 Applications in Chemistry and Combinatorial Optimisation

Application 1: Quantum chemistry

Chemistry is a natural area for quantum computing because molecules themselves obey quantum mechanics. Electrons interact, occupy quantum states and influence the energies and properties of molecules.

A quantum computer can, in principle, represent certain quantum states in ways that are difficult for classical computers to simulate exactly. Variational methods attempt to use this capability without requiring a long, fault-tolerant computation.

Finding molecular ground-state energies

The electronic Hamiltonian describes the kinetic energy of electrons and their interactions with nuclei and with one another. After selecting a finite basis set and applying an appropriate mapping, it can be expressed in terms of qubit operators.

VQE prepares a candidate electronic state and estimates the energy associated with it. The classical optimiser adjusts the ansatz parameters to lower the energy.

A sufficiently accurate energy calculation can contribute to the study of molecular stability, reaction energetics and chemical bonding. However, useful chemistry often requires accurate energy differences rather than a single absolute energy. Those differences can be small compared with the total energies involved, making precision particularly important.

Understanding chemical reactions

A chemical reaction can be studied by calculating the energies of relevant molecular configurations. By examining how energy changes as the geometry changes, researchers can investigate reaction pathways and energetic barriers.

A variational quantum calculation is one possible component of this workflow. It does not automatically provide a complete reaction mechanism. Molecular geometry, basis-set choices, nuclear motion and other effects may also need to be considered.

Studying strongly correlated systems

In some systems, electrons cannot be described accurately by simple approximations in which each electron behaves almost independently. Such systems are called strongly correlated, and they can be difficult for certain classical computational methods.

Quantum algorithms are being investigated as possible tools for these problems. Whether a particular VQE implementation offers a practical advantage depends on the molecular model, ansatz, hardware quality and available classical methods.

Application 2: Combinatorial optimisation

Combinatorial optimisation appears whenever a problem requires choosing among a large number of discrete possibilities. The solution must satisfy the problem's requirements while minimising a cost or maximising a score.

Graph problems

Graphs represent relationships between objects. Vertices can represent people, machines or locations, while edges represent connections. Problems such as Max-Cut, graph colouring and certain network-design tasks can be expressed using binary variables and objective functions.

QAOA can prepare candidate assignments and optimise their expected cost. The measured bit strings are then checked against the original graph problem.

Scheduling

Suppose a company must assign jobs to machines while respecting deadlines and resource limits. A binary variable may represent whether a job is assigned to a particular machine or time slot.

The objective might minimise completion time, energy consumption or a penalty associated with delays. Constraints can be incorporated through penalty terms or by using a circuit designed to preserve feasible assignments.

The challenge is to create a formulation that uses a manageable number of qubits and does not make constraint violations too attractive. A poor encoding can make an otherwise promising algorithm ineffective.

Routing and resource allocation

Routing problems ask us to choose paths, while resource allocation problems assign limited resources to competing needs. Both can involve enormous numbers of possible combinations.

A quantum optimisation approach may encode choices in binary variables, construct a cost Hamiltonian and use a variational circuit to generate candidate solutions. Classical methods can still be required to validate, repair or improve those solutions.

How do we know whether the quantum algorithm is useful?

A result is meaningful only when we compare it with appropriate alternatives. For chemistry, that may mean comparing energy estimates with trusted numerical methods or experimental data. For optimisation, it may mean comparing solution quality and total runtime with strong classical algorithms.

A fair evaluation should include the time required to prepare data, compile circuits, execute measurements, optimise parameters and post-process the results. Comparing only the time taken by the quantum processor can produce a misleading conclusion.

Practical obstacles across both application areas

Challenge Why it matters
Problem encoding A formulation may require more qubits or gates than the hardware can support.
Measurement cost Accurate estimates may require many repeated circuit executions.
Noise Hardware errors can reduce the quality of energy estimates or candidate solutions.
Optimisation The classical search may converge slowly or become trapped in an unhelpful region.
Classical competition Well-developed classical algorithms may already solve the problem efficiently.
Scalability A method that works on a small demonstration may not work on a useful real-world instance.
Scientific caution: Variational quantum algorithms have demonstrated useful experimental capabilities, but broad, practical quantum advantage over strong classical methods has not been established for chemistry and combinatorial optimisation in general.

Putting VQE and QAOA Together

Both algorithms use parameterised quantum circuits and classical optimisation. Their main difference lies in the objective they are designed to address and the way their circuits are constructed.

Feature VQE QAOA
Typical problem Estimating a Hamiltonian's ground-state energy. Discrete combinatorial optimisation.
Objective Minimise the expected energy. Minimise expected cost or maximise expected reward.
Ansatz structure Often selected for the physical system or hardware. Alternates cost and mixing operations.
Parameters Typically gate angles or excitation parameters. Usually cost angles γ and mixer angles β.
Measurement result Expectation values used to estimate energy. Bit strings representing candidate solutions.
Classical role Updates the trial-state parameters to lower energy. Updates circuit angles to improve expected cost.
Guarantee Exact trial-state energy is no lower than the true ground-state energy. Finite-depth QAOA does not generally guarantee an optimal solution.

The common pattern

Define the problem
→
Construct an objective
→
Prepare a parameterised state
→
Measure
→
Optimise classically

The process repeats, with the latest parameters guiding the next quantum circuit execution.

This pattern is the central lesson of the chapter. The quantum processor is not asked to solve the entire problem independently in one operation. Instead, it participates in an iterative procedure in which quantum state preparation and measurement work together with classical numerical optimisation.

A Small Conceptual Implementation

The following Python-style pseudocode demonstrates the structure of a generic variational algorithm. It is not tied to a particular quantum framework and does not implement a complete VQE or QAOA calculation. 

# 1. Define the objective and parameterised quantum circuit.
# 2. Initialise the circuit parameters.

parameters = initialise_parameters()

for iteration in range(max_iterations):

    # Prepare and execute the circuit using the current parameters.
    measurement_data = quantum_circuit_run(parameters)

    # Estimate the objective from the measurements.
    objective_value = estimate_objective(measurement_data)

    # Decide whether the stopping condition has been met.
    if converged(objective_value):
        break

    # Use a classical optimiser to propose new parameters.
    parameters = optimiser_update(
        parameters,
        objective_value
    )

# Evaluate the final result and verify its quality.
final_result = evaluate_solution(parameters)

The functions above represent conceptual operations, not built-in Python functions. A real implementation must define the circuit, measurement process, objective, optimiser, convergence criteria and result validation.

What would change between VQE and QAOA?

In VQE, the objective estimation step evaluates the Hamiltonian's energy expectation. The final result is often an estimated ground-state energy and, when needed, information about the prepared state.

In QAOA, the circuit has the alternating cost-and-mixer structure. The measurement outcomes are decoded into candidate bit strings, and the classical objective is evaluated on those candidates. The optimiser uses the expected cost to update the QAOA angles.

In both cases, the quality of the final answer must be assessed separately from the fact that the optimiser stopped running.

Chapter Summary

  1. NISQ computing studies what can be achieved with quantum processors that have limited scale and imperfect operations.
  2. Variational algorithms combine parameterised quantum circuits with classical optimisation in an iterative loop.
  3. VQE uses the variational principle to search for a trial state with low expected energy.
  4. QAOA alternates cost-dependent and mixing operations to produce candidate solutions for discrete optimisation problems.
  5. Ansatz design balances the ability to represent useful states against circuit depth, hardware restrictions and optimisation difficulty.
  6. Classical optimisers search for useful parameters, but their performance depends on measurement uncertainty, noise and the structure of the objective.
  7. Applications include quantum chemistry and combinatorial optimisation, although practical advantage over classical methods must be demonstrated for each problem.
The main takeaway: Variational quantum algorithms make the quantum computer part of a larger computational workflow. Their promise lies in finding useful ways to combine quantum state preparation with classical optimisation, while their limitations remind us that better hardware, better circuits and better algorithms are all necessary.

Looking Ahead

Variational algorithms demonstrate one way to work with today's imperfect quantum hardware. However, they are not the only approach to quantum computing, and their repeated measurement and optimisation costs can become substantial.

The next stage of understanding quantum algorithms is to study how they connect with quantum simulation, quantum linear algebra, quantum error correction and fault-tolerant computation. These areas help us understand both what current devices can attempt and what future quantum computers may be able to accomplish.
