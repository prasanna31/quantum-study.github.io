Quantum Computing and Quantum Machine Learning

Chapter 27: Real-World Applications

From discovering new medicines to designing better batteries, quantum computing offers new ways to study problems that are difficult for ordinary computers. But where can it genuinely help, what has already been demonstrated, and what remains a research goal?

Introduction: From Quantum Theory to Real Problems

Imagine that scientists want to develop a medicine for a disease. They must understand how molecules behave, how they interact with proteins, and whether a potential drug can work safely inside the human body. At the same time, engineers may be trying to design a battery that charges faster, financial institutions may be estimating risk, and electricity companies may be planning how to distribute power efficiently.

These problems appear unrelated, but they share an important feature: they involve complicated systems with many possible states, interactions, decisions, or uncertainties. Classical computers already solve many such problems successfully. However, some parts can become extremely expensive to calculate as the system grows.

Quantum computing introduces a different way to process information. Instead of using only classical bits, which represent 0 or 1, a quantum computer uses qubits. Qubits can be prepared in superpositions, and multiple qubits can become entangled. Quantum algorithms use these properties to carry out particular calculations.

The important word is particular. A quantum computer is not a universally faster computer. It is a different computational tool that may offer advantages for carefully chosen problems.

The central idea of this chapter: A useful quantum application must do more than run on a quantum processor. It must solve a meaningful part of a real problem accurately, efficiently, and preferably better than the strongest practical classical alternatives.

How does a quantum application work?

A real-world quantum application usually involves a complete workflow, rather than a quantum processor working alone.

  1. Define the problem. Identify the scientific or business question to be answered.
  2. Build a mathematical model. Represent the relevant physical system, decisions, or uncertainties in a form that a computer can process.
  3. Prepare the input. Encode the required information into a quantum state, or prepare a quantum circuit that represents the problem.
  4. Run the quantum algorithm. Apply quantum operations and measure the required quantities.
  5. Process the results. Use classical computation to analyse measurements, update parameters, or refine candidate solutions.
  6. Validate the answer. Compare the result with reliable reference methods and check whether it is useful in the real world.

In many present-day applications, quantum and classical computing work together. The classical computer manages data, optimisation, and control, while the quantum processor performs the quantum portion of the calculation. This arrangement is often called hybrid quantum-classical computing.

Three levels of evidence

Throughout this chapter, it is useful to distinguish three stages of technological progress.

  • Established classical application: The problem is already solved using classical computing, such as molecular modelling or portfolio optimisation.
  • Quantum research demonstration: A quantum device or algorithm has performed a relevant calculation, often on a small or specially chosen problem.
  • Practical quantum advantage: A quantum approach provides a meaningful advantage in cost, speed, accuracy, or another important measure on a useful problem, compared with the best relevant classical alternatives.

These stages must not be confused. Demonstrating a quantum calculation is an important scientific achievement, but it does not automatically establish that quantum computing is commercially useful for that task.

27.1 Drug Discovery and Molecular Simulation

The challenge: Understanding molecules

Every medicine works through interactions between molecules. A drug molecule might attach to a protein, block an unwanted chemical reaction, or change the behaviour of a biological pathway.

To develop a successful medicine, researchers need to understand the shape, energy, stability, and interactions of these molecules. They may examine thousands or millions of possible compounds before identifying promising candidates.

A major challenge comes from quantum mechanics itself. Electrons in molecules interact with one another, and their behaviour determines many chemical properties. A molecule with many interacting electrons can be difficult to describe exactly using classical computation.

Why quantum computers may help

Quantum computers are built from physical systems that obey quantum mechanics. This makes them potentially suitable for simulating certain quantum systems, including the electronic structure of molecules.

In a simplified picture, the goal is to estimate the energy associated with a particular molecular arrangement. The arrangement with lower energy may be more stable under the conditions being studied. Comparing such energies helps chemists understand reactions, bonding, and molecular behaviour.

H|ψ⟩ = E|ψ⟩

This is the time-independent Schrödinger eigenvalue equation. Here, H is the Hamiltonian, which represents the energy of the system; |ψ⟩ is a quantum state; and E is the corresponding energy.

Molecular simulation often involves finding the lowest possible energy for an appropriate electronic state. This is known as the ground-state energy problem.

Important quantum methods

1. Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)

VQE is a hybrid algorithm designed to estimate molecular energies. A quantum circuit prepares a trial state, and measurements estimate the energy associated with that state. A classical optimiser then changes the circuit parameters in an attempt to lower the estimated energy.

  1. The classical computer selects circuit parameters.
  2. The quantum processor prepares the corresponding trial state.
  3. Measurements estimate the energy.
  4. The classical optimiser updates the parameters.
  5. The process repeats until a stopping condition is reached.

VQE is attractive because it can be used with relatively shallow circuits in some implementations. However, its success depends on the circuit, the quality of measurements, the optimiser, and the physical device. A circuit may struggle to represent the required molecular state, and measurement noise can make accurate energy estimation expensive.

2. Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE)

Quantum Phase Estimation is a quantum algorithm that can estimate certain eigenvalues by extracting phase information associated with a quantum operation. When applied to suitable molecular Hamiltonians, it can be used to estimate energy eigenvalues.

Under appropriate assumptions, QPE can offer favourable precision scaling compared with several classical approaches. However, demanding molecular applications may require many high-quality logical qubits and long, fault-tolerant circuits. These requirements are beyond what most current quantum hardware can comfortably support.

Where machine learning enters drug discovery

Quantum computing is only one part of the drug discovery process. Classical machine learning already helps researchers predict molecular properties, identify candidate compounds, model protein structures, and prioritise experiments.

Quantum machine learning investigates whether quantum circuits can contribute to tasks such as molecular classification, property prediction, or learning patterns in chemical data. For example, a variational quantum classifier could be trained to distinguish between two categories of molecular candidates.

Such a classifier is useful only if it generalises to new molecules and performs competitively with strong classical models. Merely encoding molecular features into qubits does not guarantee an advantage.

Illustrative example

Finding a molecule that binds to a protein

Suppose researchers have identified 50,000 candidate molecules. Classical screening tools can quickly remove many unsuitable candidates. More detailed simulations then investigate the remaining molecules.

A future quantum processor might improve the accuracy of electronic energy calculations for a difficult part of this workflow. Scientists could use those results to improve their understanding of molecular interactions and decide which candidates deserve laboratory testing.

This is a possible workflow, not evidence that quantum computers can already discover a complete medicine independently.

What are the limitations?

  • Biological systems are complex, and accurate molecular energy is only one part of predicting whether a drug will work.
  • Quantum measurements may need to be repeated many times to achieve the required precision.
  • Realistic calculations may require fault-tolerant hardware with many logical qubits.
  • Drug safety, toxicity, absorption, metabolism, and clinical effectiveness require additional methods and experiments.
Key lesson: Quantum computers may eventually improve selected molecular calculations. Drug discovery as a whole will continue to require chemistry, biology, classical computing, laboratory experiments, and clinical research.

27.2 Materials Science and Battery Design

Why materials matter

Modern technology depends on materials with carefully controlled properties. Solar cells need materials that absorb sunlight efficiently. Aircraft need strong, lightweight structures. Computer chips need materials with suitable electrical behaviour. Batteries need materials that store energy, release it reliably, and remain stable over many charging cycles.

A material's properties depend on the arrangement of its atoms and the interactions between its electrons. Small changes in composition or structure can lead to large changes in conductivity, strength, chemical stability, or energy storage.

How quantum computing could contribute

Quantum simulation could help scientists investigate electronic behaviour in materials that are difficult to model accurately using classical methods. This is especially relevant when strong electronic correlations make simple approximations unreliable.

Scientists may want to estimate energy levels, compare candidate structures, understand chemical reactions, or study how electrons move through a material. These calculations could support the search for better materials, although the complete design process involves many additional factors.

Battery design: A closer look

A rechargeable battery stores energy through chemical reactions and the movement of charged particles between its electrodes. During charging and discharging, ions move through the electrolyte while electrons travel through the external circuit.

Battery performance depends on several properties:

  • Energy density: How much energy the battery can store for its mass or volume.
  • Power density: How quickly the battery can deliver or accept energy.
  • Cycle life: How many charging and discharging cycles it can withstand.
  • Safety: How well the battery resists dangerous heating, degradation, and unwanted reactions.
  • Cost and availability: Whether its materials can be sourced and manufactured economically.

Quantum calculations could contribute to understanding electrode materials, electrolyte chemistry, chemical reaction pathways, and degradation mechanisms. Better electronic-structure predictions may help scientists compare candidate materials before performing expensive experiments.

Illustrative example

Searching for a better electrolyte

Imagine that a research team wants an electrolyte that allows ions to move efficiently while remaining stable at the battery's operating voltage.

Classical screening could examine many chemical candidates. Quantum simulation might eventually provide more accurate information about selected reactions or electronic properties. Laboratory experiments would then test conductivity, stability, safety, and long-term performance.

Beyond batteries

The same ideas apply to catalysts, superconductors, semiconductors, magnetic materials, and materials for carbon capture. A catalyst can accelerate a chemical reaction without being consumed in the overall process. Finding better catalysts can reduce energy consumption and waste in industrial manufacturing.

Quantum simulation could be valuable when the electronic behaviour of the material determines how well it performs. It does not automatically solve problems involving manufacturing defects, large-scale production, material cost, or mechanical durability.

Important distinction: Predicting a material's electronic properties is not the same as building a commercially useful battery. Real-world performance depends on the entire device, manufacturing process, operating conditions, and lifetime.

The near-term approach is therefore likely to be a combination of classical simulation, machine learning, targeted quantum calculations, and physical experiments.

27.3 Finance: Portfolio Optimisation, Risk, and Option Pricing

Why finance involves difficult computation

Financial decisions are made under uncertainty. A portfolio manager must decide how to distribute money across different assets. A bank must estimate possible losses. An options trader must estimate the value of a contract whose payoff depends on the future price of an asset.

These tasks combine mathematical optimisation, probability, statistics, and large amounts of data. Quantum algorithms have been investigated for certain parts of these calculations.

Portfolio optimisation

Suppose an investor can choose among several stocks or other assets. Each asset has an expected return, but its future return is uncertain. The assets may also rise and fall together, so their relationships matter.

A common classical model balances expected return against portfolio risk. One simplified objective is:

Maximise: μᵀw − λwᵀΣw

Here, w represents the portfolio weights, μ represents expected returns, Σ is the covariance matrix of asset returns, and λ controls how strongly the investor penalises risk.

The investor must also respect constraints, such as having a total investment of 100%, limiting exposure to individual assets, or avoiding certain investments.

The basic continuous optimisation problem may be solved using classical numerical methods. The problem can become harder when it includes discrete choices, such as whether to buy an asset at all, transaction costs, minimum trading amounts, and complicated constraints.

Quantum optimisation methods

1. Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA)

QAOA is a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm designed for certain combinatorial optimisation problems. The problem is encoded into a mathematical cost function, and a parameterised quantum circuit explores candidate solutions.

The quantum circuit prepares a state whose measurement can produce different candidate solutions. A classical optimiser adjusts the circuit parameters to improve the expected cost.

For portfolio selection, a binary variable might indicate whether an asset is included. The objective and constraints can then be translated into a binary optimisation problem, often using a quadratic unconstrained binary optimisation formulation, or QUBO.

However, mapping a financial problem to QUBO can introduce penalty terms, additional variables, or restrictions. These may make the quantum problem difficult to run, even when the mathematical formulation is elegant.

2. Quantum annealing

Quantum annealers are specialised devices designed to search for low-energy configurations of a programmed optimisation problem. They can be used to investigate suitable discrete optimisation tasks.

Their usefulness depends on how the original problem maps onto the device, including connectivity, precision, constraints, and the overhead required to represent the problem. A result from an annealer must still be compared with strong classical optimisation methods.

Risk analysis

Financial risk analysis often asks questions such as: How much money might a portfolio lose during a difficult market period? What is the probability of a large loss? How do different economic scenarios affect a bank's assets and liabilities?

Two familiar measures are Value at Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR). VaR estimates a loss threshold at a chosen confidence level. CVaR describes the expected loss in the tail beyond that threshold, under the relevant definition.

Quantum algorithms have been studied for estimating probabilities and expected values. Some quantum amplitude-estimation methods offer a theoretical reduction in the number of oracle queries needed to reach a given estimation precision.

This advantage is not automatic. The required probability distribution must be prepared or accessed efficiently, and the cost of data loading, circuit execution, error correction, and result extraction must be counted.

Option pricing

An option is a financial contract that gives its holder a right, but not necessarily an obligation, to buy or sell an asset under specified conditions. Its value depends on factors such as the asset price, strike price, time to expiry, volatility, and interest rates.

Many option-pricing models calculate the expected future payoff and discount it to the present. For complex models, estimating that expectation may require substantial numerical computation.

Quantum amplitude estimation is one proposed approach to accelerating certain expectation-estimation tasks. Under ideal algorithmic assumptions, it can improve the dependence on the target precision compared with ordinary independent sampling.

Classical sampling: O(1/ε²)
Ideal quantum amplitude estimation: O(1/ε)

Here, ε is the target additive estimation error. These expressions describe idealised query complexity for suitable formulations, not guaranteed end-to-end running times for real financial systems.

Illustrative example

Estimating a portfolio's potential loss

A bank wants to estimate the probability that its portfolio will lose more than a specified amount. It creates a model of possible market scenarios and calculates the loss under each scenario.

A quantum algorithm might help estimate the probability or expected loss if the scenario model can be prepared efficiently and the necessary quantum operations can be implemented reliably.

The bank would still need to validate the model, account for unusual market events, and compare the full computational cost with classical Monte Carlo simulation and other established techniques.

Reality check: Quantum finance research has demonstrated small-scale algorithms and proof-of-concept experiments. A general, commercially decisive quantum advantage in portfolio management, risk calculation, or option pricing has not been established.

27.4 Logistics, Supply Chain, and Scheduling

The problem of making many decisions

Consider a delivery company with hundreds of vehicles, thousands of deliveries, limited fuel, delivery time windows, and customers expecting their orders on time. The company wants to minimise travel distance, cost, and delays.

This is not simply a matter of finding the shortest route. Vehicles may have different capacities, roads may have different travel times, and some deliveries must occur before others. Drivers also need breaks, and warehouses may have limited loading capacity.

Such problems belong to the broader family of combinatorial optimisation problems. Their number of possible solutions can grow very rapidly as the number of decisions increases.

Common applications

  • Vehicle routing: Decide which vehicles should serve which customers and in what order.
  • Warehouse operations: Assign picking tasks, workers, and storage locations.
  • Supply-chain planning: Decide where to store products, when to replenish stock, and how to move goods.
  • Production scheduling: Decide the order in which machines perform jobs while respecting deadlines and dependencies.
  • Airline and railway scheduling: Coordinate vehicles, crews, maintenance, and timetables.
  • Resource allocation: Assign limited resources to competing activities.

How quantum optimisation fits in

QAOA and quantum annealing can be applied to selected optimisation formulations. A delivery-routing problem, for example, may be represented using binary decision variables that indicate whether a vehicle travels between two locations at a particular step.

A cost function can reward shorter routes while penalty terms discourage invalid solutions, such as visiting a required customer more than once or exceeding a vehicle's capacity.

The resulting mathematical model can be submitted to a suitable quantum algorithm. However, a large real-world problem may require so many variables and constraints that it cannot be represented efficiently on current quantum hardware.

Quantum-inspired methods

There is an important distinction between quantum computing and quantum-inspired optimisation. A quantum computer uses physical qubits and quantum operations. A quantum-inspired method runs on a classical computer but borrows ideas from quantum physics or quantum algorithms.

Quantum-inspired methods can be useful today, but their success does not demonstrate that a quantum processor is faster. They should be evaluated as classical algorithms in their own right.

Illustrative example

Planning deliveries across a city

Suppose a company must deliver 300 packages using 20 vehicles. It wants to reduce fuel consumption without missing customer deadlines.

A practical system may first cluster nearby deliveries, use a classical routing algorithm to create good initial routes, and improve those routes with local search or other optimisation methods.

A future quantum optimiser could be tested on a carefully selected subproblem, such as a difficult assignment decision. Its results would then be compared with the classical system on the same instances and under the same constraints.

What makes a real-world comparison fair?

The quantum method must include the time needed to formulate the problem, encode it, execute the circuit, repeat measurements, and convert the result into a valid schedule. The classical method must also be given a reasonable amount of computation and access to appropriate algorithms.

In business, a solution that arrives ten minutes earlier but costs more to operate may not be better. A useful comparison should consider solution quality, reliability, computational cost, energy use, and whether the answer respects every operational constraint.

27.5 Energy and Climate Modelling

Why energy systems are difficult to manage

Electricity systems must balance supply and demand continuously. At the same time, renewable energy sources such as solar and wind depend on weather conditions. Electricity networks also face constraints on transmission, storage, and generation.

Climate modelling is a different but equally complex challenge. Climate scientists combine observations, physical equations, and numerical simulations to understand how the atmosphere, oceans, land, ice, and living systems interact.

Quantum computing is being investigated for selected calculations relevant to these areas. It is not a replacement for the complete classical infrastructure used to forecast weather, model climate, or operate electricity networks.

Energy production and storage

Quantum simulation may help researchers study materials and chemical reactions that matter for energy technologies. Examples include battery electrodes, catalysts for hydrogen production, materials for solar cells, and processes relevant to carbon capture.

If more accurate simulations identify a better catalyst, an industrial process may eventually require less energy. If a new material improves battery performance, renewable electricity may become easier to store. These are possible chains of impact, rather than guaranteed direct outcomes of quantum computation.

Electricity-grid optimisation

Power-grid operators must decide which generators to use, how much electricity each should produce, and how to maintain safe operation while meeting demand. This includes unit commitment, economic dispatch, transmission planning, and storage scheduling.

Some of these decisions involve discrete choices and complex constraints. Quantum optimisation methods may therefore be studied for selected subproblems. Nevertheless, mature classical optimisation methods already play a major role in grid operations and provide a demanding baseline.

Climate modelling

Climate models divide the Earth system into computational elements and solve equations that describe physical processes. Higher resolution and more detailed representations of clouds, turbulence, ocean circulation, and chemical reactions can demand enormous computational resources.

Quantum computers do not automatically accelerate these calculations. Many climate computations involve large classical data sets and numerical methods for which classical processors are highly effective. Encoding such data into quantum states can itself be costly.

More promising research directions include selected quantum simulations of physical or chemical processes, specialised optimisation problems, and carefully formulated sampling tasks. Whether these approaches improve useful climate predictions remains an open question.

Illustrative example

Reducing the cost of hydrogen production

A research team wants to identify a catalyst that helps produce hydrogen using less energy. It screens candidate materials with classical machine learning and simulation, then uses more detailed calculations on promising candidates.

If a quantum computer eventually provides more accurate predictions for a difficult electronic-structure calculation, that information could help researchers decide which material to test experimentally. The final result still depends on laboratory validation, manufacturing, durability, and total system cost.

Key lesson: The strongest near-term connection between quantum computing and clean energy may come from better materials and chemistry, alongside targeted optimisation, rather than replacing large-scale climate simulations.

27.6 Healthcare and Genomics

Healthcare involves more than one computational problem

Healthcare includes medical imaging, diagnosis, treatment planning, protein research, drug development, patient monitoring, and the analysis of genetic information. Each task has its own data, mathematical structure, and requirements for reliability.

Quantum computing research explores whether quantum algorithms can contribute to some of these tasks. However, many healthcare applications already work well with classical machine learning and high-performance computing. Any proposed quantum method must be compared with those established approaches.

Genomics: Reading the information in DNA

DNA contains sequences built from four chemical bases, commonly represented by the letters A, C, G, and T. Differences in these sequences can influence biological characteristics and may be associated with certain diseases.

Genomics uses computational methods to analyse DNA sequences, identify genetic variants, study gene regulation, and understand relationships between genetic information and biological outcomes.

Some genomic tasks involve very large data sets, complex statistical models, and high-dimensional patterns. Quantum machine learning investigates whether quantum feature maps, quantum kernels, or parameterised quantum circuits could help with selected learning tasks.

Quantum kernels and classification

In machine learning, a kernel measures a notion of similarity between two data points. A quantum kernel can encode classical data into quantum states and estimate a similarity based on measurements or state overlaps.

In principle, a quantum feature map may represent relationships that are difficult for some classical models to capture efficiently. In practice, the value of this representation depends on the data, the encoding, the quantum circuit, and the learning task.

If preparing the input requires excessive work, or if estimating each similarity takes too many measurements, the overall method may not be competitive. Strong classical kernel methods and modern machine-learning models must be used as baselines.

Protein and molecular research

Proteins perform many essential functions in the body. Their behaviour depends on their structure, interactions, and chemical environment. Quantum chemistry may eventually contribute to detailed calculations of electronic effects that influence molecular interactions.

This overlaps with drug discovery, but it is useful to distinguish the tasks. Predicting a protein's structure, estimating the electronic energy of a molecule, and determining whether a drug is safe are different problems requiring different methods.

Medical imaging and diagnosis

Researchers have investigated quantum machine-learning models for image classification, disease prediction, and other pattern-recognition tasks. Small experiments can test whether a quantum circuit learns from a data set, but this is not enough to establish medical usefulness.

A clinically useful system must be accurate on independent patient populations, robust to changes in equipment and data quality, explainable where necessary, and carefully evaluated for bias and safety.

Current quantum devices also have limited capacity and are affected by noise. These limitations make large-scale medical data processing challenging.

Healthcare requires a high standard of evidence. A promising quantum classifier is not a clinically validated diagnostic tool. It must undergo rigorous testing and appropriate regulatory review before being used to guide patient care.

Privacy and sensitive medical data

Genomic and medical data can reveal deeply personal information. Quantum computing does not automatically make such data private or secure. Privacy requires appropriate data governance, access controls, cryptographic protections, and responsible handling of research data.

27.7 Cybersecurity and Post-Quantum Cryptography

Why cybersecurity is directly affected by quantum computing

Cybersecurity protects information as it moves between people, devices, servers, and organisations. It relies on cryptographic methods for encryption, authentication, digital signatures, and secure communication.

A sufficiently powerful fault-tolerant quantum computer would affect some widely used public-key cryptographic systems. This makes cybersecurity one of the clearest areas in which quantum computing has important real-world consequences, even before large-scale quantum computers become available.

Shor's algorithm

Many public-key cryptographic systems rely on mathematical problems that are believed to be difficult for classical computers to solve efficiently. Examples include integer factorisation in RSA and the discrete logarithm problem in several elliptic-curve cryptographic systems.

Shor's algorithm is a quantum algorithm that can solve integer factorisation and discrete logarithm problems in polynomial time under the standard algorithmic model.

This does not mean that current quantum computers can break ordinary internet encryption at will. Practical attacks against cryptographically relevant keys would require sufficiently capable, fault-tolerant quantum hardware, which is far beyond the capabilities of today's noisy devices for those large instances.

Grover's algorithm

Grover's algorithm provides a quadratic query-complexity improvement for unstructured search. In an idealised setting, if there are N possibilities and one is a marked solution, classical search requires O(N) queries in the worst case, while Grover's algorithm uses O(√N) oracle queries.

This matters when considering exhaustive search and some cryptographic security parameters. However, the query improvement does not translate directly into a universal square-root reduction in every attack's practical cost. Circuit depth, parallelism, error correction, and implementation overhead also matter.

What is post-quantum cryptography?

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) refers to cryptographic algorithms designed to resist attacks from both classical computers and quantum computers, including known quantum algorithms.

These algorithms run on ordinary classical computers. They do not require a quantum processor. This makes them especially important because organisations can begin adopting them before a large-scale quantum computer capable of breaking existing public-key systems exists.

Important families of post-quantum cryptography

  • Lattice-based cryptography: Uses problems involving mathematical structures called lattices. Several standardised PQC schemes belong to this family.
  • Hash-based signatures: Uses cryptographic hash functions to construct digital signature systems.
  • Code-based cryptography: Builds security around problems related to decoding error-correcting codes.
  • Other mathematical approaches: Additional families have been studied, although their maturity and standardisation status vary.

Post-quantum cryptography should not be confused with quantum cryptography. PQC uses classical computation and mathematical problems believed to resist quantum attacks. Quantum cryptography uses quantum physical effects in cryptographic protocols, such as quantum key distribution.

Why organisations should prepare now

Sensitive information may need to remain confidential for many years. An attacker could collect encrypted information today and attempt to decrypt it in the future when more powerful technology becomes available. This risk is often called harvest now, decrypt later.

Organisations should therefore identify where vulnerable cryptography is used, prioritise systems with long confidentiality requirements, assess software and hardware dependencies, and plan a controlled migration to suitable post-quantum standards.

This transition is not as simple as replacing one encryption function. Cryptographic systems include certificates, protocols, firmware, key management, third-party services, and devices that may remain in use for many years.

Key lesson: Post-quantum cryptography is a practical security priority today. Organisations do not need to wait for a large-scale quantum computer before preparing their systems.

27.8 Case Studies: What Has Actually Been Demonstrated?

Why evidence matters

Quantum computing attracts attention because it combines unusual physics with the promise of solving difficult computational problems. However, it is easy to confuse a successful experiment with a commercially useful application.

A careful case study asks four questions:

  1. What exact problem did the researchers solve?
  2. What quantum hardware and algorithm did they use?
  3. What was the classical comparison, if any?
  4. Did the result establish practical value for a real application, or only demonstrate a scientific capability?

Case study 1: Quantum chemistry on small systems

Researchers have used quantum processors to perform small-scale molecular and electronic-structure calculations. Experiments have explored variational methods, energy estimation, and other quantum chemistry algorithms.

These demonstrations show that quantum hardware can be programmed to investigate meaningful scientific quantities. They also help researchers understand the effects of noise, measurement uncertainty, and circuit design.

However, many demonstrations have involved small molecules, simplified models, or carefully chosen conditions. Classical computational chemistry remains highly effective for many practical systems.

What has been established: Quantum devices can perform selected molecular calculations and provide valuable experimental evidence about quantum algorithms.

What has not been established: A general ability to outperform the best classical methods on commercially important drug-discovery calculations.

Case study 2: Google's quantum supremacy experiment

In 2019, Google's Sycamore processor performed a specialised random circuit sampling task. The experiment was designed to demonstrate that a quantum processor could perform a carefully selected sampling computation that was extremely difficult for classical computers to reproduce at the time.

The task was not a drug-discovery, financial, or logistics application. It was a controlled computational experiment designed to investigate the capabilities of quantum hardware.

Subsequent improvements in classical simulation methods and changes in computational estimates have highlighted why quantum advantage claims must be assessed against evolving classical techniques.

What has been established: The experiment was an important milestone in controlling and measuring a quantum processor on a specialised task.

What it does not establish: That the processor is faster or more economical for ordinary commercial computing tasks.

Case study 3: IBM and the development of quantum utility

IBM and other research groups have investigated quantum computation on increasingly complex circuits, including experiments in which classical simulation becomes challenging.

Such experiments are important because they test whether quantum processors can produce useful computational results as hardware scales. However, the meaning of usefulness depends on the task. A calculation that cannot easily be reproduced by a classical simulator is not automatically useful for chemistry, finance, or another practical application.

The next step is to demonstrate scientifically valuable results, verify them where possible, and compare the complete workflow against strong classical alternatives.

Case study 4: Quantum optimisation experiments

Quantum annealers and gate-based processors have been used to explore optimisation tasks, including graph problems, scheduling formulations, and other combinatorial problems.

These experiments demonstrate that optimisation models can be encoded and investigated using quantum hardware. They also reveal practical challenges such as limited connectivity, embedding overhead, noise, and the difficulty of finding high-quality solutions reliably.

In many comparisons, classical methods remain highly competitive or superior. The outcome depends on the problem structure, the size of the instance, the algorithm, and the quality of the baseline.

What has been established: Quantum hardware can explore a variety of optimisation formulations and provide experimental data about their behaviour.

What remains open: Whether quantum optimisation can consistently outperform the strongest classical methods on useful industrial problems when total cost and solution quality are included.

Case study 5: Quantum machine learning

Researchers have implemented quantum classifiers, quantum kernels, variational circuits, and other quantum machine-learning methods on simulators and physical quantum processors.

Small data sets are often used to investigate whether a circuit can learn a decision boundary or represent a useful feature map. Such experiments can reveal how circuit architecture, noise, optimisation, and data encoding affect learning.

The difficult question is whether the quantum model provides an advantage over an appropriately tuned classical model. A fair comparison must include data preparation, training, inference, circuit repetitions, and the cost of obtaining the final output.

What has been established: Quantum machine-learning models can be trained and evaluated on selected tasks, and their learning behaviour can be studied experimentally.

What remains open: Broad, reproducible practical advantages for real-world machine-learning workloads.

Case study 6: Post-quantum cryptographic standardisation

Unlike many proposed quantum applications, post-quantum cryptography has already moved into practical standardisation and migration planning. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published its first principal post-quantum cryptography standards in 2024.

These standards include ML-KEM for key establishment and ML-DSA and SLH-DSA for digital signatures. They are intended to help organisations deploy cryptographic systems designed to resist known quantum attacks as well as classical attacks.

The algorithms are classical software and mathematical constructions, not quantum algorithms running on quantum computers. Their adoption demonstrates a concrete real-world response to the future security implications of quantum computing.

What has been established: Standardised post-quantum cryptographic algorithms are available for implementation and migration planning.

What remains ongoing: Deployment across the many protocols, applications, devices, and organisations that depend on public-key cryptography.

Summary of the evidence

Application area What has been demonstrated Main unresolved challenge

How to Judge a Quantum Application Claim

Whenever a company or research paper claims that quantum computing has solved a practical problem, it helps to ask a few simple questions. These questions apply to every field discussed in this chapter.

1. Is the problem genuinely difficult for classical computers?

A quantum algorithm may look impressive when compared with a weak classical method. A meaningful comparison should include modern classical algorithms, suitable hardware, and sensible optimisation.

2. Is the input preparation efficient?

Quantum algorithms often require information to be encoded into quantum states. If loading the data takes more resources than the algorithm saves, the theoretical advantage may disappear.

3. Is the output useful?

Quantum measurement does not normally reveal every detail of a large quantum state. The algorithm must be designed so that the information extracted through measurement is sufficient to answer the original question.

4. Are noise and error correction included?

Real quantum devices experience errors. Repeated measurements, error mitigation, and fault-tolerant error correction can require substantial additional resources. A realistic evaluation should account for these costs.

5. Does the advantage matter outside the laboratory?

An application may need to satisfy strict requirements for accuracy, reliability, cost, energy consumption, or response time. A quantum method that performs well on a small demonstration may not satisfy those requirements at production scale.

6. Can another team reproduce the result?

Independent verification, transparent benchmarks, and reproducible experiments help establish whether a result is robust. This is particularly important when the classical comparison is difficult or the quantum output is noisy.

What Could Change as Quantum Hardware Improves?

Many of the applications in this chapter depend on the availability of reliable logical qubits. A physical qubit is a device-level implementation of a qubit and is affected by errors. A logical qubit is encoded across physical qubits using quantum error correction so that information can be protected against certain errors.

Error correction requires additional physical qubits and repeated operations. The overhead depends on the hardware, noise levels, error-correcting code, target reliability, and circuit requirements. Consequently, the number of physical qubits available is not the same as the number of reliable logical qubits a useful algorithm can employ.

As hardware improves, researchers hope to run deeper circuits, perform more accurate simulations, and execute algorithms whose resource requirements exceed what current devices can handle. This could make quantum chemistry, selected optimisation problems, and some estimation tasks more practical.

However, better hardware alone will not guarantee an advantage. Algorithms, problem formulations, error-correction methods, data preparation, and classical baselines must improve as well.

The likely path to useful applications

  1. Small demonstrations: Verify that quantum circuits can carry out the intended operations.
  2. Algorithm development: Improve accuracy, reduce circuit depth, and understand resource requirements.
  3. Fault-tolerant execution: Run reliable calculations using error-corrected logical qubits.
  4. Application-level validation: Demonstrate meaningful results on realistic scientific or industrial problems.
  5. Economic evaluation: Establish whether the quantum workflow offers enough benefit to justify its total cost.

This progression will not happen at the same speed in every field. Quantum chemistry may benefit from a different set of algorithms and hardware capabilities than finance or combinatorial optimisation. Each application needs its own evidence.

Conclusion: Quantum Computing as a Practical Scientific Tool

Quantum computing becomes easier to understand when it is connected to real problems. Drug discovery and materials science involve the behaviour of molecules and electrons. Finance involves optimisation and uncertainty. Logistics involves finding good decisions under many constraints. Energy research involves materials, chemistry, and the management of complex systems. Healthcare involves biological data and demanding standards of accuracy. Cybersecurity must prepare for the mathematical capabilities of future quantum computers.

These applications do not all benefit from the same quantum algorithm. Molecular simulation may use energy estimation methods. Optimisation may use QAOA or quantum annealing. Some financial calculations may investigate amplitude estimation. Quantum machine learning studies how quantum circuits can represent and learn patterns in data. Post-quantum cryptography, meanwhile, protects classical information systems against potential quantum attacks.

The most important scientific question is not whether quantum computing can be applied to a problem in principle. It is whether a practical quantum implementation can produce a better result than the alternatives.

Some capabilities have already been demonstrated in controlled experiments. Many commercial applications remain under development. Distinguishing between these stages is essential for understanding the field honestly.

The central takeaway: Quantum computing is not a replacement for all classical computing. Its potential lies in solving selected problems in new ways, particularly where the mathematical structure of the problem matches the capabilities of quantum algorithms. Its real success will be measured by verified scientific discoveries, useful engineering improvements, reliable computation, and practical advantages.

Revision Questions

1. Why might quantum computers help with molecular simulation?
Reveal answer

Molecular properties depend on interacting electrons and quantum states. Quantum computers may eventually represent and estimate certain properties of these systems more efficiently than classical methods for selected problems.

2. What is the difference between VQE and QPE?
Reveal answer

VQE is a hybrid algorithm that optimises a trial quantum state using quantum measurements and a classical optimiser. QPE estimates eigenvalue-related phase information using a quantum algorithm that generally requires deeper, more demanding circuits for challenging applications.

3. How could quantum computing contribute to battery design?
Reveal answer

It may improve selected calculations involving electrode materials, electrolyte chemistry, and chemical reactions. Laboratory testing is still required to establish safety, lifetime, performance, and cost.

4. Why must quantum optimisation be compared with classical methods?
Reveal answer

Classical algorithms are already highly effective for many optimisation problems. A quantum method is useful only if it provides a meaningful advantage after accounting for solution quality, data preparation, execution, and other costs.

5. What is the difference between quantum machine learning and classical machine learning?
Reveal answer

Classical machine learning uses classical computational representations and operations. Quantum machine learning uses quantum states, circuits, or quantum measurements as part of the learning procedure. Quantum methods do not automatically perform better.

6. Why does Shor's algorithm matter for cybersecurity?
Reveal answer

A sufficiently capable fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could threaten public-key systems based on integer factorisation and discrete logarithms. Post-quantum cryptography provides classical algorithms designed to resist known quantum attacks.

7. Does a successful quantum experiment prove practical quantum advantage?
Reveal answer

No. A successful experiment shows that a particular task was performed. Practical advantage requires a meaningful comparison against strong classical alternatives, including the cost, reliability, and quality of the complete workflow.

8. Which application area already has a concrete response to future quantum threats?
Reveal answer

Cybersecurity has developed post-quantum cryptographic standards and migration programmes. These classical algorithms are designed to protect systems against known quantum attacks.

Key Terms

Term Meaning
Hamiltonian A mathematical operator describing the energy of a quantum system.
Ground state The lowest-energy state of a system under the specified conditions.
VQE A hybrid quantum-classical method for estimating eigenvalues, commonly used for molecular energy calculations.
QPE Quantum Phase Estimation, a method for estimating phase information related to eigenvalues.
QAOA A hybrid quantum-classical algorithm for certain combinatorial optimisation problems.
Quantum annealing A computational approach that uses quantum dynamics to search for low-energy configurations of a problem.
Quantum advantage A demonstrated benefit over relevant classical alternatives for a specified computational task.
Quantum kernel A similarity function estimated using a quantum representation of data.
Fault tolerance The ability to perform reliable computation despite physical errors, using techniques such as quantum error correction.
Logical qubit A qubit encoded across physical qubits to protect quantum information against certain errors.
Post-quantum cryptography Classical cryptographic methods designed to resist attacks from quantum computers as well as classical computers.
Quantum-inspired algorithm A classical algorithm that borrows ideas from quantum computing or quantum physics.
